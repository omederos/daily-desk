Anthropic's growth slowed, and it says faster progress may be close

Anthropic's amended S-1, filed with the SEC on Monday, adds the July to September quarter to the prospectus it published last week, which this desk covered a week ago. Revenue for the quarter was about $17 billion, up about 1,127% from a year earlier, CNBC reported. But against the quarter before, growth fell to about 47%, from 144% in the second quarter and 132% in the first. Revenue for the first nine months was $33.2 billion. The net loss for those nine months was $104 billion, most of it an accounting charge for the changing value of convertible notes, not cash. The operating loss was $1.3 billion. Anthropic now has about $895.4 billion in purchase commitments and leases, "substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure."

Two other numbers stand out. Customers paying $100,000 or more a year grew from about 950 to more than 11,900 in twelve months, and they bring in 74% of revenue. And the filing says the value of Anthropic's own internal Claude use, priced at what customers would pay, grew more than 250 times from January to September, and in April passed its monthly payroll, not counting stock pay. That's a list-price figure, not what the compute actually cost Anthropic. The filing also says Claude wrote over 90% of the contributions to Anthropic's codebase in September. Business Insider picked up the payroll line, which had 146 points on HN.

The line X fixed on is in the risk factors. Anthropic says its evidence about how fast AI is speeding up its own research comes from lagging indicators, so it "may in the future not identify, a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred." It then says "our internal estimates and our expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models, indicate that we may imminently meet" the policy's definition of "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI." No current model has been designated past that line. It also says it delayed pre-training a new frontier model for more than a month to tighten security against weight theft, and that it does "not anticipate being able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human supervision." It is considering rule changes that would require human sign-off on some research decisions. An X trending story on the disclosure ran through Monday afternoon, and a post by @MTSlive quoting the line passed 1,300 likes.

AI finds bugs faster than people can fix them

For a decade Postgres has shipped its major release between mid-September and mid-October. Version 19 will most likely slip to November, and maybe later, Postgres core developer Robert Haas wrote on Friday. Three large features were pulled between August 27 and September 15. His explanation is "LLMs happened." In the same April to September window, commits to the new release branch rose from 724 last year to 1,169, and fixes carried back to the previous release rose from 248 to 433, up more than 74%. The project didn't get sloppier, he says. People across the internet are using AI to find old bugs and report them, and committers spent the time they would have used reviewing the new release on fixing those instead. AI can draft fixes, but "they still need to be reviewed and committed by humans, and those humans have not gotten any smarter." His worry is the long run: "given that we have forty years of bugs that humans were unable to find but LLMs are quite capable of discovering, how are we ever going to catch up?" It had 787 points on HN.

Ubuntu is the same problem from the other side. CVE-2026-98432, the Linux TCP bug Anthropic's Claude turned into a remote root exploit, which this desk covered yesterday, is still listed as "Vulnerable" on Ubuntu's tracker for every release from 16.04 to 26.10, with fixes marked "work in progress" for the four newest. Debian shipped its fix on October 16. A post titled "WTF Ubuntu?" by a developer who took all his Ubuntu servers offline was the top story on HN on Monday at 874 points. On X, Pieter Levels said he may move all his servers to Debian "in this new post-AGI time of rapid cybersecurity hacks which require just as rapid patching." Until Canonical ships a kernel, the stopgaps from yesterday still apply. The learn section explains why the same two-line fix takes Ubuntu so much longer.

Not every breach this week needed AI. Trigger.dev, which runs background jobs for developers, said on Sunday that an attacker got the environment variables of some customer organizations. Those often hold API keys. It has blocked the exploit and emailed affected owners. If you use it, rotate your secrets.

OpenAI offers to switch off when the grid needs power

OpenAI launched what it calls the Grid Protectors Program on Monday, a promise that its future data center campuses will cut their power use when the local grid is strained. The first one is its planned campus in Effingham County, Georgia, near Savannah, which is expected to draw about 3,200 megawatts. Under a 25-year contract with Georgia Power, the utility can cut up to 1,000 megawatts of that during peak demand. OpenAI says that's about the average use of 700,000 homes. OpenAI also pays the full cost of the lines and substations to serve the site. Georgia Power says its large-load contracts, this one included, should save customers about $950 million a year from 2029. OpenAI hasn't named other sites. The point is political as much as technical, since local fights over data centers raising electricity bills are now an election issue. The learn section explains why this saves money.

Altman signed four deals in Abu Dhabi

Sam Altman was in Abu Dhabi on Monday for ADIPEC, the region's big oil and gas conference, and met Crown Prince Sheikh Khaled bin Mohamed bin Zayed, who watched four deals being signed, the Abu Dhabi Media Office said. The state oil company ADNOC will use OpenAI's models across its operations through G42's unit Inception42, which Reuters reports makes it the first energy company to use OpenAI's Frontier platform. The government is turning a set of its rules into a form AI can read, so the AI can help officials check permit and certificate applications. Its first pilot already runs on food imports, and officials still make the final call. The wealth fund Mubadala will use OpenAI's models for investment analysis, and Inception42 signed a five-year deal to sell OpenAI's models to UAE companies, government, and schools. The computing runs on GPUs inside the UAE. Altman told the conference AI "is going to transform the economics of the energy world." It was an X trending story Monday afternoon.

Claude watermarks four older models starting Tuesday

From November 3, text from Claude Mythos 5, Opus 4.7, Opus 4.6, and Sonnet 4.6 will carry Anthropic's invisible watermark, a pattern in which words the model picks, according to a customer email quoted by AI Lately and reported by The Independent. Newer models such as Opus 5.5 already have it. It applies worldwide, though it comes from the EU AI Act, and Anthropic's help page says it follows the text when it's copied and "may persist through some editing." Only approved groups such as regulators, researchers, and journalists can run the detector for now, so you can't check your own writing. Two catches: on Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6, changing the temperature or other sampling settings through the API turns the mark off, and older models like Opus 3 aren't covered yet. xAI is adding the same kind of mark to Grok API models from November 7, per its docs. An X trending story called the change Claude "snitching"; this desk explained how text watermarks work on October 5.

OpenAI built a test for everyday errands

OpenAI announced LifeVal on X on Monday, and the write-up explains it as a test of whether a model can actually get personal chores done, like finding childcare after a move, ordering groceries for a diet, making a budget, or dealing with a government form. OpenAI picked the tasks from two large time-use studies plus its own survey of 3,995 people about where they want help. Each task has its own grading rubric, since there's no single right answer to "pick a kindergarten." OpenAI's example scores for its current models, GPT-6 Astra among them, run from about 90% to 100%. OpenAI says it has used the test internally for a year. The task set isn't public, so nobody outside can check the numbers, a point AlphaSignal also makes. The post passed 3,000 likes.

Something to learn

Why does a data center that agrees to switch off save everyone money? A power grid has to be built for its busiest hour, not its average one. On the hottest afternoon of the summer, every air conditioner runs at once, and the utility needs enough power plants, lines, and substations to cover that peak. Much of that equipment sits idle most of the year, but customers still pay for it in their bills. A new 3,200-megawatt campus makes the peak bigger, so the utility would normally have to build more plants just for those few hot hours.

This kind of deal is called demand response. A big customer agrees that, when the grid is short, the utility can cut its power, here up to 1,000 of the campus's 3,200 megawatts. Then the utility can plan for a smaller peak and build less. The data center still pays for the lines it uses all year, so the fixed cost of the grid is spread over more electricity, which pushes the price per unit down for everyone. Why can an AI campus do this when a hospital can't? A training run saves its progress regularly, so it can pause or slow for a few hours and pick up again. Some work can move to another site. Batteries and on-site generators cover the rest. Answering live chats is harder to pause, which is why the deal covers a third of the load, not all of it.

Why does one bug fix turn into many commits? Big projects keep several old versions alive at once. Postgres supports each major release for five years, so a fix has to go into the newest code and then into each older branch people still run. That's called back-patching, or backporting. It's rarely a copy and paste. The code around the bug has changed between versions, so each branch needs its own version of the fix and its own testing. That's why Haas counts commits per branch: every bug AI finds in old code costs a human reviewer several times over.

Ubuntu has the same problem with Linux. Each Ubuntu release ships its own kernel, frozen near an older Linux version and then patched for years. Ubuntu's tracker lists fixes in progress for four different kernels, 7.3, 7.0, 6.8, and 5.15. The upstream fix is two lines, but Canonical has to fit it into each of those kernels, build them, test them on its cloud and hardware variants, and ship them. Debian shipped its fixed kernels on October 16. If you run servers, this is a good reason to know which kernel your distro actually ships, and how many versions it keeps patching.
