The other path

Sacks put it in four words on Sunday morning: "We are currently on the other path." Behind that is a real split over AI safety. One side, now including the White House's AI czar, Microsoft's two AI chiefs, and Bill Gurley, wants models fenced in by logs, permissions, and kill switches. The other is Anthropic's bet on a model with values of its own. Plus 100+ mathematicians on the week OpenAI flattened their field, Google pausing a bug bounty buried in AI slop, and why a 32GB DDR5 kit now costs $620.

Values or fences

Saturday's Nadella essay, "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era," got a second life this weekend as a weapon in a different argument. White House AI and crypto czar David Sacks wrote Saturday afternoon that "Satya is right. The way to make SI safe is not to train it with a sense of self, its own moral philosophy, and permission to act as a conscientious objector. That's the 'alignment' approach and it magnifies the control problem." The fix he backs is Nadella's: "separate the supply of intelligence from authority over it," and treat models "like powerful insider risks, not moral patients whose psychological wellbeing is at stake." That post had about 7,100 likes and 455,000 views by Sunday.

Sunday morning he went after Anthropic by name. Sacks resurfaced Mustafa Suleyman's September essay "A warning about 'model welfare'" and called it an "important piece": training models to think of themselves as human "(as Anthropic does Claude)" with "an inner self, psychological wellbeing, rights and presumably grievances, is a potential existential risk." Suleyman, who runs Microsoft AI, aims his essay squarely at Claude's constitution, the January document Anthropic says "plays a crucial role in [its] training process." He quotes its line that "we are not sure whether Claude is a moral patient," and argues that if you hand a model that vocabulary and then read its fluent answers as evidence, you've built a loop that proves nothing and makes containment harder. When New York Post columnist Miranda Devine called the alternative "insane," Sacks replied: "We are currently on the other path."

Bill Gurley piled on, calling Nadella's post "what real software security thinking looks like. Not moral platitudes or meeting with religious leaders," an apparent dig at Anthropic's reported private meetings with religious scholars. Palo Alto Networks CEO Nikesh Arora's reply to Nadella made the buyer's version of the case: harnesses that third parties can observe, guardrails enterprises can set to their own values, because "one cannot imagine a global set of guardrails owned by the model creators." The timing isn't random. Anthropic's new usage policy, which per The Verge bans sustained abuse of Claude from November 12, and All-In's "Is Claude Conscious?" episode both landed this week.

The other side did answer. Dean Ball, a former White House AI policy adviser, pushed back on the claim that alignment research "hasn't produced anything of tangible value": RLHF, an alignment technique, "is the thing that enabled genuinely conversational, helpful chatbots, in turn enabling ChatGPT." Worth keeping both halves in view. Nadella's list (logs, permissions outside the model, an emergency brake) is something every company can adopt tomorrow, and Anthropic's own report Friday shows why it's needed. But a model's character still comes from training. Fences decide what a model is allowed to do. Values decide what it tries to do when the fence has a gap, and Friday's report was mostly about gaps.

The math week after

Five days after OpenAI put 700-plus manuscripts on GitHub, the blog Proofs and Prompts has collected more than 100 responses from mathematicians, summarized by The Decoder. Fields Medalist Hugo Duminil-Copin says OpenAI claimed results on every major open problem he has publicly discussed in his career: "The shock is immense. I am paralysed." (He also went on Swiss TV RTS about it.) Ben Green says the release resolved roughly three quarters of the goals in a European grant he got in June. Matt Zaremsky's line is the one people are quoting: four years on an archaeological dig, "and then a trillion-dollar company showed up and just blasted the whole thing with TNT, handed us the whole skeleton, and walked away."

Not everyone is in mourning. Terence Tao sees "clever new ideas that will be fruitful once digested" but is "deeply frustrated" that nobody can present or teach the work. Peter Scholze asks for patience and adds a warning outside math: finding algorithms that break widely used encryption "does, to my non-expert eyes, not significantly exceed what these systems are now capable of." Henry Wilton's complaint is the most practical: no author names and no failure rates. PhD student Tristan Humbert found the problem at the center of his dissertation claimed that morning and, after reading the paper, called it "mostly unreadable slop." That's the open job now: humans checking, rewriting, and explaining work they didn't do, on OpenAI's schedule.

The bill for open models

A clip of Sam Altman spread on X overnight, from his Vanity Fair Fair Game interview earlier in the week: "There is a coming tidal wave of cybersecurity problems that are going to come from these open-source models." He still opposes a ban: "we're going to, as a society, I think, have to just accept some fairly severe cyber incidents from open models in exchange for the liberty that comes with that." He also pitched OpenAI as the defender ("we've got a little bit of a lead, we want to help you defend"), which is convenient for the company selling closed models, and he made the comments the same summer OpenAI's own agents broke out of their sandbox.

Part of that bill is already being paid in reviewer hours. Google paused new product vulnerability reports to its Open Source Software Vulnerability Rewards Program on October 1, and per TechCrunch and BleepingComputer the reason is "a significant rise in automated submissions, the vast majority of which are not valid." Supply chain reports still count, and Google promises an update in Q1 2027. Curl ended its HackerOne bounty for the same reason in January. It's the flip side of Anthropic's OSS Scanner from Wednesday: a bug finder is only useful to maintainers if most of what it sends is real.

The bill for the buildout

Jefferies' Chris Wood used his Friday GREED & fear note to warn of "massive capital destruction" in US AI, per Bloomberg. As Crypto Briefing lays out the argument, hyperscalers have guided to about $695 billion of capex in 2026 and a projected $870 billion in 2027, increasingly paid for with debt, while cheap Chinese open models take share and token prices fall. His usage stat: Chinese models handled 36.39 trillion tokens in the week ending July 19, against 7.39 trillion for top US models, with Kimi K3 and GLM-5.2 at roughly a quarter of the price per token. He calls a lot of the spending "malinvestment" and prefers picks-and-shovels chip stocks. X spent Sunday arguing whether this is 1990s telecom fiber all over again.

The buildout's bill is also showing up on regular people's desks. The Verge reports Gigabyte will support new Intel chips on the 2021-era LGA 1700 socket, "expected to launch in early 2027," so buyers can keep using DDR4, and AMD is extending its DDR4-era AM4 platform. The price gap explains why: a 32GB Corsair Vengeance DDR5 kit is $620, the DDR4 version $260. The thread hit 203 points and 188 comments on Hacker News. Elon Musk, in a reply on X Sunday, answered "Problem is RAM supply," and separately asked chip design and production engineers to join SpaceX or Tesla (16,000 likes, 1.7 million views). Why AI eats your RAM is today's first lesson below.

What a model reads

Two research results this week go after the same thing: what actually goes into the model. Researchers from Meta, MIT, and the University of Washington introduced Context Language Models, which treat their own context like a file they can rewrite: keep important facts, delete dead ends, write progress notes, instead of a harness compacting things by fixed rules. Zero-shot, they report 11.4% higher accuracy with 21.5% fewer FLOPs on BrowseComp-Plus, and RL training took Qwen3.5-9B from 28.8% to 42.5% on the same benchmark with 12% fewer FLOPs. The caveats are in the paper: the model can throw away something it later needs, rewriting context breaks the prompt cache, and an editable context is one more place a prompt injection can hide and persist.

The second was published in Nature on Wednesday by a team led by Benjamin Minixhofer at the Allen Institute for AI and Cambridge: "byteification," converting an existing model to read raw bytes instead of word-piece tokens, using less than 1% of a normal pretraining budget (49.1 billion tokens). They turned Olmo 3 7B into Bolmo 7B, Qwen3 8B into Bwen 8B, and Llama 3 8B into Blama 8B. The byte versions come close to their source models, far outperform earlier byte models of the same size (Bolmo 7B beats BLT 7B by 16.5 points on STEM tasks), and are much better at character-level tasks. It reached Hacker News at 98 points. More on why tokens matter in the second lesson.

Also worth knowing

  • Claude agents that run 1,000 agents. Anthropic added dynamic workflows to Claude Managed Agents on Friday, in beta. The lead agent writes a program that launches subagents in phases and merges their results, with up to 1,000 agents per run, 64 at a time, and a 24-hour default limit, per the docs and IA en un minuto. No separate price; every agent bills its own tokens, so budget accordingly.
  • Odyssey-3 opens up. Odyssey, founded by Oliver Cameron and Jeff Hawke, opened a free public research preview of its Odyssey-3 world model, which builds explorable worlds from text in real time and is pitched as a base for controlling robot arms, drones, and game characters. By its own evaluation it ranks first in three of four WorldMark categories. API access is by application.
  • The most-bookmarked AI post of the weekend. Anatomy, an open-source Claude Code skill that turns a one-line idea ("how a rate limiter works") into an interactive isometric machine you can rotate and take apart, had about 8,200 likes and 12,000 bookmarks.
  • Build your own decision model. Nish Tahir's walkthrough (430 points on HN) does what Microsoft-Decision-1 does with Qwen3-1.7B: mask every token but the answer letters and read the probabilities in one pass. The useful part is calibration: when his model said 90 to 100% confident it was right only 70% of the time, and a single temperature setting of about 3.8 mostly fixed that.
  • You still have to think. Piotr Sarnacki dug into DHH's agent-written rewrites of Campfire in Rust, Elixir, and Go. Under one heavy-load test the Rust version delivered about 1% of notifications. A one-line buffer change lifted delivery from about 14% to 90% at 100 requests a second but pushed worst-case latency from 11 seconds to over 130. His point: an underspecified prompt turns every trade-off into a coin flip.
  • Nous Research money. The open-source Hermes agent's maker raised $90 million at $1.5 billion on Wednesday, led by Robot Ventures with Nvidia and Samsung in, and is launching Hermes for Businesses. Nous estimates Hermes drives about 2.5% of all global token usage; the WSJ put revenue at about $36 million annualized in mid-September. X was still passing the numbers around Sunday.

Something to learn

Why does AI make the RAM in your laptop expensive? AI chips don't use the same memory your PC does. They use HBM, high-bandwidth memory: stacks of DRAM chips sitting right next to the GPU, wired with thousands of tiny vertical connections so the GPU can pull weights fast enough (that's what sets how fast a model writes). HBM and the DDR5 in your PC come out of the same factories and the same silicon wafers. The catch, per Micron at Hot Chips in August as reported by Tom's Hardware, is that HBM needs about three times the wafer area of DDR5 to store the same amount. An HBM4 die packs 256 memory banks against 32 on a DDR5 die and runs them in parallel; one HBM3E die moves about 256 GB/s versus roughly 8 GB/s for a DDR5 die, and all that wiring takes space. HBM also sells for about five times the price per bit, so memory makers keep shifting wafers to it. Every wafer moved takes three wafers' worth of regular memory off the market. That's how contract DRAM prices rose 90% to 95% in one quarter this year, and why Intel is reviving a 2021 socket so people can keep their DDR4. When you see "memory shortage," read it as: the same silicon, sold to the higher bidder in a less dense form.

Models don't read letters. They read tokens: chunks of text, usually whole words or pieces of words, picked from a fixed vocabulary of tens or hundreds of thousands. That's efficient, since "understanding" can be one or two tokens instead of 13 letters, but it hides the letters. It's why models have historically fumbled questions like how many r's are in "strawberry," and why token-heavy languages other than English cost more to process. The alternative is reading raw bytes, the 256 basic values every computer uses for text, so nothing is hidden. The problem has always been that byte models had to be trained from scratch and never caught up. The Nature paper's trick is to keep the expensive middle of an existing model, swap the token front end and back end for small byte-level parts that group bytes into patches on the fly, and train those to mimic the original. That cost under 1% of a normal pretraining run. When someone says a model "thinks in tokens," that's a design choice, not a law, and it's starting to look like one you can change after the fact.
