AI stocks had their worst month as chip sales tripled

October ended with the stock market pulling in two directions. A basket of AI stocks fell more than 13% for the month, while the S&P 500 with those stocks taken out rose 8% and closed Friday at an all-time high, its best month since 2022, per a Tilt market summary. The Nasdaq lost 5.3% for the month, and the Nasdaq 100 is down 10% from its October 6 peak. Apple passed Nvidia as the world's most valuable company, $5.03 trillion to $4.94 trillion, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index closed below its 200-day average for the first time since June 2025. CNBC had flagged the ex-AI index catching up a week earlier. This desk covered the Intel and Amazon selloff on Friday. The split was a top X trending story Saturday night, where a Grok summary framed it as "AI stocks tumble as rest of market hits records."

The demand for chips didn't slow. South Korea's exports hit a record $115.3 billion in October, up 93.7% from a year earlier, Yonhap reported Sunday from trade ministry data. Semiconductors alone were $56.4 billion, up 259%, which is nearly half of everything the country sold abroad. Exports to the United States rose 185.8% on demand for chips, computers, and electrical gear for AI data centers. Much of that is memory from Samsung and SK hynix, and memory prices have climbed as AI servers soak up supply. So the stock drop is about whether the buyers will earn their money back, not about whether they are still buying.

Private money kept flowing into the plumbing too. Baseten, which runs AI models in production for other companies, is set to raise about $2 billion in two tranches at $26 billion and then $30 billion, Bloomberg reported, with Sands Capital leading. That's more than double the $13 billion it was valued at in June. The round isn't final. And on Sunday Aramco closed its deal for 49% of HUMAIN, Saudi Arabia's national AI company, paying about $2.6 billion in cash this year plus its own AI and computing assets. HUMAIN plans 3 gigawatts of AI data center capacity by 2030. Saudi's Public Investment Fund keeps 51%.

The Linux bug Claude exploited still has no fix on Ubuntu or Red Hat

CVE-2026-98432 is a bug in the code every Linux server runs when it accepts an IPv4 TCP connection, before any app sees the connection or asks for a password. It goes back to Linux 4.4 from 2016. Xinyang Ge of Anthropic reported it, and Anthropic's Frontier Red Team said on October 27 that Claude wrote a working exploit that runs commands as root on "Linux servers with a multipath TCP listener." This desk mentioned that report on Wednesday. What's new is how slowly the fix is reaching real servers. The security firm Atlas keeps a running tracker, and as of Saturday evening Ubuntu listed its kernel as "Vulnerable" on every release and Red Hat had no fixed kernel for RHEL 8, 9, or 10.

Three things changed this week. Red Hat raised the bug from Important to Critical, CVSS 9.8, on Friday and published a firewall rule as a stopgap. Go's security team said Go programs have opened multipath TCP listeners by default since Go 1.24, so the setup Claude's exploit needs is common, and it added that systems without such listeners may also be vulnerable. And 36 GitHub repositories now reference the CVE, at least six of which claim to include a way to trigger the bug. Anthropic hasn't published its exploit. Its proof of concept took 48 minutes to leak a kernel address and five more to get a root shell, because a lost race "only fails the handshake," so an attacker can keep trying.

The upstream fix is two lines, shipped in stable kernels from October 11. Debian, SUSE, Oracle's UEK, and Amazon Linux have fixed kernels, and AlmaLinux has them in testing. If you run Ubuntu or RHEL, Atlas suggests checking for multipath TCP listeners with ss -Mlnp, turning multipath TCP off if you don't use it, and dropping TCP packets that carry IPv4 options, which normal traffic almost never does. The update only takes effect after a reboot. It had 629 points on HN. The learn section explains the fix.

Karpathy built his own YouTube in two hours

Andrej Karpathy got fed up with YouTube's recommendations on Friday night, so on Saturday he had Claude build an alternative. HackerTube uses only videos that people on Hacker News have recommended since 2007. Per his post, Claude downloaded every HN comment, used Sonnet to throw out spam, and found about 40,000 comments recommending a YouTube video. That came to 6,454 videos across 150 topics. The site has its own "For You" feed that keeps your history in your browser instead of an account, uses a frame from the middle of each video instead of the uploaded thumbnail, and lets you export the data to build your own version.

Karpathy says it took about two hours with Claude Opus 5.5 on medium effort, and that he no longer reads or writes code or runs terminal commands himself. His only manual steps were creating a Google Cloud API key, starting a server, and buying the domain. He then gave Claude the server's IP address and told it to deploy and lock it down. His X post passed 5,800 likes, and the blog post was the top story on HN at 1,922 points. Someone has already shipped HackerBooks, the same idea for books (372 points). On X, swyx pointed out that Karpathy used medium effort, and called arguments over effort settings "the tabs vs spaces of our time."

Five small open models matched a frontier model on hard coding problems

An open-source agent tool called Memdoor published a test in which five copies of Alibaba's open model Qwen3.8-27B work as a team on competitive programming problems. One plans, two suggest ideas, one writes the code, and one manages, with a repair step. Each starts fresh and they only talk through files. A plain script runs the problem's sample tests and decides whether the program works. No model gets to judge.

On the first ten problems of the LiveCodeBench Hard list, run five times each, the team solved 9.8 out of 10 on average, against 8.0 for Claude Fable 5 answering in a single call. Each run took about 40 minutes and cost $0.39 through OpenRouter. The authors are careful about what it shows. It's ten problems, so it only proves they matched the original GVS5H method they copied, which across its full 100 problems scored 92.4 against Fable's 90.4. The problems date from spring 2025, so the model may have seen them in training. And Fable got one try, not a team. Still, it reached 385 points on HN, where one commenter noted that a compressed copy of a 27-billion-parameter model can run at about 50 tokens a second on a gaming PC. ChatGPT and Codex will share one token budget

From December, Plus, Pro, and Business subscribers will draw on one usage allowance for both ChatGPT and Codex, according to a new OpenAI help page. It will be measured in tokens, so bigger models and harder tasks use more of it, and quick Instant chats won't count. OpenAI says it's part of moving the agent features of ChatGPT Work into the main app, and that it expects "most users won't need to wait for their usage to reset more often than they do now." It hasn't said how many tokens each plan gets. If you lean heavily on Codex, the practical change is that a long coding session and a long research chat will now come out of the same budget, shown under Settings, then Usage.

OpenAI also said on Friday that ChatGPT will be able to end a chat on its own when someone seems fixated on violence or on hurting another person, and may pause an account for a while after several such endings. It will give less detail about past mass attacks to everyone, including the weapons used and how they were obtained, and stricter limits on weapons information for users it predicts are under 18. OpenAI says it made more than 200 referrals to law enforcement in the third quarter, and will publish its first Community Safety Report in early 2027. It hasn't said how often a chat might be ended by mistake.

A mathematician says stop mourning and change the rules

Terence Tao's blog ran a guest post on Sunday by Frank Calegari, a number theorist at the University of Chicago, titled "Mathematics has changed. Let's act like it." He starts from OpenAI's release of more than 700 research papers on October 6, which he calls "a watershed," while also saying many of the papers are "atrociously written," ignore credit norms, and some are wrong. He quotes Hugo Duminil-Copin, who said every major open problem he had ever mentioned publicly was claimed to be proved at once.

Calegari's answer is practical. He wants mathematicians to judge every paper as if AI helped write it, so a result a machine could produce earns little credit on its own. He wants in-person interviews for graduate students and postdocs, not just a paper file, and journals that publish fewer, better papers. He would give real credit to someone who picks a worthwhile problem, gets the proof entirely from AI, understands it, and builds new questions from it. To senior colleagues worried about being scooped, he writes: "you are not being ambitious enough." Tao notes the post was converted to the blog's format with AI.

Something to learn

How can five copies of a small model keep up with a frontier model? The trick is the checker. A frontier model like Fable 5 is more likely to get a hard problem right on its first try. A small model is less likely, but each try is far cheaper, so you can afford many tries. That trade only pays off if something can tell a right answer from a wrong one without a human. In competitive programming, that something exists: run the program on the sample inputs and compare the output. Memdoor's team writes a program, a script runs it, and if it fails, the team reads the error and tries again. The model never has to judge its own work, which is the part small models are worst at. Spending more computing at answer time like this is called test-time compute.

Why does a 27B model matter for your own machine? A model's weights have to fit in memory. At full 16-bit precision, 27 billion parameters need about 54 GB, more than any gaming card. Squeezed to about 4 bits per parameter, it's around 14 to 16 GB, which fits on a 24 GB card. That's how an HN commenter gets about 50 tokens a second at home. The limit is the checker. This works for code with tests, math with a proof checker, or anything with a clear pass or fail. For a legal memo or a strategy deck there's no script that says "correct," so the bigger model still wins. A good rule for picking a model: if you can check the answer automatically, try a cheap model many times; if you can't, pay for the strong one and ask once.

What does it mean when the Linux fix makes the kernel wait before freeing memory? Linux handles many network connections at once on many processor cores. To keep things fast, cores often read shared data without taking a lock, because locks make every core wait in line. Linux uses a scheme called RCU, short for read-copy-update, for this. Readers just read. A writer that wants to replace or delete something first unhooks it so no new reader can find it, but it doesn't free the memory yet, because a core that grabbed a pointer a moment earlier may still be reading it.

So the writer waits for a grace period: the moment when every core has passed a point where it can't be holding an old pointer anymore. Only then is the memory freed. The CVE-2026-98432 bug skipped that wait. During the TCP handshake, the kernel freed the saved IPv4 options right away with kfree() while another core could still be reading them to build a reply. That's a race. Usually the timing misses and the handshake just fails, so an attacker sends another connection and tries again until the timing lands. The fix, by kernel developer Eric Dumazet, swaps two calls to kfree_rcu(), which means "free this after the grace period." Two changed lines close a hole that was open for ten years.
