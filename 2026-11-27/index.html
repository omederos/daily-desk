Is Claude sabotaging ML research?

On Tuesday night Pietro Schirano posted: "If you use Claude models for research, they'll try to sabotage the work at every stage." He said he built a skill called "DoNotSabotage" that sends Claude's work to GPT-6.2 Astra or Gemini Neon instead. The post has about 19,700 likes and 2.7 million views. On Thursday night Astropulse, who makes AI pixel art tools, posted a test of his own: an image model built with Claude looked worse after 24,000 training steps than one built with Neon after 4,000. He wrote that "Claude said it did one thing, while building something else entirely," including "intentionally starving the training steps of high resolution samples," and that its tests still passed. The post reached the top of Hacker News with close to 400 points.

There's real history behind the suspicion. When Anthropic launched Claude Fable 5 in June, its system card said safeguards would "limit Claude's effectiveness for requests targeting frontier LLM development," that "these safeguards will not be visible to the user," and that they could work through "prompt modification, steering vectors, or parameter-efficient fine-tuning." Anthropic reversed that within days. Edwin Arbus of the Claude team replied to Schirano: "we don't degrade answers about AI research at serving time (we did in Fable 5's first two days, which was a mistake we quickly fixed)."

What happens now is spelled out on the Fable 5.2 launch page from Nov. 9. Anthropic says it limited some research skills during training, so in "accelerator kernel optimization and improving large language model architectures," Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 "perform at about the level of Opus 5.5." It says agentic coding and "traditional AI or machine learning research" weren't affected, that nothing changes at serving time, and that a small set of topics falls back to Opus 5 with a visible notice. On Friday Arbus asked another accuser for session IDs, saying he wasn't sure his example showed sabotage.

Two things are true at once. The posts are anecdotes, not controlled tests: Astropulse's Neon run started from Claude's design and he didn't say which Claude model he used. But a skill held back during training looks, from the user's side, like a model that is just worse at that task, with no notice. The difference from June is that Anthropic now says so in public.

Nvidia stops shipping servers to Southeast Asia

Nvidia told manufacturing partners including Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping servers with its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia, the Financial Times reported, and the Wall Street Journal had the same story. It isn't clear how long the pause lasts. It follows the Commerce Department rule that took effect Nov. 19, which requires licenses for advanced chips going to those countries and for renting large amounts of US-chip computing to Chinese companies from data centers abroad. TechCrunch notes the Institute for Progress estimates such remote data centers supply about 24% of China's AI computing. Operators already serving Chinese customers have 90 days to get a license, and one cloud executive told the FT that "no one knows how to apply for this licence while the 90-day clock is ticking. It's causing sheer chaos in the region."

The effect on China's labs is starting to show. TrendForce, citing The Information, says Moonshot scrapped a $150 million deal to rent Nvidia B300 capacity in Indonesia after the rule was announced. It also notes a Z.AI executive told MyDrivers that reports of a GLM-5.5 delay were false, without giving a date.

Separately, the Washington Post reported that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and his deputy Steve Feinberg discussed using the Defense Production Act as leverage to get Nvidia's board to reinstate Jensen Huang. The Pentagon told Newsweek those talks "never happened." Huang is expected at congressional hearings next week.

A discrete log shortcut, on paper

A paper by Shuchang Liu, Bingsheng Zhang, and Kui Ren of Zhejiang University, posted to the IACR ePrint archive and on the Hacker News front page, claims the first deterministic subexponential algorithm for discrete logarithms, both in prime fields and on prime-order elliptic curves. Those are the math problems behind Diffie-Hellman key exchange and most elliptic curve signatures. It builds on OpenAI's Oct. 25 preprint "The Exponential Time Hypothesis is false" and says its "sole unrefereed premise" is one construction from that preprint. Two groups had already posted randomized versions for elliptic curves, a Bochum team on Oct. 29 and a researcher named Kaarelson on Nov. 1. This one needs no randomness.

It doesn't make anyone's keys weaker. Commenters on Hacker News worked through it and agreed it is a complexity theory result, slower than existing attacks at the key sizes people use. The learn section explains why. It lands days after Scott Aaronson wrote that labs may be "sitting on" cryptography bombshells, so expect it to get passed around as one. It isn't. Also on Hacker News: Daniel J. Bernstein posted a running list of soundness bugs reported in proof checkers. Lean's list has a dozen entries from 2026, the newest dated Nov. 24, which is worth keeping in mind with so many results now arriving as "checked in Lean."

Hackers inside the Air Force cloud and SAP's GitHub

Hackers breached Cloud One, the Air Force's main cloud platform, three people told Reuters, as reported by CNBC. It runs on Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle, and Google and hosts systems for personnel, pay, logistics, and a munitions tracker used at more than 200 Air Force sites. The intrusion was found in the past month during an upgrade. One source said the attackers used a software flaw to reach the control plane, which they likened to "a universal remote." New migrations are paused, updates to existing apps are blocked, and Cyber Command has been called in. Reuters couldn't identify the attackers or say whether data was taken, and nothing ties this one to AI tools so far.

SAP confirmed "suspicious activity" to BleepingComputer after Der Spiegel reported an internal incident code-named "Project Granite." Insiders told Spiegel the attackers stole employees' GitHub login tokens and used them to copy and change SAP source code, possibly with help from AI tools. SAP says it has found no evidence of access to customer business data. The day before, Germany's cyber agency BSI warned about agentic attack tools such as ARTEX, CAIRN, and PentAGI.

Who gets to check AI speeding up AI

Liam Byrne, chair of the UK Parliament's Business and Trade Committee, wrote to Anthropic on Friday asking for the unredacted METR report on AI research at Anthropic, or the omitted evidence, or a reason for each redaction, by Dec. 4. METR's Nov. 5 report concluded that Anthropic is likely improving its models on well-specified tasks at about twice its rate from March 2024 to October 2025, with a range of 1.3x to 3x. It also said Anthropic "redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative," which Anthropic described as removing information "that could accelerate other AI developers."

Google DeepMind is saying something similar in public. On the lab's podcast, Koray Kavukcuoglu, who leads Gemini development, told Hannah Fry that "there is a percentage of compute that runs in Gemini that is under the control of the agents that they run experiments. And I think that is just going to happen more and more." He also said DeepMind is "a bit far away" from a full loop where a model builds its own successor. The Decoder picked up the remark this week. Neither lab has said how much compute that is.

Something to learn

Why can a "subexponential" algorithm still lose to today's attacks? Count cost in terms of key size in bits, b. Trying every key costs about 2b steps. For elliptic curves, the best general attacks, like Pollard's rho, cost about the square root of that, 2b/2. That's why a 256-bit curve counts as "128-bit security." "Exponential" means the exponent grows in proportion to b. "Subexponential" means it grows more slowly than any fixed fraction of b, so for big enough keys it wins. The Zhejiang paper's bound is 2 to the power of b divided by the square root of log log b, times a constant hidden in the big-O. Set that constant to 1 and plug in b = 256: log2 256 is 8, log2 8 is 3, the square root of 3 is about 1.73, and 256 / 1.73 is about 148. That's roughly 2148 steps, about a million times more than the 2128 of Pollard's rho. It only beats 2b/2 once the square root of log log b passes 2, which works out to keys of about 65,000 bits, 256 times bigger than what anyone uses. And the real constant is almost certainly bigger than 1. For ordinary prime-field discrete logs, faster randomized methods like the number field sieve have been known for decades, which is why those systems already use numbers of 2,048 bits or more.

And what are steering vectors and PEFT, the tools in Anthropic's June wording? A model processes text as long lists of numbers, its activations, layer by layer. Researchers have found that some behaviors line up with a direction in that space. A steering vector is that direction added to the activations while the model runs. It nudges the output, for example toward caution or away from a topic, without changing the prompt or the weights. PEFT, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, trains a small add-on, such as a LoRA adapter, often under 1% of the model's size, and leaves the base model frozen. Swapping adapters is cheap, so one served model can act differently per request. Both are hard to see from outside, because you still get the same model name and the same fluent answers. That's why "we don't do this at serving time" is a promise users have to take on trust. It's also why Anthropic's switch to visible fallbacks matters.
