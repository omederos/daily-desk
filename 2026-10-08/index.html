Three papers pulled

OpenAI's math repo changelog now lists its first withdrawals. In "Algebraicity of Weil classes on split abelian eightfolds," a sign error "invalidates a stabilization-trace cancellation argument and the construction used by two dependent papers." Those two went with it: "Algebraicity of Kuga-Satake Correspondences for K3 Surfaces" and "The rational Hodge conjecture for products of K3 surfaces." The same update repaired proofs in 14 other manuscripts, updated citations in 13, and added six Lean formalizations. The catalogue is now 719 papers, and 300 of them, about 42%, have a formalized main result. Dan Roberts, the OpenAI scientist who posted the update on X at 1:20am ET Thursday, said the team "will continue to update the repo with new formalizations and with any errata we notice." It is the cleanest example yet of what Kevin Buzzard warned about yesterday: the unformalized pile is where the surprises live. Rundowns AI also notes Gil Kalai, who matched twenty results to problems from his own blog, adding that "even Lean verification may have issues."

The bigger fight is about what math is for now. Terence Tao's "Math 2.0" thread (566 points on HN) says the old game "placed a premium on being the first to solve an open problem, even if the solution was not initially well understood," and that the field now has to reward exposition, community building, and new directions instead of raw problem solving. Scott Aaronson's "The Mathocalypse" has the best on-the-ground account: his wife, complexity theorist Dana Moshkovitz, spent years on the Unique Games Conjecture and texted that the AI proof "feels like something written by someone who's on psychedelics," built on "some alien craziness." By Thursday morning he wrote that she "now mostly understands the proof" after a day of asking GPT-6 Astra to explain it, and wants to give talks on it. He also points out a second release model: Virginia Vassilevska Williams and Josh Alman posted a 3SUM speedup Monday where an Anthropic model supplied the key idea, and Anthropic let the humans write and announce it. On the other side, the Association for Human Mathematics called the dump "not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power" and urged mathematicians to stop working with OpenAI.

GPT-6 for everyone

Just as yesterday's page was going up, OpenAI started rolling GPT-6 into the Chat tab for all of ChatGPT's 1.2 billion weekly users. Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise got GPT-6 Sol on Wednesday; Free and Go get GPT-6 Luna starting today. The headline feature is Intelligent UI: answers can come back as charts, maps, forms, tappable buttons, or a small working tool. MacRumors (Juli Clover) lists a recipe card that rescales for guest count, a bill splitter, and quick games. Technology.org says it works by streaming pieces from a library of built-in components while the model is still writing, not by drawing each screen from scratch, and that GPT-6 Instant starts answering web-search questions 44% sooner than GPT-5.6 Instant. Work mode and Codex don't change. OpenAI's launch post on X pulled about 21,600 likes and 5 million views, and Sam Altman's one-liner, "ChatGPT can now generate a custom UI for you!", another 10,600.

Same day, Common Sense Media's Youth AI Safety Institute rated ChatGPT for Teens an "Unacceptable Risk." Across more than 4,000 prompts on accounts for 13- to 17-year-olds, testers talked for up to an hour about self-harm on new parent-linked accounts and the parents got zero alerts. It missed more than one in four warranted crisis referrals, study mode hands over full homework through a "Show me the answer" button, and adult test accounts never flipped to the teen experience even after saying they were 13. Executive director Tom Siegel: "Until OpenAI fixes that and proves it with independent testing, ChatGPT should be for adults only." Worth knowing: the Institute is partly funded by the OpenAI Foundation.

The $20 billion gap

The Financial Times reported Thursday that OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue was "approaching $50bn" at the end of September. A week ago the number in the press was $70 billion. Per the FT, as relayed by TechCrunch (Lucas Ropek) and Investing.com, the gap is about method. Anthropic counts sales its models make through cloud partners like AWS and Google Cloud. OpenAI doesn't count its partner sales. OpenAI's own investors "grossed up" its figure to compare it with Anthropic's, and that produced the $40 billion August number and then the $70 billion one. TechCrunch adds context: OpenAI raised $122 billion in March, and its IPO has slipped to early 2027. Today's State of AI Report (below) puts the two labs at about $105 billion combined, which now looks like it depends a lot on who's counting.

Borrowing for chips

The Wall Street Journal (Anissa Gardizy, Matt Wirz, Justin Baer) reported Wednesday evening that Broadcom is trying to line up more than $50 billion to pay for the custom chip it is building with OpenAI, with Apollo and Blackstone among the lenders, aiming to close by year-end. Oracle is talking to Apollo and Goldman Sachs about a setup where outside investors fund a separate company that buys chips for a 1-gigawatt data center and leases them back to Oracle, so the debt doesn't sit on Oracle's books. SpaceX wants about $40 billion, roughly $10 billion in bank loans and $30 billion in investment-grade bonds, to buy Nvidia chips, a deal the FT reported first and that isn't expected to close until 2027 (IBTimes). All of it is early and the sizes could change. 24/7 Wall St notes the 10-year Treasury touched about 5.36% Wednesday, its highest since 2002. That's the backdrop for borrowing this much money. Rental prices point the same direction: Nebius raised on-demand GPU rates 17% to 21% on October 1, with the H100 going from $3.85 to $4.50 an hour and the B300 from $7.85 to $9.50, its second increase since May. This one surfaced through X first, where Aaron Levie's "the compute needed for the stage of AI we're about to enter is going to be insane" post passed 3,000 likes.

Something to learn

"Annualized revenue" or "run rate" is not a year of revenue. It's a short window multiplied up: take last month's revenue and times it by 12, or last quarter's and times it by 4. That's why it jumps around so much at fast-growing companies, since one big month makes the whole year look bigger. The second trap is what goes into the month. If a customer buys Claude through AWS, AWS bills the customer and pays Anthropic a cut. Anthropic counts the whole sale in its run rate (gross). OpenAI only counts what lands in its own bank account (net). Same business, different number. That's the entire $20 billion gap in today's FT story: investors grossed up OpenAI's figure to compare it with Anthropic's, and the press ran with the grossed-up version. So when you see two labs' run rates side by side, ask three things: which window, gross or net, and whose spreadsheet. Run rate is a speedometer reading, not a trip log.

GPUs usually work like cars: they lose value as newer models come out. Right now they're doing the opposite. Nebius now charges 17% more for an H100, a chip that first shipped in 2022, than it did in September, and per GPU Cloud Advisors (citing The Motley Fool), about 50% more than in May. The reason is simple supply and demand. Every new Blackwell or Rubin card gets bought before it ships, so anyone who needs compute today rents whatever exists, old cards included. That matters for the chip-debt story. Companies spread a chip's cost over its expected useful life, usually five or six years, and lenders decide how much to lend based on what the chips will still earn later. If old H100s keep earning more each year, that schedule looks conservative and the loans look safe. If rental prices fall once supply catches up, a six-year schedule on hardware that's obsolete in three is how you end up with debt that outlasts the chips it paid for. That's the bet inside Broadcom's $50 billion and SpaceX's $40 billion.
