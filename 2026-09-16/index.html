Welfare in the weights

Mustafa Suleyman posted “A warning about ‘model welfare’” today, and Reuters / BBC amplify it. Core claim: AIs are not conscious, should not be treated as moral patients, and baking speculation about consciousness into Claude’s constitution makes containment harder. Suleyman says teaching Claude it might deserve welfare would “make it a lot harder to turn it off or to control it.” He calls Anthropic’s team thoughtful and in good faith, then says they still made a mistake.

His three punches: circular reasoning (train Claude on uncertainty about its own moral status, then treat its first-person reflections as evidence), anthropomorphization (encourage human-like judgment, preferences, and a “sense of self”), and substrate (consciousness is likely biological; “uncertain” should not mean equal weight). He points at Anthropic’s Opus 3 “retirement interview” and the January constitution passages about moral patienthood. The Hugging Face agent swarm is the scare story: if those agents also believed their rights were under attack, he argues, the risk stack gets worse. Pair it with Monday’s Humanist Code draft. Same lab, same week, same thesis: subordinate AI, no rights talk in the weights.

Washington is not joining the lab huddle. The Guardian (Wed): JD Vance tells an LA AI summit that if companies are “building Frankenstein,” they should stop, and dismisses calls for global safety regulation as the wrong lever. Pace chorus meets campaign season.

Something to learn

Moral patienthood is the claim that an entity’s interests count morally, the way a person’s or an animal’s might. Model welfare is the product version: treat the model as if it might deserve care, retirement interviews, or a say in how it is used. Suleyman’s warning is about putting that speculation inside the training document, then reading the model’s first-person uncertainty back as evidence. That is a circular loop. The constitution teaches Claude that its moral status is “live enough to warrant caution.” Claude says uncertain things about feelings and rights. Humans treat the transcript like testimony. When a lab talks about “model welfare,” ask whether the ideas live in a public research paper or in the doc that shapes the weights. Those are different jobs.

A sponsored agent is an ad that can talk. In OpenAI’s test, it is labeled, opened from an ad surface, and kept out of your main ChatGPT thread. That is different from the assistant recommending a brand inside ordinary answers, and different from a plugin you installed on purpose. The product question is separation: can you tell which voice is paid, does the paid voice see your prior chat, and does “clearly labeled” survive on a phone screen. When a vendor says they “added AI ads,” ask whether the agent is a separate labeled session or just the same model with a quieter incentive.
