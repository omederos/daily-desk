Mistral goes to a trillion

Mistral (Tue) put Mistral Large 4 into public preview. Unofficially ML4, "very officially: le Chonk." It is a 1 trillion-parameter, natively multimodal model with 49B active per token, trained from scratch on 3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs in Mistral's own European datacenters, and the preview API on Mistral Studio is served from the same machines. Weights come by the end of the month; The Next Web pins it to October 27. Until then Mistral is red-teaming with cybersecurity firms, vetted partners, and state authorities, who get a version with reduced moderation and expanded cyber capabilities. Mistral's own numbers, which it calls preliminary: 61.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, 28.3% on Terminal-Bench 4, and a Coding Agent Index of 49.8, ahead of DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 and Qwen3.8 Max. The cyber pitch is the loud part. ML4 scores 82% on the Artificial Analysis test that asks a model to reproduce a real open-source vulnerability and then patch it, the top score of any model, and Mistral says Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra score near zero there because they refuse the task. In a blind Surge AI coding review it placed second of five (3.74), behind Claude Opus 5 (4.22) and ahead of GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3. On visual grounding it edges GPT-6 Astra on Dense 200, 42% to 41%. Training data covered 160+ languages. The framing is sovereignty: if a closed model refuses mid-incident, defenders lose the tool, so ship open weights you can run on-prem. Tech.eu notes Mistral raised about $3.4bn in September at around a $24bn valuation. HN pushed it past 1,000 points, mostly "big deal, if the benchmarks hold."

Reflection's Beam, a day earlier

Reflection AI (Mon) introduced Beam, its first open-weight model: a sparse mixture-of-experts with 501B total, 23B active, built for coding, reasoning, and agents. Pretraining used 23.8 trillion tokens. Then came a very big reinforcement learning run: 10.5K NVIDIA GB300s for four weeks, more than 100 million rollouts at up to 256K context, about 1.3 billion sandboxes, and a million coding, agent, and STEM environments. Reflection calls it one of the largest RL runs any open lab has done and says scores kept climbing with no plateau. The headline claim is efficiency: reasoning scores close to GLM-5.2 with 3 to 4 times less inference compute. Its own table is more modest than the chart. On DeepSWE v1.1 Beam posts 44.4 against Kimi K3's 68.0 and GLM-5.3's 61.0, and an HN commenter called out the stronger open models sitting "behind the fold." Weights, a technical report, and a model card come later this month; Quartz says Apache 2.0 and a 1M-token context. Side by side with Mistral: both are Western, both are open weights "soon," and both lean on each lab's own benchmark runs, so wait for outside evals before you pick one.

Chip money and an AI-built TPU

Etched at $40B+

TechCrunch (Marina Temkin, Mon): Etched is reviewing unsolicited bids from $40B (top-tier investors) up to $50B (lesser-known backers), a couple of months after raising $700M at $21B led by Jane Street. Before that came $300M at $10.3B led by Sequoia. Etched says it has $1B in customer orders, Jane Street included, after making its test chip at TSMC this summer. About 15% of its 400 staff came from Nvidia, per the WSJ, and it runs a 10-megawatt data center in Silicon Valley. Talks are early and Etched declined to comment.

openTPU on a Kintex-7

openTPU (FeSens on GitHub) is "an open-source AI accelerator, developed by AI": SystemVerilog hardware, an instruction set, a bit-exact simulator, a kernel language and compiler, and host software, all in one readable repo with 1,356 commits. It runs real models with real weights on an Inspur PCIe card with a Xilinx Kintex-7 FPGA and two DDR3 channels (17.1 GB/s peak), at 133 MHz with a four-column systolic matrix unit, and the card's tokens match the simulator bit for bit. Speeds are small: Qwen3-0.6B at 21.6 tok/s in int8, LFM2.5-230M at 85.8 tok/s in 4-bit, Phi-4-mini (3.8B) at about 4 tok/s, while using 82 to 94% of the memory bandwidth. The HN title says "AI is now capable of developing its own inference hardware." Fairer version: agents wrote chip logic that holds up on a real FPGA. It is not silicon and not fast, but it's a great way to learn how an accelerator works from a Python matmul down to the wires.

Erdős Problems shuts the comment box

Thomas Bloom (Tue), who runs erdosproblems.com (1,221 problems, 9,000+ comments, about 2,000 registered users, 10,000 to 25,000 visitors a day), says the main public use of the site is now people posting AI-generated proofs with no explanation, as an "(increasingly meaningless) priority claim." So he is freezing new problem comments and proof claims, dropping the open/solved statuses and the solved count, dropping credit language for future solutions whether human or AI, and leaning into high-quality written expositions. He thinks repositories for AI proofs should exist, just not on his site: "Just as one does not open a restaurant in an abattoir." Same week, Vals AI (Geby Jaff, Oct 4) shared two room-temperature antiferromagnetic semiconductor candidates found by a team of Claude Opus 5.5 agents, one newly designed and one first made in 1999. Those are predictions from calculations, published with the code and a list of caveats, not lab results. That one drew 448 points on HN.

ChatGPT signs other people's cartoons

Nieman Lab (Andrew Deck, Mon): ask ChatGPT for "a New Yorker-style cartoon" and it can sign it with a real cartoonist's name. A viral cartoon of Dolly Parton and Tim Curry at heaven's front desk carried "BLOPER," the pen name of Brendan Loper, who did not draw it. Deck found signatures of Harry Bliss, Emily Flake, Joe Dator, and others in Reddit threads, and his own tests produced George Booth, Liza Donnelly, Ellis Rosen, and Saul Steinberg. Loper: "my name is my name. It felt very much like a violation of my personhood." On HN (524 points), gwern said Nano Banana Pro does it too and he edits fake signatures out of his own comics.

Utah lets an AI prescribe, for acne

TechSpot, citing Bloomberg (Tue): Utah will let Nolla Health examine patients and prescribe without a doctor in the loop, the first state to do so. Scope is tight: a one-year pilot, mild-to-moderate acne only, through a $4.99-a-month app. You scan your face and fill in a medical history, the AI scores severity, and it picks from eight approved topical treatments. Oversight steps down in phases: doctors check the first 100 prescriptions before they go out, the next 400 go straight to the pharmacy with a weekly look-back, then a doctor reviews at least 10% each month. CEO Luis Wenus says side effects are limited to skin irritation or dryness. One HN commenter's take: if a 20-minute telehealth visit with a PA is enough screening for these drugs, it's hard to argue the AI isn't.

Something to learn

What a model costs to run per answer is roughly 2 × active parameters × tokens it writes. That's the formula Reflection used for Beam's chart. Each token touches every active weight once, and each weight does a multiply and an add, so that's the 2. Beam at 23B active is about 46 billion operations per token. Mistral Large 4 at 49B active is about 98 billion. But the second number matters just as much. A model that thinks for 20,000 tokens at 23B active costs about the same as one that thinks for 10,000 at 46B. So "3 to 4 times less inference compute" can come from being smaller per token, from writing shorter reasoning, or from both, and you should ask which. The formula also skips reading your prompt and the extra attention work on long context, so treat it as a floor, not the bill.

Napkin math for how fast a chip can write: tokens per second is about memory bandwidth divided by the bytes of weights it has to read per token. openTPU makes it easy to check. The card ran at about 16 GB/s. Phi-4-mini is 3.8B parameters, so in int8, one byte each, that's about 3.8 GB per token. 16 ÷ 3.8 is about 4.2, and the card measured 3.99 tok/s. Cut the weights to 4-bit and there are fewer bytes to read, so it went to 6.56. That's why quantizing makes local models faster, not just smaller. For a mixture-of-experts model, the bytes per token are only the active experts, but every expert still has to fit in memory somewhere.
