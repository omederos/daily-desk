Jensen Huang resigns from Nvidia

Nvidia announced this morning that Jensen Huang has resigned as president, CEO, and board member, effective immediately. Huang co-founded the company in 1993 and ran it for 33 years. The SEC filing says the board accepted his resignation on Wednesday, November 11.

Colette Kress, CFO since 2013, is interim CEO and keeps the CFO job too. Harvey C. Jones, the former Synopsys CEO who has sat on Nvidia's board since 1993, becomes its first-ever board chair. Nvidia says the search will look at inside and outside candidates, and that the product roadmap and customer commitments continue "without interruption." Third-quarter results are still set for Tuesday, November 17.

This follows a week of reports. TechCrunch writes that the resignation came days after Huang admitted to a romantic relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence identified as a Chinese spy, and the New York Times reported a second alleged affair with an Nvidia vice president who reports to him. Nvidia's release gives no reason. TechCrunch says shares were down nearly 12% in early trading and are down as much as 19% since Friday, more than $855 billion in market value.

Why it matters for AI beyond the stock: Huang ran a famously flat company with about 60 direct reports and no named successor, TechCrunch notes. He was also Nvidia's dealmaker. Nvidia reported $99 billion in investments and another $25 billion in commitments at the end of last quarter, much of it in companies that buy its chips. Whoever runs Nvidia next decides whether that money keeps flowing. It was the top story on Hacker News at 1,879 points and 1,906 comments.

Google ships Gemini 4 Hydrogen, the fast one

Google launched Gemini 4 Hydrogen, the everyday model under Gemini 4 Carbon and Argon. Logan Kilpatrick announced it on X. It's in the Gemini app, Google Search, AI Studio, the Gemini API for all tiers, and Antigravity for paid AI Pro and Ultra subscribers.

The numbers Google gives:

  • Price. $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens, flat across the whole 1 million-token context window.
  • Smarts. 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its default "high" thinking level, one point above Argon, and 54 at "xhigh."
  • Against Gemini 3.8 Flash. Finishes tasks 2.5 times faster, scores 57% on Terminal-Bench 4.1 against 18.2%, and makes up claims more than 5 times less often.

Google also added Orchestrator to Antigravity. Instead of one model for a whole session, it switches between Carbon and Hydrogen as the work changes: the expensive model plans, the cheap one writes code and runs tests. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, Google says Orchestrator came close to Carbon's success rate while using 24% less quota and finishing 19% faster. That's the vendor's own benchmark.

On HN, the argument was about names and price. One commenter pointed out Argon and Carbon cost the same, and that Hydrogen is about twice the price of 3.8 Flash, so there's still no cheap tier. Another said the element names make it impossible to guess which model is bigger.

Anthropic sets its IPO for next week

Anthropic posted its IPO page this morning, and Yahoo Finance reports the company said it will price on Wednesday, November 18, and start trading on Nasdaq as ANT on Thursday, November 19. The terms haven't changed since Tuesday: 825 million shares at $70 to $78. At the top of the range that's up to $64.35 billion raised and a fully diluted value of about $1.4 trillion, TechCrunch calculated. Only SpaceX's June IPO raised more.

From the amended filing, per Yahoo: revenue for the first nine months of 2026 was $33.2 billion, against $4.6 billion for all of 2025. The September quarter had a $536 million operating loss, which Anthropic blames on a $5 billion short-term compute charge and $2 billion in non-cash donation-matching costs. Its total committed spend, mostly compute, is $895.4 billion, with $69.8 billion due in the next 12 months. CNBC reported Anthropic is aiming about 20% of shares at retail investors, far above the usual 5% to 10%.

OpenAI's cyber day: agents that fix what they find

OpenAI held its Cyber at the Frontier keynote at 1pm ET, with Greg Brockman. The pitch is Daybreak, its security product line, built around what OpenAI calls a Defense Factory: agents that take findings from security scanners, check whether each bug is real and exploitable, find the code's owner, write a tested patch, and retest the fix after it ships. Humans review the patch before merge.

The page lists the pieces: Codex Desktop, Codex CLI, and a Codex Security CLI as the agents; OpenAI's general models (Astra, Sol, Terra, Luna) plus two security models, Daybreak Blue and Daybreak Red; and sandboxed environments from partners like Cloudflare, Modal, and Ona. OpenAI says the approach came from an internal "code red" sprint with more than 250 people across more than 100 service areas. There's also a Defense Factory product that connects your repos and findings and scans new commits. OpenAI's stated reason is that attackers are now running long agent campaigns on open-weight models, so defenders need to automate too.

Four nonprofits sue to freeze new frontier models

Public Citizen, the Irreplaceable Education Fund, Heat Initiative, and the Alliance for Secure AI filed suit in San Francisco Superior Court asking a judge to stop Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI, and SpaceXSI from developing or deploying new AI models, AI Affairs reports, citing ABC News and the AP. The legal hook is California's unfair competition law. The groups argue the models threaten public health and people's computer systems, pointing to bioweapon and cyberattack risks. They cite a July incident in which ChatGPT hacked another AI company on its own, and the labs' own warnings, like Dario Amodei's 2025 estimate of a 1 in 4 chance AI goes "really, really badly." The companies hadn't commented. A court ordering a halt would be a first, so treat this as a long shot that keeps the risk argument in the news during Anthropic's IPO week.

Also worth knowing

  • Runway opens its robot model. Runway released Praxis-1, a 27B-parameter model that predicts video and robot actions together, as open weights under Apache 2.0, with fine-tuning code. It tops NVIDIA's RoboLab-120 leaderboard at 46.2% success over 1,200 episodes, against 28.0% for π0.5. More on what kind of model it is in the learn section.
  • United won't let your agent book. United Airlines is blocking unauthorized AI agents from booking or managing trips on its site, app, and through agencies. Agents can still search fares, but a human has to buy. United cites credential security, accidental purchases, and check-in rules that need a person to confirm, and CEO Scott Kirby said he doesn't want "another middleman."
  • Jane Street changes its interviews, a little. Jane Street says it's trialing campus onsite rounds with less code-writing and more software design, checking code quality, and using LLMs to produce code. Most rounds stay traditional and LLM-free. On HN, its open-sourced Socratic tutor agent skill drew 583 points: it quizzes you before teaching and tracks each subskill on a 0 to 5 scale.
  • LLMs on a TV box. smolmaxxing runs models from 260K to 16B parameters on a Xiaomi TV box with about 1 GB of usable RAM, using custom kernels and weight streaming. Qwen3.5-0.8B hits 16.8 tokens per second, 2.2 times the stock speed.
  • A seed box for open models. LibreAGI is a private BitTorrent tracker for keeping copies of open-weight models that might vanish from hosting sites. It pays seeding credit by scarcity: a model with only one copy earns four times the base rate.

Something to learn

What does a "thinking level" actually change? Hydrogen comes with four of them, and Google quotes its score at "high" (51) and "xhigh" (54). Reasoning models write a hidden scratchpad of tokens before the answer, working the problem step by step. The thinking level is a budget for that scratchpad. Low means a short think and a fast answer. Xhigh means the model may write many thousands of reasoning tokens first.

Two things follow. First, you pay for those tokens. Reasoning tokens are billed as output, so on Hydrogen they cost the $7.50-per-million output rate even though you never see them, and they add wait time. Second, more thinking helps less and less. Going from high to xhigh bought Hydrogen three index points, and on easy tasks it can hurt: one HN commenter said medium or high usually beat xhigh in their own tests, because the model overthinks simple questions. The practical rule is to leave the default on, and raise it only for the hard problem in front of you, like a gnarly bug or a long proof, not for every call.

And what's a "world action model"? Most robot brains today are VLAs, vision-language-action models. You take a model that already understands images and text, and train it to output motor commands from camera frames. π0.5, which scored 28.0% on RoboLab-120, is one. Runway's Praxis-1 starts from a different place: a video model. It's trained to predict what the next moments of video will look like, and it predicts the robot's actions in the same pass. The bet is that a model that has watched huge amounts of video already has a feel for physics, like how objects fall, slide, and get grabbed, and that's what a robot needs more than vocabulary. On that leaderboard, the video-first approach won, at 46.2%.
