Meta's frontier bet

Monday morning Meta released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, calling them its most capable models to date and its first real frontier pair aimed at personal and professional agents. The research blog pitches two features Meta has been building toward since Muse Spark: Active Memory (carry what matters across days, weeks, and months, and pull it back when a task needs it) and Active Focus (keep working a hard goal, try other approaches, ask before important actions). Domain claims cover law, finance, and health; coding is in the same breath. Both models were trained at Meta's Prometheus data center in Ohio.

Availability is split on purpose. Muse Wonder is on the Meta Model API and Muse Code today. Muse Wonder Full is Muse Code subscribers only, no API. Wonder will soon power more of the Muse agent, starting with paid users. Per TechCrunch, API pricing is $4.00 per million input tokens and $20.00 per million output, in the same band as GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5. Meta's own card puts Wonder and Wonder Full next to Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra on its chosen benches, and says Wonder leads Fable, Opus, and Astra on MemoryBench.

Outside numbers arrived the same afternoon. Artificial Analysis scored Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full at 52 and 56 on Intelligence Index v4.3 at max reasoning effort, up from Muse Spark 1.3's 48 six weeks earlier. Wonder Full landed top three on six of ten sub-evals. Knowledge is still a soft spot (AA-Omniscience 25 to 30 versus Opus 5.5 at 46 and Astra at 43), with low hallucination rates as the offset. Alexandr Wang's launch thread on X led with Active Memory, Active Focus, and domain expertise; Mark Zuckerberg's company account posted the product line. Early hands-on takes on X are loud and mixed. Treat those as chatter until more independent runs land. The HN thread is already past 200 points.

Karpathy's funnel

Sunday night Andrej Karpathy dusted off an April post and said the gap in shared understanding of what LLMs can do is no longer two camps talking past each other. It is "a sharp funnel." His napkin math: about 6 billion people have barely touched LLMs; 1 to 2 billion use free-tier chatbots as a better search or writing aid; about 20 million use frontier models professionally in math and code and watch weeks of work collapse into a prompt (apps built, copied, translated, even decompiled from binaries); and roughly 5,000 people have internal access to systems that look like swarms of agents on multi-week software projects, cyber offense and defense at machine speed, and math that still needs human archaeology. He names three drivers: impact scales with ambition and horizon; capability is jagged, peaking where work is digital, verifiable, and valuable; and access tiers (free, paid, internal) keep the groups from seeing the same thing.

By Monday afternoon the thread had about 37,000 likes, 4.9 million views, and 629 points on Hacker News with 900-plus comments. Read next to Muse Wonder and it is a useful frame. Meta is trying to move more people from the free-chatbot tier into the agent tier. Wonder Full staying inside Muse Code is one more reminder that the tip of the funnel is still gated.

More code, same software

A Harvard study summarized by Ars Technica (Fiona Chen and James Stratton, using Jellyfish data across 700-plus firms and about 300 million work events through March 2026) puts numbers on a complaint every review queue already knows. After AI coding agents arrive, firms see about 30% more lines of code, 20% more commits, and 23% more pull requests. Resolved Jira issues and epics do not move in a statistically clear way. The slack goes into review: average time from PR open to merge rises about 49%, PRs that need changes nearly double, and comments per PR rise about 35%. About 14% more workers end up doing review. AI review tools are common (80% of measured firms) but still leave most of the work to humans: agents wrote about 23% of review comments and touched about 11% of PRs. The authors find no clear employment drop from the tools. The clean line from the paper: coding-time gains get absorbed by downstream constraints. That is today's second lesson below.

Legal agents, two bets

Z.ai opened gated access to GLM-Legal on Monday, a legal-domain agent model built on GLM-5.3-Flash for contract review, evidence analysis, and compliance work. The headline number is a 19.2% all-pass rate on Harvey LAB across 24 practice areas, up 12.5 points from the base model, with claimed parity to closed frontier systems on LegalBench and PLawBench on a leaner footprint. Access is by consultation; AutoClaw integration is coming. The same day Meta's own card leaned on Harvey LAB for Wonder's legal claims. Two labs, same bench, different product shapes: a Chinese open-weight specialist versus a closed US consumer-agent stack.

Slow-moving tsunami

At the IMF-World Bank meetings in Bangkok, Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva called AI a "slow-moving tsunami" for labor markets. The IMF's estimate: about 40% of jobs worldwide will eventually be augmented, downgraded, or eliminated, with exposure around 60% in advanced economies and 23% in low-income countries ("just because AI is further away," not because they are safer). She put young graduates first in line as routine entry jobs shrink. Singapore's President Tharman Shanmugaratnam, co-chair of the World Bank Jobs Council, said crisis-era hiring supports may need to become permanent tools, and that in the US "for the first time in a century, demand for graduates has gone down." World Bank President Ajay Banga pushed "small AI" that does not depend on giant language models for farmers and clinics, and warned that passive policy will widen divergence. Georgieva also said AI investment is helping keep global growth near 3% despite an energy shock. Put next to Karpathy's funnel: the people who feel the tsunami first are often still stuck in the wide end of the capability picture.

Something to learn

Active Memory is not the same thing as a bigger context window. A context window is how much text the model can see in one request, often measured in tokens (Wonder's public card puts it at about 1 million). Fill that window with chat history and tool output and the model can use it for that turn. When the window fills, something has to go: summarization, dropping old turns, or a fresh session that starts blank. Active Memory, as Meta describes it for Wonder, is a separate system that decides what is worth keeping across days and weeks, stores it outside any single prompt, and retrieves the relevant pieces when a new task shows up. That is closer to a filing cabinet plus a librarian than to a longer notepad. Why it matters for agents: a personal agent that books travel, tracks a lease dispute, or remembers your health constraints cannot rely on you repasting the whole history every time. The failure modes split too. A long context fails by overflowing or by burying the fact you care about in the middle. A memory system fails by storing the wrong thing, retrieving the wrong thing, or leaking private details into the wrong session. When a lab says "best memory," ask which they mean: raw context size, or a store that survives past the prompt.

Why can a team generate 30% more code with agents and still not close more tickets? Software shipping is a queue, and the slowest stage sets the pace. Agents speed up the writing stage. Review is still mostly human: someone has to read the diff, ask for changes, and decide the change is safe to merge. In the Harvard / Jellyfish data, that stage got about half again as long, and change requests nearly doubled, so the extra PRs stacked up in the same gate. More arrivals into a full queue do not raise throughput; they raise wait time. AI review bots help a little (they left most comments and most PRs to people in that sample), but they do not remove the need for a human who owns the merge. So when a vendor promises "10x engineering," separate three numbers: code produced, PRs opened, and features actually shipped. The first two can jump while the third sits still. Fixing that usually means changing what you send to review (smaller diffs, stronger tests, clearer acceptance checks), not just buying a faster writer.
