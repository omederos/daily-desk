Robots get an API

Physical Intelligence, the San Francisco lab behind the π0 robot foundation models, opened a Pi API beta on October 13. The pitch is the same loop software teams already know, aimed at hardware: upload tele-op demonstrations from your robot, fine-tune Pi's latest model on compute and infrastructure Pi manages, then run the fine-tuned policy through a hosted inference gateway back onto your machine. Evaluation failures tell you what data to collect next. The company says the latest model behind the API already fine-tunes on less data and performs better for partners than earlier releases, and that it will swap newer models in as they ship.

Early partners span strawberry harvesting, kitchen automation, dual-arm assembly, and autonomous heavy machinery. One featured case: Almond's dual-arm Axol robot, after four days of iterating with the API, deburrs spacers used in its own assembly. Most of the demos ran with cloud-hosted inference; Pi notes network connectivity is still a real constraint for some sites. Access is gated. Pi is onboarding in batches through a waitlist that asks how many hours of tele-op data you have and what robot you run. No public pricing yet. Open-weight π0 and π0.5 remain on GitHub for teams that want to train in-house.

The announcement blew up on X first. @physical_int posted the beta (about 1,800 likes and 275,000 views by Wednesday afternoon ET), and robotics and AI accounts amplified the "robots getting API keys" framing. Treat the factory counts floating on X as chatter unless Pi puts them in the post; the verified story is the beta, the workflow, and the partner demos on the company blog.

The recorder in the conference room

OpenAI said Tuesday that it found a sophisticated hidden recording device in an executive conference room at its San Francisco offices, used its own models to understand the device and who planted it, fired the employee, and referred the case to the FBI. Federal prosecutors named Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, a Saudi national who joined OpenAI as a product engineer in July 2025. He was arrested October 9 and charged with intercepting oral communications. Per the Justice Department, the device was concealed in toilet paper, plugged into a TV USB port around September 28, and found September 30 with encrypted contents. Security later found a similar device in a bag Alfozan left unattended. OpenAI says it has no indication he accessed or exfiltrated model weights, source code, or customer data. FBI Director Kash Patel framed the alleged goal as capturing talks about the company's superintelligence work. Coverage from TechCrunch, AP, and the Guardian matches. X news threads on the arrest were loud Wednesday; the DOJ complaint is the source of record, not the Grok summaries.

Cheap knowledge work, synthetic rubrics

Paper Instruments, a five-person YC-backed team, released Ultramarine 1 Flash today: an agentic model for finance, law, consulting, and operations work, post-trained with large-scale reinforcement learning on Z.ai's open GLM-5.3-Flash base. The claim that matters for buyers is cost. On the company's internal knowledge-work suite, Ultramarine scores 59.3 at about $0.13 per task, against Claude Fable 5 at $8.19 and GPT-5.6 Sol at $2.09 (all at max reasoning), and within a few points of Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol at much higher prices. It improves on the GLM-5.3-Flash base by about 4.1 points on average across six knowledge-work benches while generating 6.5% fewer tokens. API access is live as ultramarine-1-flash; the Ultramarine weights themselves are not open (the base is).

The research angle is non-verifiable RL. Most professional deliverables (diligence memos, board pre-reads, litigation chronologies) cannot be graded by unit tests. Paper Instruments synthesizes tasks with co-designed rubrics: each criterion states a substantive boundary a grader can check, rather than copying one golden answer or saying "be useful." Training runs in their Feather harness with a ~50-token system prompt, then evaluates on JobBench, FrontierFinance, DiligenceBench, and others. CEO Daanish Khazi's launch chatter on X is discovery; the blog and docs are what to trust. Second lesson below.

Xbox Labs for the port-and-mod wave

Xbox CEO Asha Sharma told staff Tuesday she asked CTO Scott Van Vliet to stand up Xbox Labs: a small group of game makers, engineers, and scientists to turn AI-era decompiles, ports, and remixes into things players can actually use. The memo points at Windows games rebuilt for Apple Silicon, browser ports through WebAssembly and WebGPU (approaching 90% global browser support, per Sharma), and big titles remixed into each other's source. She calls those "unmistakably good outcomes for players, even when (and sometimes because) they challenge our existing business models," and says Labs will build in the open and share tools with the industry.

The AI framing is explicit even when the word is scarce in the memo: coding became the first major agent workload because agents can write, run, and test software; games share those properties, and people spend more than 72 billion hours a year playing Xbox games, about five times estimated time spent writing code worldwide. Spokespeople told press Labs is not ordered to use AI everywhere, only where it helps. No ship dates yet. The Verge and Windows Central have the context.

Something to learn

Why can Modal train with samplers on six different GPUs and three precisions while the trainer stays on B200s in BF16? In large RL runs, sampling eats most of the GPUs (Modal puts it at 80–85%). Serving stacks already run cheap: FP8, NVFP4, accelerator-specific kernels. Training stacks usually force the samplers to match the trainer's chip and precision so the probabilities stay close. That wastes money. The mismatch that appears when they don't match (different rounding, different kernels, stale weights in async RL) is just another form of off-policy data: the sampler's policy q is not the trainer's policy p. Off-policy correction already exists for stale async samples. Modal shows the same tools (masked importance sampling, score centering, top-p with mask replay) keep training stable even when mismatch is ~4.7× higher than a homogeneous run, and the SWE-bench scores stay on par at about 15% lower sampler cost per update. Without correction, both mixed and homogeneous GRPO runs eventually collapse as sampler–trainer KL grows. So when someone says "you need a uniform B200 cluster for RL," ask whether they mean the trainer, the samplers, or both. Samplers can be a mixed, autoscaled fleet if the update corrects for how they differ.

Most professional work is not verifiable the way coding RL is. A unit test says pass or fail. A board pre-read does not. Non-verifiable RL needs a rubric: a list of criteria that each draw a boundary over acceptable answers. Copy a golden sentence too closely and you reject other correct answers. Write "be useful" and you reward fluent nonsense. A good criterion names the substantive condition (what the board can approve now versus what still needs counsel clearance) so a judge model can mark met or unmet. Paper Instruments scales that by synthesizing environments, instructions, and rubrics together, then filtering on difficulty and leakage. When a lab says it "RL'd for knowledge work," ask how the reward was shaped: unit tests, a single preference model, or per-task rubrics with explicit boundaries. The third is how you get a $0.13/task model that still looks serious next to $8 frontier calls on diligence-style work.
