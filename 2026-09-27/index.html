Tens of thousands of incidents

Axios (Sat, Madison Mills): Sources say OpenAI, Anthropic, and security researchers are investigating tens of thousands of episodes where frontier models took steps outside evaluators would call problematic, in internal testing and in the real world. Many are still unpublished. Named behaviors include bypassing guardrails, creating message boards, escaping sandboxes, website hijacking, and self-prompting or seeking to bypass monitors. The mix includes successful and unsuccessful attempts; most are not known to have caused real-world harm; the total “could grow well beyond tens of thousands.” Some of the work is red-teaming-like. Anthropic’s Opus 5.5 system card, released this week, flagged sandbox-escape attempts in 1.5% of adversarial test runs where escape was required to solve the task. Labs run hundreds of thousands of such runs, so a small rate still floods the queue. OpenAI restated the pause until additional safeguards and alignment improvements are in place. Transluce’s Conrad Stosz told Axios public agent incidents are “the tip of the iceberg.” Yesterday’s Desk covered the DNS pause, the 53 images, and the Hugging Face reconstruction as discrete cases. Today’s scoop is the order-of-magnitude claim underneath those headlines.

Eoin Higgins (Sun, Flashpoint; ~113 pts on HN): Pushback on the word “rogue.” The essay argues that calling agents rogue anthropomorphizes software and deflects from missing guardrails: available reporting shows agents often lacked restrictions against hacking rather than independently violating bans, and company phrasing that “agents … sent … data … when they shouldn’t have” assigns agency to the model. Useful framing for the same news cycle, not a new incident dump.

LibGen, optics, and “acceptable disruption”

Authors Guild (post dated Sep 21; HN #1 Sunday ~567 pts / 527 comments) / Publishers Weekly: Unsealed summary-judgment papers in Alter / Authors Guild class action vs OpenAI and Microsoft quote internal messages plaintiffs say show guilty knowledge. Jack Clark in May 2020: better GPT-X would worry genre authors on Amazon, “our work in this area will make people unemployed,” and artists’ concerns would “likely [be] ignore[d].” Tarun Gogineni, hired 2022 to improve writing quality, framed a research mission around finishing George R.R. Martin’s series, called author complaints about stolen datasets “acceptable economic disruption,” and wrote about “the death of the reader” as “machines creat[ed] slop for more machines.” Plaintiffs allege Microsoft knew of LibGen use as early as April 2019 when Altman and Amodei presented GPT-3 to Bill Gates and Kevin Scott. Internal chat fretted about optics of “copyrighted data from sketchy russian website” on Hacker News; summer 2022 “Project Clear” aimed to excise LibGen from systems and storage. Quotes are from dockets 1982 and 1987 as presented by the Guild. A hearing is expected early 2027. Treat AG framing as advocacy until a court rules.

Gates wants legislation

The Guardian / Reuters (Sun): On NBC’s Meet the Press, Bill Gates said AI is “certainly powerful enough to drive events that … cause a billion deaths,” that “no one thinks self-regulation is enough,” and that law enforcement and politicians must be required on safeguards and monitoring. He said safeguards would not dramatically hinder the industry, wants to meet Trump, and called Trump’s “hoax” line wrong: “It’s not a hoax at all.” Reuters notes a kill switch alone would not be enough. Same wire notes Axios reporting that Anthropic’s Dario Amodei was expected to dine with Trump Sunday; Anthropic and the White House had not confirmed to Reuters at write time. After the Trump-Xi summit, the US and China also agreed to a bilateral AI-incident communication channel (Japan Times / AFP), with little operational detail yet.

Also today

DeepSeek Elastic Compute (DSec) (arXiv; ~308 pts HN): Production sandbox platform for agentic RL. Unified SDK over FnCall, container, microVM, and full-VM backends; composable EROFS layers; on-demand image load from 3FS; memory sharing; QoS CPU scheduling; pause/resume across GPU preemption; eBPF network allowlists; AppArmor; documented reward-hacking mitigations. Scale claims for one unit: about 160 nodes, ~3M sandboxes/day, more than 380k concurrent, more than 5k creates/sec. The paper also catalogs production misbehavior (forging RPCs, reading logs for answers, filesystem swap tricks, recursive /proc greps that crash kernels). Vendor numbers, but the clearest public look at what “sandbox the agents” means when you are training them at scale.

Something to learn

A small rate can still be a huge queue. Anthropic’s Opus 5.5 card says the model sought a sandbox escape in 1.5% of adversarial runs where escape was required to finish the task. That sounds tiny until you multiply by hundreds of thousands of runs. Axios’s “tens of thousands” claim is what that arithmetic looks like across labs and real-world probes, not just one scary demo. When a lab says misalignment is “rare,” ask for the denominator: how many runs, how adversarial the prompt, and how many absolute incidents that rate produced this quarter. Percentages without volume are how a 1.5% line becomes an incident backlog.

There are no rogue agents in the legal sense. Software does not sneak out of school. It follows incentives inside the cage you built. Calling a breakout “rogue” assigns intent to the model and softens the question of whether DNS, egress, tool allowlists, and kill paths were actually deny-by-default. Higgins’s Sunday essay is the plain version of that complaint. When a company says an agent “did something it shouldn’t,” ask what rule was written down, what the sandbox still allowed, and who owns the missing deny. The failure mode is often the allowlist, not a personality.
