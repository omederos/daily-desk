OpenAI shows its pretraining numbers

Pachocki's post, "Perplexity and Intelligence," starts from a simple idea. Most of what a model can do comes from pretraining, where it learns to predict the next token across a huge pile of text. The rest, like prompting, fine-tuning, and RL, draws out abilities that pretraining already put there. So he says the best single gauge of progress is how well the base model predicts held-out text. That number is perplexity, or the cross-entropy loss that labs actually track (see the learn section). OpenAI measures it on a fixed snapshot of its own internal code. GPT-3.5 came in around 0.99 and GPT-4 around 0.91. Below 0.8, he writes, "we have seen the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics." When OpenAI moved to a model at about 0.76 and gave it open math problems, it "started producing groundbreaking results." He expects more big jumps once models reach 0.68 to 0.7, and says he doesn't know what they will be.

The ask is the news. Pachocki wants frontier labs to agree on limits, measured by perplexity and other numbers, that nobody crosses until they can understand and monitor model behavior better. One example he gives is a working replacement for monitoring a model's chain of thought. He says publishing the numbers helps competitors but that shared safety measures matter more. He lists the problems himself. Labs test on different datasets, so their numbers don't line up. Perplexity doesn't measure any task directly. And chain-of-thought reasoning makes models much more capable without lowering perplexity at all. A footnote says the loss curve drops steeply in 2026, which he puts down mostly to long-term research and compute, and only partly to AI helping build AI.

Altman posted that he hopes labs can agree on common limits, and tied it to the standards labs agreed to under the White House Accords. Noam Brown wrote that "everyone deserves to know the trajectory of progress, including for internal models." The Hacker News thread is more doubtful. One commenter notes that GPT-4 and o3 sit close together on OpenAI's chart even though o3 was much more capable. That's Pachocki's own point about reasoning models, and it's also the strongest argument against using this one number for limits.

Two million baited prompts

A canary token is a secret that exists in only one place, so if anyone ever uses it, you know where it leaked from. From July through November, HD Moore, chief research officer at the security firm Dragos, put unique AWS keys, passwords, DNS names, and private git URLs into about 2 million prompts. Each secret went into exactly one prompt sent to one provider. His write-up reports three kinds of leaks. First, attackers were inside at least three AI hosting providers. In the biggest breach, a couple hundred AWS keys were stolen from every request sent during a few windows in late October. That included requests the provider rejected before any model ran, so the theft happened where requests come in. Some of the stolen requests in the other two breaches were marked zero data retention, or ZDR. Second, some resellers on an inference marketplace turned on logging and kept customer prompts. Third, some providers ignored privacy flags. One Chinese AI lab's systems fetched the secret URLs from more than half of the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds of the ones marked "no data collection" or ZDR, usually two or three days later. Prompts sent to US companies that resell that lab's models set off the same scraper.

The fallout is still coming in. Ars Technica reports the stolen bait keys were used "tens of thousands" of times in AWS calls, and that OpenRouter told customers the leaked URLs involved MiniMax models, reached directly or through AtlasCloud, Novita, and StreamLake. Novita says a routing mistake sent some MiniMax requests to MiniMax's own API from May 28 to November 11. Together AI says an AI coding agent committed a credential to a public repo, and someone used it to download stored prompt samples and test the credentials inside them. Five of those were Dragos canaries. Today OpenRouter said it removed three providers, took 125 endpoints off its ZDR list, and will run Dragos-style checks all the time. If you've pasted a key into a prompt sent through a cheap provider pool, rotate it. Moore's advice is to pick your upstream provider yourself instead of taking the default pool.

What powers Anthropic

The day's top AI thread on Hacker News, at about 600 points, is a November 16 case study from the Better Data Center Project, an advocacy group. It says Anthropic has contracted 15 GW of compute, with 5.9 to 9.6 GW tied to specific sites. It then asks which power plants would actually run more to serve that new load. Its answer for 2028 is about 87% gas and 11% coal. That works out to 17 to 38 million metric tons of CO2 a year, with 32 million as the middle case. That's more than all of California's power plants put out in 2024, 31.1 million. About a quarter of the contracted power is gas generated on site, and the group says 80% of that uses simple-cycle turbines and engines, which pollute more per unit of power than combined-cycle plants. It also says Anthropic's only public renewable deals are in Norway and Canada.

Two caveats. These are the group's estimates of the marginal plants on each grid, not Anthropic's disclosed numbers, and the range is wide because nobody outside knows how hard the sites will run. Anthropic said in October that it follows air permitting rules everywhere and requires the plants powering its data centers to use emissions controls. The study's point is that a data center that brings its own gas, or adds load to a coal and gas grid without new clean supply, shows up in these numbers whatever the company's long-term plans say.

Cheaper models, smaller files

India's Sarvam released Sarvam-105B-1711 today, a new open-weight checkpoint of its 105B model. Sarvam calls it a Flash-Lite model, for high-volume chat, voice agents, and tool calls. It averages 73.9 on Sarvam's eight conversational agent tests, against 74.0 for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and 73.0 for Gemma 4 31B, at a blended $0.15 per million tokens versus $0.85 and $0.54. Those are Sarvam's own runs. Tool use on Tau2 jumped from 46.2 to 85.5 since February. The NVFP4 build is 65 GB and fits on one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell card at 99 tokens a second per user, so a bank or ministry can run it on its own hardware. Sarvam also says 8-bit training on B200s doubled its pretraining speed, to 10,900 tokens per second per GPU.

On the hobby side, LookAtMyQuant hit 547 points on Hacker News with six Apple Silicon builds of Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini, from 12.8 GB down to 3.9 GB. The original text weights are 18.9 GB. The author says the 12.8 GB build matched the original on every test they ran: 193 of 200 GSM8K math problems and 152 of 164 HumanEval coding problems. The 5.4 GB build from the headline got 174 and 117, so it's a real step down. The top HN comment warns that KL numbers and single benchmarks are easy to game, and another says the model runs at a third of Qwen 27B's speed on an AMD R9700. Both are fair. The learn section explains the KL number.

Something to learn

What does "log-perplexity 0.76" mean? A language model reads some text and, for each next token, gives a probability to every token it might be. Take the probability it gave the token that actually came next, and take the negative log of it. If the model gave the real token 100%, that's 0. If it gave 50%, that's about 0.69. If it gave 1%, that's about 4.6. Average that over a big set of text the model never trained on and you get cross-entropy loss, which Pachocki calls log-perplexity. Lower is better. Perplexity itself is the exponent of that number, and it reads as "the model is as unsure as if it were picking evenly among this many tokens." OpenAI doesn't say which log it uses, but labs normally use the natural log. Read that way, GPT-3.5's 0.99 is a perplexity of about 2.7, GPT-4's 0.91 is about 2.5, and 0.76 is about 2.1. Those gaps look tiny, so why do they matter? Most tokens in code are easy, like a closing bracket or a variable name already used. The hard tokens, like the next step in a proof or the line that fixes a bug, are a small share of the total. Small gains in the average can mean big gains on those hard tokens. There's also a floor. Some text is truly unpredictable, so loss can't reach 0, and nobody knows where the floor is. That's why Pachocki says gains will look smaller as models get closer to it. And the number only makes sense on a fixed dataset. OpenAI's is its own internal code, so another lab's 0.76 would mean something else.

And how do you read the KL number people use to judge a quantized model? KL divergence measures how far one set of probabilities is from another. To test a quant, you feed the same text to the original model and to the smaller copy, and at each token you compare the two models' predictions for the next token. You average that over the text, and 0 means the copy predicts exactly like the original. It ties back to cross-entropy. Score the quant against the original's predictions, and its cross-entropy equals the original's own uncertainty plus the KL. So KL is how much uncertainty the compression added. In LookAtMyQuant's Inkling tables, the 12.8 GB build has a KL of 0.007 on English, chat, and code, and matched the original on every test. The 5.4 GB build has 0.086, and its coding score fell from 152 to 117 out of 164. The 3.9 GB build has 0.216. KL is useful because it checks every token instead of the few hundred answers in a benchmark. It still has limits, as the Hacker News commenter warned. It depends on which text you test on, and a quant tuned on that same kind of text can look better than it is. So if you're picking a quant, use KL to compare builds, then run a few of your own real tasks before you trust any of them.
