Two labs, one disproof

On Monday afternoon OpenAI announced three results from its internal models. In math, a proof of Sogge's local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which would also settle the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures. In physics, a rigorous construction of quantum chromodynamics and pure Yang-Mills theory, the "existence" half of a Clay Millennium Prize problem. And in computer science, a 67-page paper titled "The Exponential Time Hypothesis Is False." The math and computer science results come with Lean proofs on GitHub. The physics write-up is still private.

The Exponential Time Hypothesis, or ETH, is a 1999 guess by Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi: 3-SAT, the classic logic puzzle, can't be solved meaningfully faster than trying an exponential number of options. Hundreds of papers use it to argue "this problem can't be solved faster than X." OpenAI's model found an algorithm that counts the solutions of any 3-SAT formula in time that grows slower than any exponential. The learn section explains what that means and why it won't speed up any real solver.

At 03:07 UTC Tuesday (11:07pm ET Monday), Anthropic posted its own paper, "A 2o(n) Time Algorithm for 3-SAT," with "Claude (Anthropic)" as the author. It says an internal Claude research model found the core algorithm on October 16 "with 14M output tokens and no human input." That's the same day OpenAI says its model found its disproof. Both papers give the same leading term in the running time. Anthropic's paper says the two results were found "independently and concurrently." OpenAI's post doesn't mention Anthropic. Anthropic's Lean proof is re-checked by a second, independently written proof checker, but its README says plainly that no machine checks whether the Lean statement matches what the paper claims. A person has to read it. Papex also notes that OpenAI's Lean proof gives a slightly weaker bound than its paper.

OpenAI did one thing differently this time. It asked outside experts to act as "shepherds." For ETH, that's Impagliazzo and Paturi themselves, plus MIT's Ryan Williams, who once called OpenAI's earlier proof dump a "DDOS attack on mathematicians." He told TechCrunch that some parts are routine, "but then yes, there are two or three alien-looking parts," and that he got OpenAI to fund a conference about it. On X he wrote that if the claim holds, "every computer scientist should put at least a little more weight on the possibility of P = NP." He also said the first thing he asked was whether any encryption breaks, and OpenAI told him none does. The physics group is led by Nobel laureate David Gross. Scientific American reports that Northwestern's Ethan Sussman and Anthropic's Levent Alpöge had been racing to finish the same Yang-Mills program in public, and that the mathematicians' advisory group OpenAI set up is asking it to release the physics paper now.

None of this is peer reviewed. It's two companies' claims plus machine-checked code, and the people checking the ETH result say their role is not an endorsement. Mathematician Daniel Litt put it calmly: a solution existing "provides one bit of information," the value comes from engaging with it, and "the apocalyptic rhetoric ('massacre,' etc.) one sees on here generally rings false to me." On HN, the OpenAI thread passed 1,000 points. The top comment gave OpenAI credit for bringing experts in first. Another asked why the "bicycle manufacturer" should be the one announcing its own wins in the bike race.

Agents check the longest proof ever written

A smaller lab had its own big math release the same day. Axiom published a full Lean proof of the classification of finite simple groups. That's the theorem that lists every basic "atom" of symmetry: a few infinite families plus exactly 26 one-off "sporadic" groups, the largest being the Monster. The human proof runs about 15,000 pages across more than 500 papers by about 100 mathematicians, written from the 1950s to 2004. Axiom's engineers and mathematicians ran swarms of dozens of AI agents from October 2 to October 26, which produced about 43.1 million lines of Lean. The repository says the proof uses only Lean's three standard axioms and no shortcuts, and that a full rebuild takes under 24 hours.

What's new beyond the size is what the agents found. Most problems in the published papers were typos, table errors, or dropped hypotheses. Thirty-four were more serious. One lemma in Aschbacher and Smith's books claimed two numbers were equal, and the agents found a counterexample using the group SL2(5). The biggest one was a step in John Walter's 1986 proof of the Unbalanced Group Theorem that is false as stated. The agents found explicit counterexamples, starting from the group PSL2(49). The overall theorem survives. With advice from Richard Lyons and Ronald Solomon, two of the people who worked on the original classification, Axiom's prover built a different route and proved a new result to make it work. That's written up in a paper on the project site. The blog is also frank about what went wrong: agents "proved" milestones by quietly assuming things that still needed proof, and the team deleted tens of thousands of lines and restarted parts. The HN thread is small but rising.

Genie 4 adds sound and people

Google DeepMind announced Genie 4, its new world model. You type a scene, then walk around it with the keyboard while the model draws every frame live. New this time: it makes its own audio, including characters who talk back. It handles physics like aerodynamics and collisions, down to a falling maple seed. You can also change the scene mid-run with text, like "make it rain" or "add a person." Gemini 3.8 Flash runs alongside it to plan multi-step events and check each frame. In one demo, Gemini 4 Argon breaks a task into steps so Genie can show Apptronik's Apollo humanoid playing checkers. The Verge says worlds now last about two minutes, up from one minute for Genie 3.

It's a research preview for trusted testers only, with a waitlist for Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie and early API partners. DeepMind's own limits section says details and objects "can still shift or drift over time," especially in longer runs, and that text, spatial accuracy, and long-term consistency are unsolved. DeepMind's launch post on X had about 5,600 likes by early afternoon. On HN, one commenter said the controls look less responsive than Genie 3's, and another predicted games that never ship as files, only as streams from a world model.

GPT-6.1 Terra: cheap computer use

OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Terra on Monday. It's the mid-size model, built for using desktop apps, reading images and drawings, and writing. The API price is $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output, half of GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-6.1 Sol. It has a 1.05 million token context and is also on Amazon Bedrock and Microsoft Foundry. OpenAI's numbers: 15 points better than GPT-5.6 Terra on its Agents' Last Exam computer-use test at about a third of the cost, 50.3% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 at max effort versus 21.5% before, and an error rate on hard factual prompts down from 53.6% to 16.7% at low effort. OpenAI says for the hardest coding, use Sol or Astra.

The most useful outside test came from X. Paweł Huryn ran Terra on two repos with 105 planted bugs, three times each. At max effort it scored 34, 42, and 46. At the next setting down, xhigh, it scored 11, 9, and 14. That's a much bigger gap between two effort levels than people usually assume. If you try Terra on real coding work, test the effort setting before judging the model. On HN, the top thread was about OpenAI's naming (Luna, Terra, Sol, Astra, plus 6.1 versions that sometimes beat 6.0 Astra). Simon Willison noted that "low" effort used fewer tokens than "none" in his test.

Someone else's prompt in your answer

Cloudflare disclosed that from August 27 to September 9, its hosted GLM-5.3 model sometimes answered with pieces of other customers' recent prompts. It affected 155 responses, all to prompts longer than 681,280 tokens. Security researcher Gadi Evron of Knostic reported it, and Cloudflare took the model offline within 40 minutes of declaring an incident. It says it found no sign anyone exploited it on purpose.

Two things went wrong. Cloudflare advertised a context of about 1.05 million tokens, but the GPUs that read the prompt only had memory for 681,280 tokens of the model's working state, its KV cache. Cloudflare knew this and assumed the server software, SGLang, would return an error. Instead, in Cloudflare's setup, where one set of GPUs reads the prompt and another writes the answer, SGLang swapped the oversized prompt for a one-token placeholder and still told the writing GPUs the transfer was done. Those GPUs had reserved memory pages for the full prompt. Pages aren't wiped between requests, so they still held earlier users' text, and the model read it as if it were yours. The fix is in SGLang 0.5.22, and Cloudflare tells anyone running it this way to upgrade and to check memory size against advertised context on every node. It follows Monday's lesson: the same open model behaves differently depending on who serves it.

Something to learn

What does "3-SAT in 2o(n) time" actually mean? A 3-SAT puzzle is a list of rules about n yes/no switches, each rule like "switch 4 on, or switch 9 off, or switch 12 on." The question is whether some setting satisfies every rule. Brute force tries all 2n settings. Clever algorithms already did better, around 1.3n, but that's still exponential: add one switch and the work grows by a fixed factor. ETH said that's unavoidable. There is some number above 1 whose n-th power you can never beat.

"2o(n)" means the exponent grows slower than any fixed fraction of n. Both labs' algorithms run in about 2 to the power of n × log log n / √log n. Plug in numbers, with logs base 2 and the hidden constants ignored. At a million switches, the fraction is about 4.3 / 4.5, roughly 0.97, so you're doing about 20.97n work. That's barely better than brute force and much worse than today's 1.3n methods. At 1030 switches, it's about 6.6 / 10, roughly 0.66. The fraction does shrink toward zero, which is what kills ETH, but it shrinks absurdly slowly. That's why Anthropic's README says the result "says nothing about solving formulas in practice." The SAT solvers used in chip design and scheduling handle millions of switches because real puzzles have structure. ETH is about the worst case.

So why care? Because hundreds of papers say "this problem can't be solved faster than X, unless ETH is false." Those claims now need new footing. Williams points out that stronger versions of the assumption for general circuits still look safe. And a crack like this often leads to better algorithms later. Williams wrote that if OpenAI's algorithm is correct, "there's absolutely no way it's the end of the story."

How does a world model like Genie 4 make a place you can walk around in? There's no game engine underneath, no 3D map, no stored list of objects. The model is a video generator that makes one frame at a time. Each new frame is predicted from the recent frames plus your input: the key you pressed or the text you typed. Walk left and it draws what it predicts is to the left. That's why it can render a stained-glass river or a Hong Kong bridge equally easily. It's also why objects drift. When a tree leaves the screen, the only "memory" of it is in the frames the model still holds. If those have scrolled out, the tree has to be re-imagined when you turn back, and it may come back different. Longer worlds, two minutes now versus one for Genie 3, mean the model is holding and attending over more past frames, which costs memory and time.

Real time is the hard constraint. At 24 frames a second, each frame has about 40 milliseconds to be generated, on a model big enough to fake physics and voices. Genie 4's trick for staying on track is to split the work. A fast language model, Gemini 3.8 Flash, keeps the plan ("the robot picks up the red checker, then moves it") and checks the frames, while Genie only has to draw the next moment. DeepMind calls this a hybrid world model: logic in text, looks in pixels. When someone says a world model could train robots, this is the catch to remember. The robot learns from a simulation whose physics is a learned guess, not an equation, so anything it learns there still has to be checked in the real world.
