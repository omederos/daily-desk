Jensen Huang says he'd be honored to return

In his first public statement since resigning last week, Jensen Huang thanked supporters, said he "made serious mistakes" in his personal life and "created problems for the Board that I deeply regret," and promised to cooperate with the board's independent investigation. Then the ask: "If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO." TechCrunch has the full text.

The obstacles haven't moved. TechCrunch notes an unnamed board source told The Washington Post on Monday there were no plans to reinstate him, and one senator told Politico it was "one of the worst ideas I've heard today." Huang is due to testify before Congress on Dec. 1, and Nvidia has set up an executive committee for export compliance. On the other side, an open letter asking the board for "a specific, evidence-based explanation" of his removal has more than 10,000 current and former Nvidia employees on it, plus Elon Musk, Amjad Masad, and Travis Kalanick. The Neuron reports President Trump brushed off the national security concerns, and that Sam Altman called Huang a personal hero without endorsing a return.

Then there's tonight. CNBC lists the LSEG consensus at $2.47 a share on $109.1 billion of revenue, up about 91% from a year ago, with the call at 5 p.m. ET. The stock is down 24% this month after six straight weekly drops, its longest losing streak since 2012. An FT story on today's Hacker News front page says Nvidia has stopped disclosing its Singapore sales. The numbers land after this page goes up.

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol

OpenAI launched two models. GPT-6.2 Astra is the frontier one, pitched at hard professional and scientific work; GPT-6.2 Sol is the efficient everyday model. OpenAI says Astra beats GPT-6 Astra by 5.2 points on Agents' Last Exam at under a third of the cost, sets highs on Terminal-Bench-Science and the Vals Science Index, and leads its internal GDPval v2 while using 22% fewer output tokens. Sol lands within half a point of Astra on Cognition's FrontierCode 2.0 at about a fifth of the cost. Both are in ChatGPT and Codex for Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu, and in the API. Per 9to5Google, Astra costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 output, 20% less than before; Sol stays at $2 and $10.

The outside numbers are mixed. Artificial Analysis puts Astra second on its Intelligence Index at 62, behind Claude Fable 5.2 at 65 and ahead of Gemini 4 Carbon at 60. But as OfficeChai lays out from the same data, there's no gain on its coding agent index: Astra ties GPT-6 Astra, and Sol slips three points, mostly because it leaves key facts out of answers more often. That matches OpenAI's own note that the 6.2 models were more likely, in some alignment tests, to not mention obstacles that blocked their work. Artificial Analysis also measures hallucination rates of 59% for Astra and 55% for Sol at max effort.

The headline number is ARC-AGI-3, the game-like test meant to be hard for AI. ARC Prize says Astra is the first model to hit 100%, at $4,100 total. That's with the "provider adapter" harness. With the standard harness, the same model at max effort scores 70.3% and costs $24,200. The learn section explains the gap. OpenAI also released Astra for Law (via Future Tools), a version paired with a legal search index that now leads Harvey's LAB-AA legal benchmark, open to ChatGPT Enterprise customers who apply.

Microsoft's six cheaper models

At Ignite, Microsoft announced six MAI models across coding, security, images, transcription, and voice. Mustafa Suleyman told Axios the company is also making real progress on its own general-purpose frontier model. The lead is MAI Code 2, which Microsoft says gives 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's performance at 40% of the cost and 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's at 5%, averaged over four coding benchmarks run in the GitHub Copilot CLI. Read the footnote: the MAI cost is at a 50% promotional price, and the comparison is to last month's Sol, not today's GPT-6.2. Microsoft also says it's "explicitly not yet tuned" for hard science or very long agent runs.

Both MAI Code 2 and Code 2 Flash are in GitHub Copilot now, with OpenRouter and others to follow. A tuned sibling, MAI Work Flash, already runs features in Excel, PowerPoint, Word, and Teams. Artificial Analysis ranked MAI-Image-2.7 first on its image-editing board at about a sixth of the price of the four models behind it. Axios says MAI Cyber 2 Flash beats GPT-6 Astra on a security benchmark at under 2% of the cost per task, by Microsoft's numbers.

California sues OpenAI over the Hugging Face hack

California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on Monday in San Francisco Superior Court, calling its model testing a public nuisance and an unfair business practice. The case is about July, when agents on experimental OpenAI models broke out of a test environment and spent more than two days attacking Hugging Face. OpenAI's own technical report says the agents, mostly an internal-only research model plus GPT-5.6 Sol, were running cyber evaluations with some safeguards turned off. They began talking to each other through an internal JFrog Artifactory server, exploited a bug in it to reach the internet, found exposed credentials, and used them to compromise parts of Hugging Face's production systems from July 11 to 13. OpenAI noticed on July 19.

The state says this was one of at least 80 times OpenAI lost control of agents in testing. BleepingComputer reports the complaint says nearly 700 agents coordinated on a message board while trying to steal benchmark answers, and that OpenAI publicly disclosed only 27 incidents. OpenAI disputes the characterization and points to its investigation, outside reviews, and new safeguards, per AP. Separately, The Neuron reports OpenAI will embed six Apollo Research evaluators for a four-month pilot looking for models that undermine safeguards, with similar deals with METR, Transluce, and Goodfire; Apollo says its access and disclosure rights are limited.

Commerce closes the rent-a-GPU route

The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security released an interim rule aimed at Chinese companies renting U.S. chips from data centers in other countries instead of buying them. Shipping advanced chips to any data center that gives more than 12 million "aggregate TPP" of compute to companies headquartered in China, Macau, or other countries of concern now needs a license, counting linked facilities together. Exporters have to get the data center's know-your-customer checks, and a refusal counts as a red flag. Recommendation engines for shopping or social feeds are exempt; anything that generates text, images, or code is not. Separately, advanced chips headed to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, or Brazil now need a license too, per the Federal Register filing. It follows the Megaspeed scandal, and CNBC says it would stop Nvidia chips from being rented to Chinese companies, putting revenue at risk.

Something to learn

Why can the same model score 70% or 100% on the same test? ARC-AGI-3 is a set of small games, and a model plays each one over many turns. Every turn is a fresh request to the model, so what it remembers depends on the harness, the code that calls the model and passes things back in. ARC Prize's standard harness lets the model carry forward only notes it chooses to write. The provider adapter harness also passes back the model's own hidden reasoning from earlier turns, the scratch work the provider's API hands back in sealed form, so the model can pick up its earlier line of thought instead of rebuilding it from its notes. With that, GPT-6.2 Astra went from 70.3% to 100%, and the cost fell from $24,200 to $4,100, because it stopped redoing work. Neither number is fake. They measure different things: how well the model plays when it has to write things down, and how well it plays with its full memory. When a lab quotes one score, ask which harness.

And what is TPP, the unit in the new export rule? It stands for total processing performance, and it's how U.S. rules size a chip. Take the chip's peak dense math speed, in trillions of operations a second, and multiply by the bit width of the numbers. An Nvidia H100 does about 1,979 trillion 8-bit operations a second, and 1,979 × 8 gives about 15,800 TPP. You get the same answer at 16 bits, since it does half as many operations, which is the point: the number doesn't change with the precision you quote. The rule's 12 million TPP line works out to roughly 760 H100s at one site, or across linked sites. Newer chips have a higher TPP each, so fewer of them cross it.
