Nvidia's board calls Arm's Rene Haas

A day after Jensen Huang resigned, the search for his replacement has a first name. Nvidia's board has approached Arm CEO Rene Haas, the Financial Times reported, citing people familiar with the matter. Per Reuters, Haas is one of several candidates, has not been offered the job, and may not take it. He spent seven years at Nvidia before joining Arm, and has run Arm since 2022. Arm declined to comment.

The market reacted to both halves of that. Investing.com says Arm shares fell 6.5%, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell about 5%, with AMD and Broadcom among the decliners. Nvidia's own Grace and Vera CPUs are built on Arm designs, so a move would leave Nvidia's new CEO coming from one of its key suppliers. Semafor's Reed Albergotti, writing on Yahoo Finance, says Nvidia had tried to hire an outside number two before Huang left but couldn't find anyone willing to work in his shadow. One person close to the search told him there are fewer than 10 people in the world truly able to run the company. Other names floated include Apple's chip chief Johny Srouji, and, at President Trump's suggestion, Tim Cook.

The scandal keeps growing. TechCrunch relays a Reuters report that Huang also had a relationship with a third woman, another Nvidia employee, who wasn't named. And Washington now has two inquiries. Republican leaders of the House China, Foreign Affairs, and Intelligence committees demanded closed-door testimony, and Huang agreed after resigning. Separately, Sen. Ron Wyden and Rep. Richard Neal, the top Democrats on the tax committees, wrote to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asking him to preserve records on Nvidia's China export licenses, and why Megaspeed, the company where the alleged spy worked, kept buying chips after the warnings. As the minority party they can ask but can't compel. Nvidia reports quarterly results on Tuesday, November 17, the first test of interim CEO Colette Kress.

Claude reads Gödel's lost notebooks

Kurt Gödel, the logician behind the incompleteness theorems, left 16 notebooks from the early 1940s: 1,009 pages, much of it in Gabelsberger, a German shorthand that was obsolete even then. Jan von Plato at the University of Helsinki has led a project to decode them since 2017, and progress ran at about a page a week. In September he gave Claude his transcriptions plus six of his own books on Gödel, The Guardian reports. "In a couple of days, I got to see a 650-page explanation of the workbooks. It had to be controlled page for page," he said.

His first question was whether Gödel had really proved, in the 1940s, the independence of the axiom of choice, a result Paul Cohen won the Fields Medal for in 1966 and Gödel later claimed he'd had first. Claude's answer was "almost": one definition was wrong, but Gödel had the right one two years earlier. Von Plato says the reconstructed proof passed Coq, a program that mechanically checks proofs. He then had Claude pull out every theorem Gödel proved, sketched, or had an idea for. It found 663. In Coq, about 400 checked out as stated, about 200 needed repair, like a missing assumption, and about 50 were false. Gödel himself sometimes wrote "falsch!" in the margins.

Notre Dame logician Joel David Hamkins, who hasn't seen the reconstruction, told the Guardian that if it holds up it's remarkable, but "how much is Claude and how much is Gödel is a job for the historians." The useful part for everyone else is the method: a human expert transcribes, the model reads everything at once, and a proof checker catches what the model gets wrong. It was on the Hacker News front page this afternoon.

SpaceX plans $74 billion of AI compute

SpaceX announced two supercomputing campuses worth more than $74 billion together: over $24 billion at Starbase in South Texas and about $50 billion in Cochise County, Arizona. The Texas site will make its own electricity with a gas-fired plant instead of drawing from the grid. The Arizona one would use dry cooling and no industrial groundwater. Reuters notes SpaceX's AI business made up more than half its third-quarter revenue.

The Arizona campus is huge even by this year's standards. The Arizona Daily Star says it would draw more than 6 gigawatts when finished, about 2.5 times all the data centers in Maricopa County combined, per an Arizona Republic analysis. The largest operating U.S. data centers today run under 1.5 gigawatts. It still needs a county permit, a petition with 2,400 signatures opposes it, and the hearing is December 9. The Texas Tribune reported the Texas land was de-annexed from Brownsville, which has a data center moratorium, and annexed by Starbase.

The other side of the power rush: SoftBank took a $3.7 billion charge on gas turbines and other power equipment it had reserved for its U.S. data centers and no longer wants, Bisnow reports, about $2.85 billion of it in nonrefundable deposits.

Amodei and Dimon, five days before the IPO

Dario Amodei sat down with JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon in a livestream from JPMorgan's Manhattan headquarters, one of the last stops of the roadshow. Yahoo Finance has the quotes. Asked why go public now, Amodei said Anthropic has to commit to compute long before it knows the demand, and called that its "deepest business dilemma." "We actually tend to buy on the low side," he said, and more cash means more buffer. He said Anthropic wants to "underpromise and overdeliver," not squeeze out the highest valuation. Pricing is still set for Wednesday, November 18, and trading for Thursday. Yahoo notes investors had talked about $2 trillion a few weeks ago, and the range now implies about $1.4 trillion.

Two smaller Anthropic items. Claude had a two-hour outage this morning: login failures on claude.ai and Claude Code from 4:58am to 6:58am ET, per the status page, a 457-point thread on HN. And Anthropic opened a private beta of self-hosted Claude Managed Agents, which runs the agent server, database, sandboxes, credentials, and session history inside a company's own cloud. Model calls can go to Anthropic, Bedrock, Google, Microsoft Foundry, or the company's own gateway.

A Fireworks breach reaches Notion AI chats

Fireworks AI, a popular host for open-weight models, says an attacker got into its development environment through a third-party cloud provider on October 23 and took billing data, model weights, and datasets for some customers, BleepingComputer reports. TechCrunch notes Fireworks customers include Cursor and Harvey. Notion is the first big confirmed victim. Its advisory says the stolen data included Notion AI chat transcripts used for its Insights feature, from fewer than 1% of workspaces, mostly not labeled with a user or workspace. Notion turned off Insights, stopped serving open models through Fireworks, and tells affected customers to rotate any passwords or keys they pasted into those chats.

The lesson is in the fine print. VentureBeat points out Fireworks says it has "zero data retention" by default, and that's true for live requests. But batch jobs, stored responses, and training data do get stored, and Notion's Insights ran as classification jobs. If your vendor promises zero retention, ask which features it covers.

OpenAI folds its robotics team into the main lab

OpenAI has broken up its standalone robotics team, Fortune reports in an exclusive the company confirmed. Robotics researchers move to the main research group under Mark Chen, and the applied robotics and hardware staff move to the Applied group under Greg Brockman. OpenAI says this speeds robotics up rather than retreating: its researchers found GPT-6 Astra could run some robot tasks directly, so a separate robot model looks less necessary. OpenAI still plans a humanoid robot, with a first public demo targeted for 2027. Fortune visited OpenAI's booth at the CoRL robotics conference in Austin this week and found it empty. It's the second time OpenAI has shut a robotics team, after 2020. The opposite bet shipped yesterday, when Runway opened Praxis-1, a robot model built from a video model.

Something to learn

What does Arm actually sell? Not chips. Arm designs the rulebook a processor follows, called an instruction set, plus ready-made processor core designs that use it. Companies pay Arm a license fee up front and then a small royalty on every chip they ship. Apple, Qualcomm, Amazon, and Nvidia all build chips this way, and then pay a foundry like TSMC to make them. Nvidia's Grace and Vera CPUs, the ones that sit next to its GPUs in AI servers, run on Arm. SoftBank owns most of Arm, and Nvidia tried to buy it for $40 billion in 2020 before regulators in the U.S., U.K., and EU pushed back and the deal died in 2022. That's why Haas makes sense on paper: he knows Nvidia from the inside, he runs a company whose designs are in Nvidia's servers, and he knows how to sell to the whole industry, which Nvidia also has to do.

And what are llama.cpp and a GGUF file? llama.cpp is a program, written in plain C and C++, that runs language models on ordinary hardware: laptops, Macs, phones, gaming GPUs. Tools like Ollama and LM Studio are built on it. A GGUF is the single file it loads. It holds the model's weights plus everything needed to use them, like the tokenizer and the model's layout, so one download is enough. Most GGUFs are quantized, meaning the weights are stored with fewer bits. In a name like Q4_K_M, "Q4" means about 4 bits per weight instead of 16, so the file is roughly a quarter of the size, "K" means the weights are squeezed in small blocks that each get their own scale, and "M" is the medium mix, where a few sensitive layers keep more bits. The rule of thumb: Q4 or Q5 versions keep most of a model's quality and fit a model on a machine that couldn't hold the full one. Q2 or Q3 is where answers start to get noticeably worse.
