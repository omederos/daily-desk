An Anthropic mathematician claims the Riemann hypothesis

Levent Alpöge, a number theorist who works at Anthropic, posted a short note on his site: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." He says the method also proves the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and that he expects the Extended version to follow. He says Claude "far outpaced" him near the end, and that its key suggestion was a chain of theta lifts whose length was the square root of the length of the prime sum. His timeline starts Oct. 6.

There is no paper. Alpöge released the proof only as Lean code, in 14 compressed parts, and says writing it out for people would mean "some massive unreadable travesty of 'slop'" of more than 250,000 pages. He also says he asked Dario Amodei on Sunday night whether he could announce, and calls the obvious cynical reading of the timing, the day after the IPO, false. Terence Tao describes it as an "enormous" Lean-only proof made with an internal AI model, and says a first look suggests it uses "the full power" of the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry. Checking is happening in public on the Lean Zulip chat.

Early reactions are careful. Scientific American says MIT's Andrew Sutherland compiled the Lean himself and called it "an historic moment." Kevin Buzzard told New Scientist "let's see if it compiles first." OfficeChai quotes Elliot Glazer saying the Lean statement really is GRH. Compiling proves the code follows from Lean's rules. It doesn't explain why the proof works, and nobody can yet. Daniel Litt called it "strong evidence a proof exists, but that's the beginning of the story, not the end." The Hacker News thread passed 1,100 comments by midday.

One more twist. Stanford's Ravi Vakil rushed out a separate GRH proof today by Alessandro Masullo. Vakil says it is "all human, with no AI," that Brian Conrad has been checking it for months, and that the plan had been to polish it for a year before announcing. It's a draft that went out today, earlier than planned.

Navier-Stokes without a push

Alpöge and Buckmaster also posted a 79-page paper and a Lean package that claim a smooth fluid in 3D can blow up in finite time with no outside force. In September, OpenAI claimed the forced version, and Buckmaster accused OpenAI of trying to take credit for his and Alpöge's work. The forced version lets you add a push to the fluid. The unforced case has no such help, and it's the original question. Tao says the paper is technically readable and follows an expected route: build an approximate self-similar solution with a computer, then prove a real solution sits close to it. Separately, Tao lists an unforced blowup for the Euler equations by Alpöge, Buckmaster, and Matei Coiculescu, with "heavy AI assistance," as the most digested of today's three results.

The repo's README is honest about the gaps. The Lean library is about 2,270 files and 600,000 lines. The numerical certificate the proof depends on isn't in the repo yet. The check that the certificate meets the paper's conditions uses interval arithmetic in the Arb library, outside Lean. And a patch dated today was "not rebuilt" before the repo went up. So the Lean covers the logic, while one key computation still has to be trusted, or rerun once the data is released.

An AI-designed CPU, on paper

Architect Labs says its agent swarms designed Frostbite, a CPU with a new instruction set, and handled the compiler, instruction set, RTL, verification, and final layout file themselves. It runs programs written as interaction nets (see the learn section). Its specs are contracts written in Lean, and agents tested every change against them. On a 7 nm process the final layout runs at 500 MHz. One four-tile block of Frostbite is about the same area as one core of an unnamed dual-issue, in-order CPU. Against that core it averages 4.59x the performance per watt across 25 workloads, and 3.83x on the 15 it was never tuned on.

The catch is in the post itself. These are pre-silicon numbers from a layout that hasn't been built, against a small in-order core, not the big out-of-order cores in laptops and servers. The company says it will put Frostbite on FPGAs for anyone to try and release the RTL and instruction set. The post went up Wednesday and hit 500 points on Hacker News today.

Nvidia shops, Moonshot cancels

Nvidia is in advanced talks to buy Inception, the startup behind the Mercury diffusion language models, for at least $500 million plus performance payouts, The Information reports via The Decoder. Diffusion models refine a whole answer in parallel instead of writing one token at a time. The co-founders, Stefano Ermon, Aditya Grover, and Volodymyr Kuleshov, would join Nvidia to work on its open Nemotron models and robot models. Talks started before Jensen Huang resigned. Tech Funding News says Microsoft was interested earlier this year, and that the price is below an earlier valuation.

Moonshot AI, which makes Kimi, canceled a $150 million contract to rent Nvidia B300s from an Indonesian cloud company starting next year, The Information reports via the Jakarta Post. The cancellation followed Monday's Commerce rule on remote access to chips. Per Kukmin Daily, Moonshot had agreed to pay 50% up front, against a usual 15%. The paper also cites Sidley Austin saying the license threshold equals about 300 advanced chips, and says Commerce has opened an investigation into how Moonshot gets its chips.

Something to learn

What does the Riemann hypothesis actually say? Start with primes. Below a million there are 78,498 of them, and a smooth formula, the logarithmic integral li(x), guesses that count to within about 130. The question is how far off that guess can ever get. Riemann tied the answer to one function, the zeta function, which adds up 1/ns over all whole numbers n and then gets extended to complex numbers s. It has "trivial" zeros at -2, -4, -6, and so on, and infinitely many other zeros in a strip where the real part is between 0 and 1. Riemann's formula writes the prime count as li(x) plus one wave for each of those zeros. A zero with real part σ makes a wave about xσ tall. The hypothesis says every one of those zeros has real part exactly 1/2. Then every wave is about √x tall, and the guess is never off by much more than √x, which is as good as the error from random coin flips. One zero off the line would make the primes lumpier than that. That √x is why Alpöge's note talks about a chain of length √X. OpenAI's October batch of math results, covered here then, only kept the zeros of related functions out of the region past 7/8. "Generalized" means the same claim for a whole family of related functions, the Dirichlet L-functions, which count primes in patterns like "ends in 3" or "leaves remainder 1 when divided by 4." Hundreds of theorems start with "assuming GRH." One example: under GRH, the Miller test can prove a number is prime by checking a short list of bases, with no randomness. If the proof holds, all of those become plain theorems.

And what's an interaction net, the idea behind Frostbite? A normal CPU runs a list of instructions that can read and write any memory, so the hardware has to work out at runtime which instructions depend on each other. Big cores spend a lot of their area on that out-of-order logic, and multicore chips spend more keeping every core's view of memory in sync. An interaction net, from Yves Lafont in 1990, writes a program as a graph. Each node has exactly one main port. The program runs by rewriting any two nodes joined at their main ports. Each rewrite touches only those two nodes, no two such pairs ever overlap, and doing them in any order gives the same answer. Every value is used exactly once, and copying something takes an explicit duplicate node. So the dependencies are in the graph itself, and the chip doesn't have to find them. Frostbite takes that literally. It's a grid of tiles, each tile rewrites the pairs it holds, takes spare work from a neighbor when it runs out, and powers down when there's none. There's no central scheduler and no shared memory to keep consistent. The cost is that programs have to be written in a language that compiles to nets, Vine in this case. And until a chip is built, the 4.59x is a simulation result.
