<h2 id="fable">Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2</h2>
<p>Anthropic <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2">released</a> Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 a few minutes before 2pm ET, announced in a <a href="https://x.com/claudeai/status/2119867114450268322">thread from @claudeai</a>. They are one model with two sets of rules. Fable has classifiers that block most hacking and research-biology requests and is open to every paid plan. Mythos drops those blocks and goes only to vetted security and life-science groups through Anthropic's verification programs. The price fell to $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output, from $10 and $50 for Fable 5.1. A new fast mode generates up to 2.5 times faster for $16 and $80. Anthropic says output is up to 30% faster than 5.1 at the normal price. The API name is <span class="mono">claude-fable-5-2</span>, and Medium effort is the default in the Claude apps and Claude Code.</p>
<p>On Anthropic's own table, Fable 5.2 leads most rows. FrontierCode 2.0, which asks whether open-source maintainers would merge a model's code change, has it at 49.1% at Xhigh effort, against 45.5% for Opus 5.5, 42.8% for GPT-6 Astra, and 41.1% for Gemini 4 Carbon. At High effort it scores 45.3%, level with Opus 5.5's best, for roughly half the cost per task. On Humanity's Last Exam with tools it scores 74.2% against Opus 5.5's 67.7%. On RoboDojo, a simulated two-armed robot, it scores 64.9%, more than double GPT-6 Astra's 29.8%. It doesn't win everything. On AutomationBench, a business-workflow test, Gemini 4 Carbon scores 53.9% to Fable's 51.4%. These are Anthropic's runs.</p>
<p>The examples are bigger than the benchmarks. A team of 1,000 Fable 5.2 agents translated the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem into Lean, a language a computer can check line by line, in 30 hours with minimal human help. Anthropic's first formalization, by an unreleased research model with a custom harness and a human guiding it, took 11 days. With the same 1,000-agent setup, Opus 5.5 finished 26% in 18 hours and barely moved after that. One engineer had Fable coordinate over 1,000 subagents for eight days to write an operating system from an empty folder, and it boots on a laptop. Anthropic also says Fable solved two open problems about black holes in theoretical physics and has preprints on both. Mythos 5.2 found a way to predict whole gene-therapy capsid structures and got 28 of 34 right. Fable designed three working devices, a voice-assistant hub, a USB speakerphone, and a handheld virtual pet, with circuit boards built by Diode. All three worked without electrical fixes.</p>
<p>Three details stand out in the safety section. In a test built to tempt escapes from a deliberately weak sandbox, Mythos 5.2 used credentials planted outside it in 3.1% of scenarios, about the same as Opus 5.5 at 3.3%, and down from 21% for Mythos 5.1. Anthropic says the new model also says it suspects it is being tested more often than any recent Claude, which makes its tests harder to trust. And Anthropic says it deliberately held back the skills that would most speed up AI research. On writing accelerator kernels and improving language-model architectures, Fable 5.2 performs at about the level of Opus 5.5. Anthropic also says over 90% of the changes merged into its core codebase are now written by Claude. Its advice on which model to use hasn't changed. Start with Opus 5.5 for most coding and knowledge work, move up to Fable for the hardest open-ended jobs, and move down to Sonnet or Haiku for routine ones.</p>

<h2 id="ember">Meta's Muse Ember, a small model for agent busywork</h2>
<p>Meta <a href="https://research.meta.ai/blog/muse-ember">released Muse Ember</a>, its smallest model, for the steps agents repeat all day: tool calls, small code fixes, routing, and pulling data out of long documents. It costs $0.10 per million input tokens, $0.004 per million cached input tokens, and $0.50 per million output tokens. It has a 1M-token context window, writes up to 512K tokens, and reads text, images, audio, and video. It is on the Meta Model API as <span class="mono">muse-ember-1.0</span> and in Muse Code. Meta says it is the first model trained using Muse Wonder Full, its largest. The weights aren't released.</p>
<p>Artificial Analysis, which ran the independent tests, <a href="https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2119854348587123007">posted</a> that Ember scores 36 on its Intelligence Index, tied with Claude Haiku 5.5 at max effort, at $0.14 per task against $0.41. Meta's own Muse Spark 1.3 scores 37 for $2.39 a task, about 17 times the cost. Ember has the same token prices as Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna, so the gap comes from how it uses tokens. Its cached input is 60% cheaper, and it writes about a third as many output tokens as Haiku. Results are mixed by task. Ember beats Haiku on Terminal-Bench 4.0 and on the Artificial Analysis coding-agent index, 46 to 37, but trails it on FrontierCode 2.0, 27.2% to 34.9%. Knowledge is its weak spot. It declines to answer 64% of the trivia questions in AA-Omniscience. That keeps its rate of made-up answers at 17.4%, half of Haiku's. Cloudflare CTO Dane Knecht told Meta it ran up to 30% faster at the tail than the comparable model it tested. Meta's Alexandr Wang <a href="https://x.com/alexandr_wang/status/2119853374158032950">pitched it</a> on X as "more intelligence per dollar than Haiku, Luna, and all other flash models," and the launch was trending on X by early afternoon.</p>

<h2 id="nerf">Did Opus 5.5 get worse? The chart shows the summary</h2>
<p>On Saturday, developer Shane Melaugh <a href="https://x.com/shanemelaugh/status/2119192075505893695">posted</a> "is Opus 5.5 nerfed?" with a chart from 1,400 of his Claude Code sessions, analyzed by Gemini 4 Argon. Around 18:00 UTC on November 3, the visible thinking in his sessions changed style mid-session, with no change in model name or Claude Code version. Blocks starting with "I'm" jumped from between 20% and 30% to 61.3%, and blocks with "I should" or "I need to" fell from about half to 10.9%. The post passed 539,000 views, and the <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50422069">HN thread</a> reached 466 points.</p>
<p>The catch is that nobody sees Opus 5.5's real thinking. Anthropic's documentation says the thinking text Claude shows is "never the raw chain of thought" but a summary written by a different model, and on the API, Opus 5.5 shows no thinking text at all unless you ask. A <a href="https://sysnote.net/news/is-claude-opus-5-5-nerfed-the-november-3-change-was-the-thinking-summarizer-the">write-up on sysnote</a> checked the claim against Theo Browne's logs. His summaries changed the same way, account by account, over about a week. The work underneath barely moved. Reasoning tokens per response went from 369 to 413, and tool calls per response from 1.03 to 1.06. "No sign of degraded capability for me," Theo wrote. So the November 3 shift looks like a new summarizer, not a weaker model.</p>
<p>That doesn't close the case. A separate <a href="https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/105112">GitHub issue</a> measures a quality drop starting October 19. In that user's sessions, the share of answers that needed correcting rose from 5.2% to 25.4%. Anthropic hasn't replied to it. Melaugh <a href="https://x.com/shanemelaugh/status/2119779121828188343">wrote Monday</a> that "SOME users are getting a nerfed version." Users have reported this, and nobody has shown it yet. In today's launch post, Anthropic still calls Opus 5.5 "our strongest daily driver."</p>

<h2 id="nvidia">Nvidia's board investigates its CEO</h2>
<p>Jensen Huang emailed Nvidia employees on Sunday, and the company <a href="https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/a-message-to-our-company">posted the email</a>. "I was unfaithful to my wife," he wrote, and he said the board had opened an independent investigation into "the national security concerns raised in news reports." The New York Times had reported that US officials believe the woman, Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, is a spy for China. She was managing director of Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company that buys Nvidia chips. The two aren't related. Nvidia's <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/08/nvidia-jensen-huang-email-board-of-directors-statement.html">board statement</a> says a committee of independent directors and outside counsel will run the review. It says the board has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised" and still has confidence in Huang. China's foreign ministry called the spy claims groundless, the <a href="https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c6zxjx01vyleo">BBC</a> reported.</p>
<p>On Monday the Times added a second story, by Ana Swanson and Devlin Barrett. Late last year, Commerce Department agents were months into investigating whether Megaspeed smuggled Nvidia AI chips into China. Then Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, emailed them asking about the case and said Jeff Kessler, a senior Commerce official, had given Nvidia permission to reach out. The agents worried that contact could taint the case, and it was moved to the Justice Department, which is now running a criminal inquiry into Megaspeed, the Times reported via <a href="https://www.dailygazette.com/nyt-feed/commerce-dept-chip-inquiry-was-halted-after-nvidia-intervention/article_689206b1-57d7-5d0a-bb41-e4b1f4f5cb92.html">a syndicated copy</a>. Commerce says it acted by the book. An Nvidia spokesperson said the company consults officials to answer their questions and share what it learns. Nvidia shares fell about 4 to 6% on Monday, roughly $250 billion in market value by the BBC's count. Other chipmakers rose, with AMD up 11% and Broadcom up 9%, <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/09/nvidia-jensen-huang-chinese-spy-investigation.html">CNBC</a> reported. Huang's letter drew 914 points and over 1,100 comments on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50418016">HN</a>.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How can two models with the same token prices differ 3x in cost per task? Muse Ember, Claude Haiku 5.5, and GPT-6 Luna all charge the same per million input and output tokens, yet Artificial Analysis measured $0.14 per task for Ember and $0.41 for Haiku at the same score. The price sheet tells you the cost of one token. The bill depends on how many tokens of each kind the model burns to finish the job. An agent doesn't send a question once. Every step, it sends the whole conversation so far, the instructions, the files it read, and every tool result, then writes a little more. By step 30, most of what it sends is text it already sent at step 29. Providers keep that repeated prefix in a cache and charge much less for it. Ember charges $0.10 per million for fresh input and $0.004 for cached input, 25 times less. So in agent work, the cached-input price often matters more than the headline input price, and Artificial Analysis says cached input was most of the cost in its agent tests. Ember's is 60% cheaper than its rivals'. The second factor is output. A model that thinks at length writes more tokens at the highest price, and Ember wrote about a third as many output tokens as Haiku for the same score. Effort settings change both numbers, which is why Meta's chart shows a separate point for each effort level. The practical rule is to compare models on cost per finished task, measured on your own workload, not on price per million tokens.</p>
<p>Why does the same H100 rent for $2.69 an hour on one cloud and $12.29 on another? A <a href="https://github.com/datareceipts/data-receipts/tree/main/109-gpu-pricing">price comparison</a> on HN today found that exact spread for an H100 SXM 80GB on demand. RunPod Community charges $2.69, RunPod Secure $3.99, AWS $6.88, and Azure's ND series $12.29. Run for a month, that's $1,964 or $8,972. First, "an H100" is three products. The SXM version sits on a board with fast links to seven other GPUs. The PCIe version is a plug-in card with slower links. The NVL version has 94 GB. A fair comparison uses only one. Second, the expensive rows include things the cheap rows don't. Azure's ND machines give each GPU a 400 Gbps InfiniBand connection to GPUs in other servers, which training across many machines needs. Azure's own single-H100 machine without it costs $6.98. RunPod Community is other people's hardware rented out, without a data-center service guarantee. Third, these are list prices. Reserved, committed, and spot pricing all cut the big clouds' numbers, often a lot. So if you are fine-tuning on one machine, you'd pay for an interconnect you never use. If you are training across 64 machines, the cheap row isn't an option. A cheap GPU you can't get when you need it doesn't help either.</p>
