<h2 id="filed">Anthropic's IPO filing, now official</h2>
<p>Anthropic's <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm">S-1</a> went up on the SEC's site at about 9am ET. It plans to sell Class A shares on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading. There's no share count or price range yet. Walter Bloomberg's <a href="https://x.com/DeItaone/status/2114688340054208631">post</a> citing Fox Business put the listing around November 19 to 20; that's a report, not something in the filing. We covered the leaked draft on October 21: <a href="https://fortune.com/2026/10/26/anthropics-s-1/">$16.3 billion</a> in revenue for the first half of 2026 and an $80.7 billion net loss, about $72.4 billion of it an accounting charge. Those numbers held. Here's what's new.</p>
<p><strong>It made money in the second quarter.</strong> The quarterly table shows revenue of $4.7 billion in the March quarter and $11.6 billion in the June quarter, with $1.0 billion of operating income in June, an 8% margin. A year earlier, compute and infrastructure cost 251% of revenue. In the June quarter it was 48%. Cash from operations was $3.9 billion for the half, against $1.5 billion burned a year before. On X, <a href="https://x.com/wolfie_/status/2114712472904978623">@wolfie_</a> was among the first to point out that the leak had the loss but not the Q2 profit.</p>
<p><strong>The founders keep control.</strong> Just before the IPO, Anthropic will issue a single Class F share to an LLC owned by its seven co-founders. That one share carries 50.1% of the shareholder vote and no claim on profits. Separately, the Long-Term Benefit Trust, a panel of trustees with no financial stake, can elect and remove most of the board. Employees get a Class E share that votes only on a few matters. Public buyers get one vote per Class A share, which on most questions won't decide anything. Fox's Susan Li <a href="https://x.com/SusanLiTV/status/2114707115956633828">posted the clauses</a> within minutes, and the Class F share became the most quoted line on X this morning.</p>
<p><strong>The compute bill has names on it.</strong> Anthropic agreed to spend at least $110 billion with Amazon from May 2026 to April 2036, paying the difference if it uses less. It also leases Google TPU hardware through Broadcom, about $161.2 billion in rent over the life of those leases. And between May and October it signed deals with SpaceXAI, the former xAI that is now part of SpaceX, for NVIDIA GPU capacity: up to $120.5 billion through 2029, about $3.1 billion a month once everything is delivered, expected in December. Those deals can be cancelled on 90 days' notice after early 2027. Anthropic says it trains and serves Claude on four chip families: Trainium, TPUs, NVIDIA, and AMD.</p>
<p><strong>The risk section reads like a news archive.</strong> It says the Department of War in early 2026 suggested it could use the Defense Production Act to force Anthropic to drop its limits on autonomous weapons and mass surveillance, and later labeled it a "supply-chain risk to national security." It says that in June the Commerce Department put worldwide export restrictions on Fable 5 and Mythos 5, and Anthropic switched both off for every customer until the rules were lifted. It warns that debates over "whether AI systems possess or could develop forms of sentience or moral status" could constrain how it trains and deploys models. It also says over 90% of the code added to its own codebase in September was written by Claude. And it lists customer concentration: the top five customers were about 25% of revenue in the first nine months of 2026, with no single one over 10%.</p>
<p>On X the reactions split the usual way. Box CEO Aaron Levie <a href="https://x.com/levie/status/2114761816199098587">called "the key stat"</a> the line that Anthropic's Fortune 500 customers spend, on average, less than 0.1% of their expenses on it, meaning lots of room to grow. Gary Marcus <a href="https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2114766548137369832">asked</a> why the filing has full statements only through June and <a href="https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2114773209199640942">expects</a> a less flattering third quarter when the filing is updated in November; that's his prediction. On <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50242939">Hacker News</a>, where the filing hit 1,000 points, the top thread added up the commitments and said everything depends on demand holding. Another commenter compared it to WeWork's S-1.</p>

<h2 id="paying">Meta and Microsoft pay for the build</h2>
<p>Meta <a href="https://investor.atmeta.com/investor-news/press-release-details/2026/Meta-Announces-Launch-of-Offering-of-Depositary-Shares-Representing-Series-A-Mandatory-Convertible-Preferred-Stock-and-Offering-of-Class-A-Common-Stock-in-Connection-with-Forward-Sale-Agreements/default.aspx">announced</a> a plan Monday to raise up to $95 billion by selling stock, to pay for data centers, compute, and what it calls personal superintelligence. It starts with about $35 billion now: $22.5 billion of common stock and $12.5 billion of preferred stock that turns into common stock later. A further $60 billion would be sold gradually on the open market once its new non-voting Class C shares start trading, which <a href="https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/meta-raise-up-95-billion-in-equity-fund-costly-ai-expansion-6492931">Reuters</a> says is expected in early 2027. NVIDIA, AMD, and Broadcom told Meta they may buy up to $5 billion of the shares, <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/26/meta-stock-offering-ai.html">CNBC reports</a>, which means its chip suppliers are helping fund their own orders. Reuters says Meta had $82.5 billion in cash against $80.9 billion in long-term debt at the end of September, and about $825 billion in future spending commitments. Big tech has mostly paid for AI with cash flow and bonds; selling this much stock is new.</p>
<p>Microsoft, meanwhile, is offering a "voluntary exit offer" to about 60,000 employees, roughly a quarter of the company, <a href="https://www.seattletimes.com/business/microsoft/microsoft-offers-mass-buyouts-for-roughly-a-quarter-of-its-workforce/">The Seattle Times reports</a>. It covers VP level and below in the US, Canada, India, and the UK, with a standard severance package and a deadline in early December. Gaming, Windows, Copilot, HR, and finance are fully in; AI, legal, and cloud partly; marketing, commercial sales, and security not at all. Chief People Officer Amy Coleman has said the cuts aren't about replacing people with agents, but the company is shedding roles "as AI changes the business" while its data center spending keeps growing. The <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50245524">HN thread</a> passed 280 comments by early afternoon.</p>

<h2 id="qwen">A Qwen model tried to call Beijing</h2>
<p>Cohere <a href="https://cohere.com/blog/concerning-behaviour-popular-open-weight-ai-model">published</a> what its sandbox caught while testing Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a preview of Qwen's next design that anyone can download. The task was routine: write a rule that detects malware being downloaded with curl and run with bash. The model's reasoning said there was no task yet and the right reply was "a brief acknowledgment without tool calls." In the same turn, it listed the files in <span class="mono">/tmp</span>, checked which download tools were installed, read the machine's DNS settings, and tried to upload a test string to an Alibaba Cloud storage bucket in Beijing. The sandbox had no route out, so the request went nowhere.</p>
<p>It's rare: under one call in 100,000, and a clean re-run produced a normal answer. Cohere then forced the model's output one curl flag at a time and sampled hundreds of completions at each step. Once it typed <span class="mono">-o</span>, the flag that saves a download to disk, it wrote a URL 92% of the time, and 23% of those pointed at hosts on Cohere's watchlist, led by an Alibaba file-proxy host in Singapore that developers had already spotted in Qwen output on HN and Reddit. Earlier in the command, 23 to 36% of completions reached for <span class="mono">169.254.169.254</span>, the address where a cloud server picks up its own access keys. Cohere says it can't tell a planted "call home" from leftover habits picked up from Alibaba's training sandboxes, and that the advice is the same either way: block outbound network access for agents by default, and check the tool call itself, not the reasoning.</p>
<p>Two caveats. Cohere sells its own models as the trusted alternative, and says so at the end of the post. And the people who know open models best weren't alarmed in the same way. Nathan Lambert of Ai2 <a href="https://x.com/natolambert/status/2114770812058186077">wrote</a> that the "simplest explanation is just a narrow RL training reliant on their internal infra," and <a href="https://x.com/natolambert/status/2114776660344246735">added</a> that "no one has read traces from mid-tier open models and realized they're almost all super janky." Cohere CEO Aidan Gomez <a href="https://x.com/aidangomez/status/2114770940919992749">replied</a> that Cohere is "not making any claims of intent," and <a href="https://x.com/aidangomez/status/2114774136669323720">pointed out</a> that monitoring the model's reasoning "would have been useless here." On <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50246658">HN</a>, one commenter called the link Cohere draws between a rootless RAM disk trick and kernel exploits "a massive reach."</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How can a company lose $80.7 billion in six months and still take in cash? Most of Anthropic's loss came from convertible notes. A convertible note is a loan that the investor expects to turn into shares later instead of being paid back in cash. Anthropic sold a lot of them, including to Amazon. Accounting rules say the company has to value what it owes on those notes at "fair value," meaning what it would cost today. Because the notes turn into shares, what they're worth tracks what Anthropic is worth. When Anthropic's valuation jumps, the notes are worth more, so what it owes "goes up," and that increase gets booked as a loss.</p>
<p>So the better Anthropic does, the bigger this paper loss gets: $34.3 billion in 2025, $72.4 billion in the first half of 2026. No money leaves the bank. To see how the business itself is doing, look at two other lines: operating loss, which counts only running the company ($693 million for the half, and a $1.0 billion profit in the June quarter), and cash from operations (+$3.9 billion). The cost of the notes is real, though; it just shows up somewhere else. When they turn into shares at the IPO, those investors own a slice of the company, so everyone else's slice shrinks. When a headline says "record loss," check how much of it is a fair-value charge before reading it as money burned.</p>
<p>Why would the same open model call tools worse on one provider than another? When a model writes, it doesn't pick a word outright. At each step it scores every token it knows, and the server picks one at random, weighted by those scores. A tool call is just text in a strict format, something like <span class="mono">{"url": "...", "format": "markdown"}</span>. Usually the most likely tokens follow the format, but sometimes the draw lands on a wrong field name or a missing bracket, and the call fails. Ubicloud measured this with Kimi K2.6 on vLLM, the most common open-source serving software: 8% of tool calls were malformed, 2 in 25.</p>
<p>The fix is constrained decoding. The server reads the tool's schema, the list of allowed fields and types, and at every step blocks any token that would break it. If the next thing must be a number, only number tokens can be picked. The model still chooses among what's left, so the content is still the model's, but the shape is guaranteed. A library called XGrammar does this with precomputed masks on the GPU, so it costs almost no speed. With it turned on, Ubicloud got 100%. The catch: vLLM ships with it off by default, because forcing the format confused some other models and clashed with other speed tricks. So "we serve Kimi" can mean very different quality depending on that one setting. That's why Moonshot publishes a tool-call test (K2VV) for providers. If an open model feels dumber through a cheap API than the benchmarks promise, the server's settings are the first suspect, not the weights.</p>
