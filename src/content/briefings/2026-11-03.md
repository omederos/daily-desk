<h2 id="weights">A frontier model edited another model's weights, and the fine print matters</h2>
<p>A model's weights are the billions of numbers that decide what it does. Normally nobody edits them by hand. Training software nudges them a tiny bit at a time with an algorithm called gradient descent. On Monday, four MIT CSAIL researchers, Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab, published <a href="https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/">"Sparks of Weight-Space Programming"</a>, which argues that frontier models can now read and write those numbers directly. They asked OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra to make Qwen3.5-0.8B, a small open model, better at math without any training. Astra traced the model's habit of thinking in circles to one pattern written by two of its layers and removed it, changing 131,072 weights, or 0.016% of the model. MATH-500 went from 7.9% to 48.1%, and an instruction-following test, IFEval, from 21.0% to 62.3%. The edited model is on <a href="https://huggingface.co/RikiyaT/Qwen3.5-0.8B-weight-space-programming">Hugging Face</a>, and an independent re-run there scored 51.0% on MATH-500. In other tests, Astra was given only the raw numbers of tiny networks and asked which game each was trained to play, and it wrote small game-playing networks from scratch.</p>
<p>Takehi's <a href="https://x.com/rikiyatakehi/status/2117313742891327994">announcement on X</a> passed 53,000 likes and 7.8 million views, and the blog post reached 2,906 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50341174">HN</a>. The pushback came fast. Matthew Honnibal, who wrote the spaCy library, <a href="https://honnibal.dev/blog/astra-read-write-misleading">called the post misleading</a> because it mixes different methods under one claim. The "just by looking at the raw weights" setting, with no tools, appears only in tests on networks of 17,000 to 172,000 weights. The Qwen fix used Python and let Astra run the model on up to 50 batches of practice problems, which is what a human ML engineer would do. His post reached 749 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50351111">HN</a>. Then Simon Willison <a href="https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/3/qwen-can-interpret-model-weights/">ran the paper's no-tools game prompts</a> on Qwen 3.8 27B on his laptop. It named Pac-Man, Pong, and Breakout correctly after 46 to 65 minutes of thinking each, and missed Freeway. His conclusion: it's a cool paper, but "some of the wider commentary I've seen around it does not feel justified." He also thinks a patient human with a notepad could get there. The learn section explains what Astra changed.</p>

<h2 id="schools">Google will turn off AI for schools, and Search with it</h2>
<p>Google <a href="https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-learning-era-updates-2026/">said</a> on Tuesday that Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Google Search will be "entirely off for K-12 student accounts until school administrators explicitly turn them on." The change starts early next year. The catch is in the last item. "Search and A.I. are deeply integrated," Google's education chief Chris Phillips told <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/03/us/google-search-schools-restrict-ai.html">The New York Times</a>, so a school that blocks AI entirely gets no Search, and students see a message that it isn't available. Schools that allow AI will choose between regular Search with AI Overviews and a student version built on Guided Learning, which walks students through a problem instead of giving the answer. The Times found the consumer version of Guided Learning still wrote a full essay for an AP history prompt. Until now Google's AI was on by default for schools using Classroom. Randi Weingarten of the American Federation of Teachers told <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/google-shut-search-schools-opt-ai-rcna607280">NBC News</a> that Google "prioritized its business interests over the education of our children." 307 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50353192">HN</a>.</p>

<h2 id="security">Attackers came in through AI tools</h2>
<p>Binance explained how attackers took more than $6 billion on October 29. In June, a wallet engineer searched for a popular AI coding tool, downloaded a tampered Homebrew install, and installed it, according to a Binance security report quoted by <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/03/binance-co-ceo-teng-says-hackers-used-fake-job-interviews-to-steal-6point3-billion-in-crypto.html">CNBC</a>. Binance replaced the laptop, but by then the attackers knew who worked on the wallet team. In September they posed as recruiters and gave another engineer a coding exercise with hidden malware. From there, co-CEO Richard Teng said, they moved "through our internal AI tools" to the system that approved wallet transactions. The FBI has linked the attack to North Korea's TraderTraitor group. Binance says it has replaced 98% of the stolen assets.</p>
<p>GitBook, which hosts documentation for many software companies, <a href="https://www.gitbook.com/status/security-incident-2026-11-03">said</a> it was hit by "a sophisticated, highly targeted attack using AI agents" on Monday evening, European time. The attacker got some infrastructure credentials and may have reached customers' integration tokens, Git Sync secrets, API tokens, and the questions visitors asked GitBook's assistant. GitBook revoked every personal API token and found no sign that content was changed. If you use it, rotate anything connected to it and re-authenticate its MCP server.</p>
<p>The third one is a vendor, not an attacker. A Postman user <a href="https://github.com/postmanlabs/postman-app-support/issues/14370">documented</a> that opening the Postman desktop app installed Postman plugins into Codex and Claude Code, added a startup hook telling Codex to load Postman's skill for "any non-trivial API engineering task," and ran installer code that tries to mark that hook as trusted. The reporter says this shows unwanted changes, not data theft, though the HN title says otherwise. It had 434 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50354960">HN</a>, and Postman hasn't replied on the issue.</p>
<p>Defenders are moving too. OpenAI <a href="https://openai.com/index/preparing-our-next-security-sprint-to-harden-openai/">announced</a> another internal security sprint, this time with outside researchers from Cathedral using OpenAI's models against its systems. In the last sprint, a quarter of its product engineers spent their time finding and fixing weaknesses with Codex. It's also more than quadrupling bug bounty payouts for critical findings, up to $172,000 with bonuses, per its <a href="https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2117444048227123277">post on X</a>. Vercel started rolling out Device Bound Session Credentials, which tie your login cookie to a key in your laptop's security chip so stolen cookies can't be used elsewhere. CEO Guillermo Rauch <a href="https://x.com/rauchg/status/2117670076828803088">pitched it</a> as protection against a "Binance-style attack."</p>

<h2 id="shelf-life">Most famous LLM failures have faded</h2>
<p>Owen Queen and James Zou at Stanford <a href="https://zou-group.github.io/llm-claims-shelf-life/">retested 50 widely cited findings</a> about language models on 20 models released between July 2024 and September 2026. By September, 22 had expired, meaning less than a fifth of the original effect remained. 17 had weakened, 8 still held, and 3 had grown stronger. The ones that faded were mostly things models couldn't do: counting letters, finding facts in the middle of long documents, giving up a right answer when the user pushed back, and making up citations. GPT-3.5 invented 55% of its references in a 2023 study. Claude Sonnet 5.5 invented about 1% and GPT-6.1 Sol none, though Qwen3.7-Flash still invented 69%. The findings that held were about what models prefer: flattering the user, judges favoring their own answers, near-identical answers to open prompts, and a left political lean. The authors' advice is that papers should report the exact model and date, because a finding describes one model at one time. Ethan Mollick's <a href="https://x.com/emollick/status/2117445039211086096">post</a> summing it up passed 1,300 likes.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>What does designing a chip actually involve, and where could Claude help? It's a series of steps, and each one hands its output to the next. Architects first decide what the chip should do: how many math units, how much memory on the chip, how data moves around. Engineers then write the design in a hardware description language like Verilog. It looks like code, but it describes circuits: registers, adders, wires, and what happens on each tick of the clock.</p>
<p>Then comes checking. Software can be patched after release, but a chip can't, so teams simulate the design for billions of clock cycles, write test programs that try to break it, and use math tools to prove certain mistakes can't happen. On large chips this checking commonly takes more engineering time than writing the design. Next, synthesis turns the code into a list of logic gates for one factory's process, and place and route decides where billions of transistors sit and how the wires run between them. Every signal has to arrive within one clock tick, so engineers loop through fixes until it does. That's called timing closure.</p>
<p>The last step is tape-out: the final layout goes to a foundry such as TSMC, which makes the masks and starts production. The first chips come back months later. A bug found then means a fix, a new set of masks, and months more. Most of these steps run on software from three companies: Synopsys, Cadence, and Siemens. That's why Synopsys and Cadence stocks fell on Tuesday, and why Anthropic asked for their data: real chip designs are trade secrets, so there's little of it on the public internet to train on. The likeliest place for Claude to save time is the part that looks most like software: writing the Verilog, writing tests, reading failure logs, and scripting the tools. The physics-heavy steps still run on the vendors' software.</p>
<p>What did Astra actually change inside Qwen? As a model reads each token, it carries a running state, a long list of numbers that every layer reads and adds to. Part of each layer is a block of thousands of small units. When one fires, it adds its own fixed pattern of numbers to that state, and those patterns are stored in a matrix called <span class="mono">down_proj</span>. Researchers have found that many behaviors line up with a single "direction," one particular pattern in that list. A well-known 2024 paper found that refusing a request mostly comes down to one direction.</p>
<p>Astra found a direction that, when strong, kept the model re-thinking without ever answering. It came from layers 15 and 19. So in each of those layers it took 64 units and removed from their patterns the part that points that way. The units still fire and still do their other jobs, but they can no longer push the model toward looping. That's 131,072 numbers changed, the 0.016% in the headline. The technique, removing a direction, is an old interpretability move that people do with Python all the time. What's new is that a model chose to do it and found the right direction with 50 practice runs. That's Honnibal's point: it's impressive, but it's a different skill from naming a tiny network's game by staring at its numbers.</p>
