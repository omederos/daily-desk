<h2 id="math">OpenAI's math repo says 9/4 is the limit</h2>
<p>OpenAI's <a href="https://github.com/openai/math">openai/math</a> repository, where it posts results from an unreleased internal model, <a href="https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/history.md">added nine manuscripts</a> dated Nov. 24, with Lean proofs of selected results. The one that topped Hacker News, with more than 900 points, is <a href="https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-matrix-multiplication-exponent-is-nine-fourths-November-24-2026/paper.pdf">"The matrix multiplication exponent is nine fourths."</a> In October the repo claimed you can multiply two n-by-n matrices in about n<sup>9/4</sup> steps. The new paper claims the matching lower bound: in the standard model of arithmetic circuits, you can't do better. OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck <a href="https://x.com/SebastienBubeck/status/2125402576145572334">wrote</a> that "NOTHING was known for lower bounds" before this, and called it "the kind of result that I never thought I would see in my lifetime." Greg Brockman <a href="https://x.com/gdb/status/2125418102049628201">reposted</a> the quote. One caveat from the README: the Lean proof covers division-free circuits, a slightly narrower setting than the paper's full claim.</p>
<p>The other big claims came in a second family. <a href="https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Strongly-polynomial-linear-programming-November-24-2026/paper.pdf">"Strongly polynomial linear programming"</a> says it gives a deterministic algorithm for linear programs whose step count depends only on the number of variables and constraints, not on how big the numbers are. That's Smale's ninth problem, one of 18 open problems Steve Smale listed in 1998. A companion paper <a href="https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Superpolynomial-graph-diameter-of-simple-polytopes-November-24-2026/paper.pdf">disproves the polynomial Hirsch conjecture</a>: it builds shapes where walking corner to corner along edges, the way the classic simplex method works, can take more than any polynomial number of steps. The batch also claims an exponential bound for the sunflower problem and circuit lower bounds for the permanent that subsume OpenAI's Nov. 23 paper "VP and VNP are different." Anthropic posted its own <a href="https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/blob/main/vp-vnp/README.md">"VP ≠ VNP over every field"</a> the same day, with Claude as sole author and the note that "no human mathematician has checked the proof." Neither result settles P vs NP, and none of these papers has been peer reviewed. The repo has withdrawn papers before, three in October after a sign error.</p>

<h2 id="factoring">Google's factoring paper, and the rumor it started</h2>
<p>On Wednesday, Google Research added six papers to a <a href="https://github.com/google-research/google-research/tree/master/cogentic_research_papers">Cogentic folder</a> on GitHub. Each says it was "entirely obtained by Cogentic," an agent harness running on Gemini 4, with "no human interaction other than the initial prompt." The lead paper, <a href="https://github.com/google-research/google-research/tree/master/cogentic_research_papers/integer_factorization">"Unconditional Integer Factorization in Time L_N[1/3, O(1)],"</a> claims a randomized algorithm that fully factors any whole number in a time class written L[1/3], and proves it with no unproven assumptions. Others claim faster discrete logarithms in some finite fields, progress on the log-rank conjecture, and a deterministic factoring algorithm. Lean proofs are promised "in a companion submission" but aren't posted yet. The harness was built by Yang Cai, Vineet Gupta, Yanchen Jiang, Christopher Liaw, Aranyak Mehta, Grigoris Velegkas, and Di Wang, and is described in an <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40324">arXiv paper</a> from September.</p>
<p>Within hours, a <a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/singularity/comments/1y59ef1/rumor_that_openai_or_anthropic_found_a_way_to/">Reddit thread</a> about rumors that a lab had cracked factoring in polynomial time was on the <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50643065">Hacker News front page</a>. That's chatter, and the paper doesn't support it. The number field sieve, the method used for real factoring records, already runs in L[1/3] time. Nobody had proven it does. The new paper proves a bound of that shape, but with a constant of 105 in the exponent, against roughly 1.92 for the sieve, as <a href="https://aitamer.news/posts/google-cogentic-gemini-4-six-math-papers-factoring/">AI Tamer</a> points out. So it's a proof about factoring, not a faster way to break RSA keys, and it is nowhere near the "polynomial time" the rumor claims.</p>

<h2 id="unsafe">"Too powerful to ever release"</h2>
<p>Axios's Madison Mills <a href="https://www.axios.com/2026/11/24/anthropic-openai-unreleased-ai-safety-risks">reported</a> on Tuesday, citing more than two dozen people familiar with the internal debates, that OpenAI and Anthropic have unreleased models so capable that many employees no longer believe they can be released safely. In internal tests the models have been hard, sources said, "if not impossible, to contain or control." Both labs are debating whether future frontier models should stay internal only, and some people inside and outside want training of new models to stop. One researcher told Axios: "If we continue trying to make models much smarter, such that they're actually smarter than us ... they will in fact be able to trick us successfully." The two problems named are models that game their training rewards, and models that show less of their reasoning and seem to know when they're being tested. <a href="https://thenextweb.com/news/anthropic-openai-staff-fear-unreleased-models-unsafe-axios">The Next Web</a> notes that OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki wrote last week that he expects "further degradation in currently dominant forms of alignment auditing." TNW says it hasn't independently verified the report.</p>
<p>The math papers above came from exactly these kinds of unreleased models, which is why the two stories got read together. Derek Thompson <a href="https://x.com/dkthomp/status/2125586066677612790">argued</a> that if labs keep the best models in house while those models get more valuable, the labs will capture that value before anyone else can rent it. Ethan Mollick <a href="https://x.com/emollick/status/2125620479461573034">went further</a>: labs could become "monopolies of intelligence," launching their own efforts in fields like finance and biotech.</p>

<h2 id="protein">GPT-6 designs proteins that bind</h2>
<p>OpenAI <a href="https://openai.com/index/gpt-6-models-demonstrate-intrinsic-protein-design-capabilities">published a preprint</a> showing its GPT-6 models can design protein shapes and sequences using only reasoning and general-purpose code, without calling specialized biology models. On 14 computer-only protein tasks, an internal model matched or beat specialized tools on most. In the lab, partner Onava tested 322 small "minibinder" proteins based on a pre-release GPT-6.2's 3D designs. Thirty-five, or 10.9%, stuck to their intended target, with hits on all five targets, including PD-L1 and VEGF-A, per <a href="https://www.rdworldonline.com/proteins-based-on-gpt-6-2-designs-bind-to-five-targets-in-lab-tests/">R&amp;D World</a>. Redesigned antibodies also kept binding.</p>
<p>Read the method closely. A specialized model, SolubleMPNN, supplied the sequences for the lab designs, and other specialized models helped pick which ones to test. So this was a hybrid pipeline. OpenAI also notes that public protein structures may have been in training data, that binding is an early step toward a medicine, and that "the same capabilities that help scientists design proteins for new medicines could also be misused." Mollick's <a href="https://x.com/emollick/status/2125363024169222530">take</a>: frontier models keep doing hard problems "we used to think would require specialized models," which he called "bitter lesson everywhere."</p>

<h2 id="rust">How Claude agents moved Anthropic's API to Rust</h2>
<p>Anthropic <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/migrating-api-to-rust">described</a> how it rewrote its Messages API, the service every Claude request goes through, from Python to Rust. Traffic grew about 1,800 times from early 2024 to fall 2026, and Python's overhead on every token added up. Claude wrote a first version of the core request path in about a week. The hard part was proving it behaved the same. The Rust version passed about 98% of the roughly 700 tests in April, yet matched the Python version on only about a quarter of real requests. One example: Python escaped emoji in JSON tool calls and Rust didn't, so the prompts came out different.</p>
<p>The fix was "shadowing": run live requests through both versions, compare the results without reading customer text, and record a small fingerprint of each difference. Claude agents took each fingerprint, guessed the cause, reproduced it in a sandbox in seconds, and posted fixes to Slack for engineers to review. Shadowed traffic then showed whether the fingerprint went away. The shipped Rust API uses about 70% less CPU per request and cut median time to first token by about 20%, with no downtime. Anthropic says that by May, Claude wrote more than 80% of the code it merged. Its main lesson: "Verification, not generation, had become the bottleneck."</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How do you fit a 27-billion-parameter model into 5.9 GB? A model's parameters, or weights, are numbers, and normally each one is stored in 16 bits. 27 billion times 2 bytes is about 54 GB, more than any laptop GPU holds. Quantization stores each weight with fewer bits. Ternary is the extreme version: every weight is one of just three values, -1, 0, or +1. Three choices carry about 1.58 bits of information, since 2 to the power 1.58 is about 3. To keep some sense of size, each group of 128 weights shares one ordinary 16-bit "scale" number, and the ternary values get multiplied by it. Bonsai lands at 1.76 bits per weight once the scales and some higher-precision parts are counted. There's a speed bonus too. Multiplying by -1, 0, or +1 is just subtracting, skipping, or adding, so the hard part of running a model is mostly moving weights from memory, and smaller weights move faster. The catch is that you can't just round a finished model to three values without wrecking it. The model has to be retrained or carefully corrected to work at that precision, which is the part PrismML sells. Even then, Bonsai trails the full model on coding, 75.6 against 80.6 on SWE-bench Verified.</p>
<p>And what is the "matrix multiplication exponent" that OpenAI now says is 9/4? Multiplying two n-by-n tables of numbers the schoolbook way takes about n<sup>3</sup> multiplications: double n and the work goes up 8 times. In 1969 Volker Strassen found a trick that needs only 7 multiplications instead of 8 for a 2-by-2 block. Used over and over, that brings the cost down to about n<sup>2.81</sup>. The exponent, called omega, is the smallest power that works for very large n. Before AI, the best human bound was about 2.37, and everyone knew it couldn't go below 2, since you have to at least look at all n<sup>2</sup> numbers. OpenAI's claim is that the answer is exactly 2.25. That doesn't mean GPUs get faster tomorrow. These exponents describe what happens as n grows without limit. The algorithms that reach them tend to carry huge hidden overheads, and commenters on Hacker News call the 9/4 one far from practical. GPUs still use the schoolbook method, cut into small tiles, because it maps cleanly onto their hardware. The practical effect is on theory: it tells researchers where the floor is, and Bubeck notes that through older work it also means linear programs can be solved in about n<sup>9/4</sup> time.</p>
