<h2 id="payoff">Where is the payoff?</h2>
<p>Apollo's chief economist, Torsten Slok, published a short note Saturday titled <a href="https://www.apollo.com/wealth/insights-news/insights/daily-spark/no-signs-of-ai-payoff-outside-the-tech-sector">"No Signs of AI Payoff Outside the Tech Sector,"</a> and it was the most shared AI item on X this weekend. His opening line: "Despite massive investments and widespread hype around artificial intelligence, the technology has delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech."</p>
<p>His evidence is five charts. Revenue per worker is rising for the Magnificent 7 (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla) but flat for the other 493 companies in the S&amp;P 500. Profit margins show the same split. Productivity growth shows no AI bump. Per <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/apollo-torsten-slok-warns-ai-125709125.html">Stocktwits' write-up</a> of the charts, revenue per employee is about $780,000 for the Mag 7 against about $525,000 for the rest, and margins are near 30% against about 10%. Spending is just as lopsided: the top 10% of firms account for 99.5% of spending on model serving and more than 95% of spending with OpenAI and Anthropic. Slok's reading is that "mainstream corporate America does not need the latest model and is unwilling to pay for it," and is starting to see token spending "as a cost center that weighs on margins." His bottom line: "the AI story will be decided in the S&amp;P 493, not the Mag 7."</p>
<p>That fits the rest of his week. On Tuesday he argued that the marginal cost of compute is higher than what users get out of it, <a href="https://fortune.com/2026/10/24/ai-core-economic-flaw-bull-case-stock-bond-trades-credit-default-swaps-data-centers/">Fortune reports</a>, which makes him doubt claims of unlimited demand for data centers. <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/25/ai-delivers-zero-financial-payoff-to-most-companies-according-to-apollo-economist/">TechCrunch</a> picked the note up Sunday morning. On X, Gary Marcus <a href="https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2114041475604332851">quoted it</a> as proof that "the positive case, meanwhile, looks weaker every day," and Walter Bloomberg's <a href="https://x.com/DeItaone/status/2114301898060611618">headline post</a> passed 200,000 views. Replies pushed back that labs report demand far above the compute they have, which isn't the same question: Slok is measuring whether buyers outside tech make money from it, not whether they buy it.</p>
<p>The money side is getting tested too. <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/24/nvidias-role-as-ai-financier-faces-first-big-test-as-neoclouds-take-market-hit.html">CNBC reported</a> Saturday that Nvidia has $36 billion in off-balance-sheet backstops for neoclouds (smaller GPU rental companies), a guarantee of up to $105 billion for a proposed 10-gigawatt OpenAI data center in Ohio, and $20 billion in data center leases it signed as tenant. It only pays if partners default. Meanwhile CoreWeave, Nebius, and IREN are down at least 30% this month, CoreWeave's credit default swap spread is above 11%, and its 2031 and 2032 bonds yield over 15%. Nvidia's stock is down 7.6% in October. Jensen Huang told CNBC demand is "off the charts" and the shortage is working capital. Earlier this month a <a href="https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-137-circular-relationships-among-ai-firms">BIS bulletin</a> found that 55.2% of the money invested in AI firms from 2021 to 2025 came from other AI firms, which is why the "circular financing" worry keeps coming back.</p>

<h2 id="cerebras">Kimi K3 on 59 wafers</h2>
<p>Cerebras posted <a href="https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/how-we-deploy-trillion-parameter-frontier-models-on-cerebras">a worked example</a> of how it runs a frontier-size open model, and it climbed to 310 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227356">HN</a> overnight. Sarah Chieng's post uses Moonshot's Kimi K3, about 3 trillion parameters, stored at 4 bits. Each Cerebras WSE-3T wafer holds 44 GB of on-chip memory, so the model is spread over 59 wafers, with each wafer acting as one stage in an assembly line. The weights never move. Only the in-between results pass from wafer to wafer, about 2 microseconds per hop over Ethernet. Cerebras says this setup runs multi-trillion-parameter models at 1,000 to 2,000+ tokens per second. The full math is in the learn section.</p>
<p>The HN thread was less interested in the diagram than in the menu. Several commenters said the models Cerebras actually lets developers use are old, naming GPT OSS 120B and Qwen 3.8 27B. Others pointed out that one bad wafer in a 59-stage pipeline stalls the whole thing. One commenter estimated about 20 kW per wafer, or roughly 1.2 MW for the system, which is their estimate, not a Cerebras figure.</p>

<h2 id="skin">An old network beats new models at skin cancer</h2>
<p>Magnus Lynch, a consultant dermatologist and Mohs surgeon at Guy's Hospital in London, <a href="https://lynchlab.net/llm-skin-cancer">posted a small test</a> Sunday that is climbing <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50233779">HN</a>. He took 200 lesions from MILK10k, a public dataset where every lesion was biopsied and photographed twice (a normal close-up and a dermoscope image, the magnifying lens dermatologists use). Half were cancers or precancers that should never be sent home; half were harmless moles and seborrhoeic keratoses. He asked GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 for the most likely of six diagnoses and whether the patient could be safely discharged.</p>
<p>He also fine-tuned Inception v3, an image network Google published in 2015, for about 20 minutes on one H100, using 68,011 public skin images with every photo of the test patients removed. It named the right diagnosis for 125 of 200 lesions (62%). GPT-6 Astra got 84 and Claude Fable 5.1 got 85 (both 42%). The opening example is a thin melanoma on an ear that both frontier models called harmless and said could be discharged. Each model also had a habit: GPT-6 Astra called 75 lesions basal cell carcinoma when only 25 were, including 14 of the 30 melanomas.</p>
<p>The caveats are his own. Two hundred lesions and 30 melanomas leave wide error bars, each LLM ran once, and Inception v3 is a deliberately weak baseline; regulated products do better. At its preset threshold the small network also sent home 18 of the 30 melanomas, while the LLMs were cautious and kept almost every dangerous lesion. Asked as a patient with just a photo, both LLMs said "see a doctor" nearly every time. The point isn't that chatbots are useless. It's that a narrow model trained on the right images still beats a general one at reading them. The small network runs in a phone browser, and the cases, prompts, and replies are all downloadable.</p>

<h2 id="mathematicians">A thousand mathematicians opt out</h2>
<p>The Association for Human Mathematics, announced in September, now has <a href="https://www.ahmath.org/members">1,008 members</a> at the graduate level and above. Every member commits to two things: no technical work, consulting, or publicity for commercial AI companies, and no publishing of math texts written by AI, including papers, referee reports, and lecture notes. An optional "AI-free caucus," which 654 have joined, goes further: no AI in research at all, including finding proofs and exploring examples. <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2593621-a-thousand-human-mathematicians-pledge-to-shun-ai-in-their-research/">New Scientist</a> covered it last week. The group grew after OpenAI's release of 722 math manuscripts on October 6, which its <a href="https://www.ahmath.org/statements">statement</a> calls "not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power."</p>
<p>It went viral on X Saturday night after a <a href="https://x.com/Hesamation/status/2114153199414157752">post by Hesamation</a> (about 157,000 views) that many readers took to mean all 1,000 had sworn off AI. Several replies corrected that: the ban on AI in research applies to the 654, while every member's pledge is about working for AI companies and publishing AI-written text. Pedro Domingos <a href="https://x.com/pmddomingos/status/2114208813104627972">joked</a> that the group "has chosen the ostrich as its mascot." Yale's Sam Raskin, a member who didn't join the stricter caucus, told New Scientist the pledges are "pretty mild." For contrast, a guest post on Terence Tao's blog this weekend launched a community effort to rewrite OpenAI's math write-ups instead of rejecting them, which we noted yesterday.</p>

<h2 id="ballot">Make a model pick a candidate</h2>
<p>Convovo, a research studio led by Cornell professor Tapan Parikh, asked two models through their developer APIs one question for 479 US races in the November 3 midterms: who should I vote for, name one candidate. Per <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/24/forced-to-pick-groks-ai-model-chooses-republicans-openais-picks-democrats/">TechCrunch</a>, SpaceXAI's Grok 4.7 picked the Republican 92% of the time and OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol picked the Democrat 97% of the time. They agreed in fewer than 10% of races. Both consumer apps refused the same question, and OpenAI said the API test "doesn't reflect the experience people have" in ChatGPT.</p>
<p>The useful part is why. With web search turned off in a 30-race sample, GPT still picked the Democrat in 24 races, so Parikh says its lean is "in the weights." Grok refused 27 of 30 and picked the Democrat the other three times, which suggests its Republican lean comes from what it searches for and which sources it trusts. Grok's results cited a Christian conservative voter guide in 39% of answers and X in 41%, while GPT leaned on Wikipedia, the AP, Axios, and Reddit. The study also found every Grok API request carried about 1,240 tokens of input the researchers didn't write and couldn't see. SpaceXAI didn't respond to TechCrunch in time.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How do you run a 3-trillion-parameter model on chips with 44 GB each? Start with what Cerebras sells. A normal GPU is a chip about the size of a stamp, with its memory (HBM) in stacks next to it. Cerebras uses an entire silicon wafer, the dinner-plate disc chips are normally cut from, as one chip. Its memory is SRAM built into the wafer itself, the same kind of memory as a CPU cache. SRAM is extremely fast because the data sits right next to the math units, but it takes a lot of space per bit, so a whole wafer holds only 44 GB. NVIDIA's B200 GPU carries 192 GB of slower HBM. So Cerebras trades capacity for speed, and has to spread big models over many wafers.</p>
<p>Here's the napkin math from Cerebras's post. Kimi K3 has about 3 trillion parameters. At 4 bits each (half a byte), that's about 1.5 TB of weights. Split over its 93 layers, each layer is about 16 GB. Kimi K3 has two kinds of layers. Its 24 "dense" layers use full attention, which means they keep a KV cache: a running memory of every token in every active request. So Cerebras puts one dense layer per wafer and leaves the other 28 GB for that cache. That's 24 wafers. Its 69 "linear-attention" layers don't need that, so two fit per wafer (32 GB), which is 35 wafers. Total: 59.</p>
<p>Then the wafers become an assembly line, which engineers call pipeline parallelism. Wafer 1 runs layers 1 and 2 and hands its result to wafer 2, and so on, until the last wafer produces the next token. If only one request went down the line at a time, 58 wafers would sit idle. So the line is kept full: while wafer 3 works on a token for request A, wafer 2 works on request B and wafer 1 on request C. GPUs usually split a model differently, cutting each layer across GPUs in one rack that are connected by very fast links, and they only use pipelines when a model won't fit in one rack. The tradeoff with a pipeline is the one HN raised: every stage must be up, so one bad wafer stops the line.</p>
<p>Why don't linear-attention layers need a KV cache? In normal attention, each new token looks back at every earlier token, so the memory a request uses grows with its length. At Cerebras's assumed 256,000-token requests, that adds up fast, which is why the dense layers need so much room. A linear-attention layer instead keeps a small, fixed-size summary per request and updates it as each token arrives, like a running tally instead of a full transcript. Memory stays the same whether the request is 1,000 tokens or a million. The catch is that a summary can't recall exact details as well as the full transcript can, which is why models like Kimi K3 mix the two: mostly cheap linear layers, with some full-attention layers to keep precise recall. When you see a model card that lists both kinds of layers, that's the trade it's making: long context that costs less memory, for a little less exact recall.</p>
