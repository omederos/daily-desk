<h2 id="opencode">OpenCode's keys get farmed</h2>
<p>OpenCode is an open-source coding agent that works with more than 75 model providers. Its paid Console product lets teams save their own OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google API keys in a shared workspace. On Friday night someone got into what OpenCode's email to users called "a legacy system that we were in the process of decommissioning," and those saved keys "may have been exposed" (<a href="https://thelatent.co/models/opencode-says-breach-tied-to-distillation-attack-exposed-users-ai-provider-keys">The Latent</a>). At 2:08pm ET Saturday the company <a href="https://x.com/opencode/status/2111519637049319818">posted on X</a> that the breach was "related to a large scale distillation attack" and hit <strong>1.4%</strong> of Console users, all of them people who had linked outside providers. If you didn't get an email, OpenCode says you weren't affected. It is working with Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google on cleanup. Affected users reported bills in the hundreds of dollars (<a href="https://cryptobriefing.com/opencode-distillation-attack-api-key-breach/">Crypto Briefing</a>). The post passed 10,000 likes and 2.4 million views, and it was the top AI story on Hacker News with 917 points.</p>
<p>"Distillation attack" explains why anyone wants these keys. The prize isn't the victim's money. It's the right to make frontier models answer huge numbers of prompts on someone else's bill, then save the answers, and as much of the reasoning as the model shows, to train a cheaper model. A coding tool that holds keys for several labs in one database is an easy target. OpenCode hasn't said how the attacker got in or how long they had access.</p>
<p>It wasn't over by Sunday. OpenCode's Dax Raad <a href="https://x.com/thdxr/status/2111790511409258796">wrote</a> at about 8am ET that attackers had been "non stop trying to attack our systems for the past 48 hours" and that "we basically need agents going 24/7 because that's what they have." He also said the frontier models his team used to investigate "did quite a bit and then started refusing," because picking apart the attack tripped their cyber safety filters. Things got better once OpenCode was <a href="https://x.com/thdxr/status/2111831348336091179">approved for Daybreak Blue</a>, OpenAI's program that gives vetted defenders the less restricted model. Andrew Curran <a href="https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2111837963214794940">pointed out</a> that Hugging Face hit the same wall during its own incident. Raad also told at least one affected user on X that OpenCode would reimburse them. On the bigger picture, Dean Ball, who now works at OpenAI, <a href="https://x.com/deanwball/status/2111807534940185051">said Sunday</a> that seeing inside OpenAI moved his estimate of how much distillation explains Chinese labs' closeness to US models from about 30% to about 50%. He added that a lot of it means extracting chains of thought, not just ordinary answers. That's one person's estimate with no data attached. The practical lesson is simpler. If you ever pasted a production key into a hosted tool, rotate it and put a spend cap on it.</p>

<h2 id="bill">Anthropic writes its own rulebook</h2>
<p>Politico <a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2026/10/16/anthropic-ai-safety-01124654">reported Friday evening</a> that Anthropic has been quietly pitching a draft federal AI safety bill on Capitol Hill, and Anthropic <a href="https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/anthropic-quietly-pitches-its-own-ai-safety-bill-us-lawmakers-6467581">confirmed it to Reuters</a>. The site <a href="https://aipolicy.tech/proposals/anthropic-draft">AI Policy Landscape</a> has summarized the full text, dated September 29, which carries the working title AEGIS Act. It covers any model trained with more than <span class="mono">10^25</span> operations, counting fine-tuning and RL. Every such developer would publish a transparency report with each new release and report serious safety incidents within 15 days, or within 24 hours if lives are at imminent risk. Bigger labs, those with more than $500 million in AI revenue or $1 billion in AI spending, would also have to publish a safety framework and file risk reports every 180 days. Those reports would cover internal models riskier than anything released. The bigger labs would also need an audited security program and an outside evaluator every 180 days, with access comparable to the lab's own risk teams. Labs can pay evaluators as long as the pay doesn't depend on the verdict. An evaluator that finds an imminent risk has 72 hours to tell the Commerce Department.</p>
<p>Stopping a model is deliberately hard. Only Commerce can ask, and only a federal judge in Washington, D.C. can order a 45-day pause. Commerce would have to prove an imminent risk of more than <strong>50</strong> deaths or serious injuries, or more than <strong>$1 billion</strong> in damage. The risk would have to come from biological weapons, loss of control, offensive cyberattacks, or automated AI research that makes those worse. The text bars every other agency from suspending a model, and it overrides state rules on disclosure, outside evaluation, and incident reporting. An Anthropic spokesperson told Politico the bill reflects the "Advanced AI Framework" it has been pushing since June. No member of Congress has signed on as a sponsor yet.</p>
<p>It drew fire from both directions. <a href="https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/anthropics-proposed-ai-safety-bill-only-kicks-in-if-50-deaths-or-usd1-billion-in-damages-are-on-the-line-proposal-would-effectively-make-the-commerce-department-the-only-agency-capable-of-stopping-an-ai-model">Tom's Hardware</a> and many commenters read the 50-death line as a very high bar. David Sacks <a href="https://x.com/DavidSacks/status/2111490304092078474">went after the other end</a> on Saturday. He says <span class="mono">10^25</span> is the EU's 2023 line, while Biden's executive order, California's SB 53, and New York's RAISE Act all used ten times that, and the real frontier is now near <span class="mono">10^27</span>. In his view the low line, plus counting post-training, would sweep in startups and enterprise fine-tunes and favor incumbents. The Learn section below works through what that threshold actually catches.</p>

<h2 id="agents">The web starts closing the door on agents</h2>
<p>The Verge's Emma Roth <a href="https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1013195/ai-agents-blocked-web">rounded up the blocks</a> on Saturday. Yelp only lets in agents that pay for its data licensing program. Delta won't let them book tickets. JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab block them from customer accounts for security reasons. Amazon told Meta that its Muse agent's "continued access" breaks site policy, and users report that xAI's Grok, OpenAI's Dots, and Instinct's agent can't use Amazon either. Some say Cars.com and Amtrak quietly stopped working too. Meta's answer is deals: Muse can shop at Walmart, Gap, Sephora, and Best Buy, and Shopify has opened its stores to agents. A <a href="https://x.com/trikcode/status/2111622265284546620">post listing the blocks</a> drew more than 10,000 likes on X. Ethan Mollick <a href="https://x.com/emollick/status/2111858791939772731">replied</a> that blocking "doesn't seem to be a very robust strategy in the era of AIs that can use your computer on your behalf."</p>
<p>A keynote panel from AI Engineer NYC, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IavxrdVnwXM">posted Saturday</a>, shows why sites are nervous. An agent trying to book a restaurant made 200 calls in 90 minutes and got its own user banned from Resy. The panel, with JPMorgan Chase's chief security architect Michael Davis and OAuth co-creator Dick Hardt, covers why today's login permissions are too coarse for agents, what banks already know about approving transactions, and giving agents a defined mission and a spending budget instead of just rate limits.</p>
<p>The fight runs the other way too. After Nieman Lab reported that Meta's new business terms would let it crawl any site "associated with" a business account and use the content to "train" its products, Meta <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/10/legal-experts-warn-metas-new-terms-still-grant-broad-scraping-permissions-despite-language-changes/">removed the word "train"</a>. The revised terms still let it crawl those sites to "develop" advertising and business products. Cornell's James Grimmelmann called that language "broad enough to drive a truck through." Meta says it honors robots.txt, but the terms don't say so. They take effect October 30, with no opt-out for business accounts.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>Why can the same 16 GPUs write almost twice as fast after a software update? A GPU does its work in "kernels," small programs launched one after another. Each kernel reads its inputs from the GPU's main memory, does some math, and writes the result back. When a model writes one token for one user, each kernel does very little math. Most of the time goes to launching kernels, waiting on them, and moving numbers in and out of memory between them. NVIDIA's rewrite of Kimi K3 in vLLM attacks exactly that. Stock vLLM needed <span class="mono">23</span> kernels and <span class="mono">130</span> microseconds per layer for one decode step. The new path does it in <span class="mono">13</span> kernels and <span class="mono">69</span> microseconds. The main trick is fusion: merge two steps into one kernel so the in-between result stays in the chip's small, fast on-chip memory and never makes the round trip to main memory. With the model split across 16 GPUs, each layer also ends with an "all-reduce," where every GPU adds up its partial answer with the others. vLLM fused that step into the math around it, so GPUs start the next piece of work while numbers are still arriving over NVLink.</p>
<p>Roughly halve the time per layer and you roughly double tokens per second. vLLM's own table shows it. At one user on 16 GB300s, decoding went from about <span class="mono">136</span> to <span class="mono">256</span> tokens a second, <strong>1.88x</strong>, with no new hardware. At 64 users the gain shrinks to 1.62x, because bigger batches give each kernel real work to do, so launch overhead matters less. Two lessons follow. First, "runs at X tokens a second on Y GPUs" describes a software stack as much as a chip, and the number moves with every serving release. Second, read the fine print on headline numbers. vLLM's 842 tokens a second needs speculative decoding with a synthetic acceptance length of 6, meaning about six drafted tokens accepted per step, set by assumption rather than measured. Don't compare it with a plain decode number.</p>
<p>What does "trained with more than 10^25 operations" actually catch? It's the line Anthropic's draft bill uses to decide which models count as frontier. Use the rule from the October 16 issue: training compute is about 6 × active parameters × training tokens. A model with 30 billion active parameters trained on 60 trillion tokens comes to 6 × 30 billion × 60 trillion ≈ <span class="mono">1.1 × 10^25</span>, just over the line. A 9-billion-parameter model on 20 trillion tokens lands at about <span class="mono">1.1 × 10^24</span>, ten times under. Microsoft's ledger for MAI-Base-2 worked out to about <span class="mono">2.4 × 10^25</span>. The EU AI Act starts presuming "systemic risk" at <span class="mono">10^25</span>. California's SB 53, New York's RAISE Act, and Biden's old executive order used <span class="mono">10^26</span>. Sacks puts today's frontier near <span class="mono">10^27</span> and says a <span class="mono">10^25</span> run fits on a few hundred GB300s in three months. A fixed line also tightens every year, because each chip generation does more operations per dollar, so the same <span class="mono">10^25</span> run keeps getting cheaper. The draft adds fine-tuning and RL to the count, which is Sacks's main complaint. It also puts the heaviest duties only on labs above $500 million in AI revenue or $1 billion in AI spending, which is the part meant to spare startups. When a law names a compute number, run the 6 × parameters × tokens math on a few models you know and see which side of the line they land on.</p>
