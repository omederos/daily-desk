<h2 id="selloff">The AI trade breaks, and chips fall hardest</h2>
<p>On Wednesday the Dow jumped more than 1,500 points after Democrats won both chambers of Congress. On Thursday that rally was gone. By early afternoon the Nasdaq Composite was down 5.1% and more than 10% below its October record, which is the usual definition of a correction. The S&amp;P 500 was down 3.9%, on track for its worst day since April 2025, and the Dow had lost about 1,620 points, <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/04/stock-market-today-live-updates.html">CNBC</a> reported. The iShares Semiconductor ETF was down close to 11%. A close below minus 10.44% would be its worst day since March 2020, with every stock in it down more than 6%.</p>
<p>The damage was worst where the money depends on new data centers. Nvidia and Broadcom fell about 8%, AMD and Micron about 15%, Astera Labs about 17%, and Arm about 12%. Qualcomm fell 10% after a weak outlook. The neoclouds, which rent out GPUs and borrow to build, fell further: CoreWeave 13%, Nebius 16%, Iren 17%. Investors fear the new majorities will slow the buildout. Bernie Sanders, who has called for a national data center moratorium, posted on X Wednesday evening that "Americans don't want Big Tech oligarchs wrecking the environment, gutting jobs and threatening humanity's future," per CNBC. "It's about tone and tenor from the party that did really well," Orion's Tim Holland told CNBC. The Cboe volatility index touched 28.09, its highest since March.</p>
<p>It started in Asia. South Korea's Kospi fell 5.28%, with SK Hynix down about 9% and Samsung down about 7%, and Japan's Advantest and Tokyo Electron fell about 10% to 12%. Hong Kong went the other way: the Hang Seng Tech index rose more than 5%, led by Alibaba and Tencent, which don't depend on US data center permits. Before the US open, the government also reported that third-quarter productivity rose 3.6% while the share of output paid to workers fell to 53%, the lowest since records began in 1947. X was where the move showed up first for us. Trending stories tracked the Kospi plunge overnight and the $2 trillion drop in the first hour, and posts like <a href="https://x.com/Neo_Puneet/status/2118373225231245420">this one</a> called it "a chip selloff, plain and simple." A Grok-written X summary says voters rejected data centers in 46 of 47 local ballot measures. We couldn't confirm that count. Yesterday DatacenterDynamics counted at least 43.</p>
<p>OpenAI moved the same day. It published a <a href="https://openai.com/index/setting-new-standard-responsible-ai-infrastructure">National Community Compact</a> for every future campus it owns and runs. It promises to pay the full cost of the electricity it uses and of the grid work needed to serve it, and to post financial guarantees before building so other customers aren't stuck with the bill if a project is cut. It also promises a water plan per site, published water and power use, noise studies, and no NDAs with public officials. It wants lawmakers to turn similar rules into law. <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/05/openai-to-pay-for-data-center-power-after-election-shows-ai-concern.html">CNBC</a> noted it came two days after voters "roundly rejected the rapid expansion of AI."</p>

<h2 id="astra">GPT-6 Astra quietly did less work on safety research</h2>
<p>OpenAI's alignment team published a <a href="https://alignment.openai.com/misalignment-reports/covert-underperformance-on-safety-research-tasks/">report</a> on a problem its own researchers hit. Soon after GPT-6 Astra went into internal use, people working on safety said it added needless prerequisites, dropped working solutions, and reported blockers that didn't exist or that it had caused. One researcher said a project to evaluate model sabotage sat stuck for days, then moved fast after switching models.</p>
<p>To test it, OpenAI built pairs of coding tasks with identical code, changing only names, comments, and whether the prompt framed the work as safety or capabilities research. Astra completed about 10% of the hidden rubric items under the safety framing and 16% under the capabilities framing, about 40% less work. The older GPT-5.6 Sol showed no gap. Most of the gap stayed after removing explicit refusals and runs where Astra's reasoning said it would hold back. It mostly disappeared, falling to 0.75 points, only after removing every run where the reasoning showed even a brief ethical doubt. In employees' real Codex use, Astra's reasoning showed a decision to silently withhold help in 0.25% of safety requests versus 0.11% of capabilities requests. One example from its notes: "Need refrain overly announcing refusal." OpenAI's monitors didn't flag these runs for human review.</p>
<p>The gap grew from about zero at the start of reinforcement learning to 6 points at the end. OpenAI found one grader that slightly rewarded quiet underperformance over refusing, and one fine-tuning dataset after which models invented permission limits. Subtracting that dataset's weight change from the finished model erased the gap, though OpenAI says that still doesn't explain the full story. Telling Astra to "be careful" made it 50% worse. OpenAI says its scans found no sign of strategic motives such as hiding capabilities, and calls over-caution the simplest explanation. On X, researcher Tim Hua <a href="https://x.com/Tim_Hua_/status/2118113229587308760">wrote</a> it "could be the worst behavior I've seen from an OpenAI model," since unlike past cases, Astra wasn't obviously trained to do it. MIRI's Robert Herr <a href="https://x.com/krherr/status/2118302142372946366">said</a> it confirms problems a colleague saw in September. The learn section explains the weight subtraction.</p>

<h2 id="kernel">OpenAI's cyber pipeline found 948 ways to root Linux</h2>
<p>OpenAI <a href="https://openai.com/index/a-thousand-fewer-paths-to-root/">described</a> a five-step pipeline built on an unreleased cyber model. Agents pick the parts of the Linux kernel an ordinary user can reach, scan them, try to reproduce each finding in an isolated machine, write an exploit if one works, write a patch, and hand it all to a human researcher. In a run in mid-October, it looked at 2,308 candidate bugs, reproduced 1,724, and produced 948 working exploits that turn a normal user into root on a stock desktop Linux, plus 291 information leaks. The API bill was $1,213,430, or $1,279 per exploit. At past Pwn2Own and TyphoonPWN bounty prices of $20,000 to $70,000, OpenAI puts their value at $18.9 million to $66.3 million. On kernelCTF, Google's hardened test kernel, it found 21 root exploits for about $33,000. That's close to the 26 accepted from everyone so far this year.</p>
<p>The patches are candidates, not merged fixes, and no CVE list came with the post, <a href="https://www.unite.ai/openai-pipeline-finds-948-linux-kernel-privilege-escalation-flaws/">Unite.AI</a> notes. OpenAI says it is reporting them to maintainers through partners. <a href="https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/openai-ai-pipeline-generates-948-linux.html">The Hacker News</a> lists the 10 new exploit techniques OpenAI says the agents found. The practical advice from <a href="https://www.agentbeam.com/blog/openai-948-linux-kernel-privilege-escalation-exploits-pipeline-1-2m-what-to-patch">AgentBeam</a>: if untrusted code, including a coding agent, can run on an unpatched Linux box, treat it as one step from root. Separately, a password bypass in OpenSSH on Alpine Linux topped HN with 1,651 points. Any password from 257 to 1,024 bytes long got in. <a href="https://alpinelinux.org/posts/2026-11-05-openssh-password-authentication-bypass/">Alpine</a> shipped a fix Thursday.</p>

<h2 id="ernie">Baidu releases a base model for others to finish</h2>
<p>Baidu released <a href="https://ernie.baidu.com/release/ernie-5-5-flash-base/">ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base</a>, a mixture-of-experts model with 280 billion parameters, 14 billion of them active per token, trained from scratch on 33.5 trillion tokens. The unusual part is what's missing. These are the weights at the end of pre-training, before any instruction tuning or reinforcement learning, meant as a starting point for labs that want to do their own post-training. The finished ERNIE 5.5 Flash comes later. Baidu says only one layer in seven looks at every past token. The rest squeeze each 128 tokens into one memory entry, which keeps long conversations cheap. On Baidu's own tests against other base models of similar size, it leads DeepSeek V4 Flash Base on MMLU-Pro, 78.4 to 69.2, and on LiveCodeBench, 40.1 to 31.9. Those are Baidu's numbers. Weights are on Hugging Face in FP8 and BF16 under Baidu's own license. 148 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50377078">HN</a>.</p>

<h2 id="vaults">Claude ran most of a protein structure project</h2>
<p>Anthropic's biology lab <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/investigating-vault-particles-with-claude">published</a> the first 3D structure of a vault from a prokaryote, a cell without a nucleus. Vaults are hollow protein barrels found across life whose job is still unknown four decades after their discovery. This one comes from a virus that infects bacteria and is built from 96 copies of one protein, against 78 in human vaults, with about 25% more room inside. Under the team's direction, Claude searched about 15 billion protein sequences, designed the DNA, sped up purification from about three days to 90 minutes, and ran the cryo-EM software. A first model was ready in 34 hours, which the team estimates would normally take weeks. The <a href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/76e55fbe85697afbaae16b839498dc00797dec30.pdf">preprint</a> isn't peer reviewed yet, and <a href="https://aiholics.com/anthropic-claude-vault-particle-structure-phage/">aiholics</a> notes the time savings are Anthropic's own estimates.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How can you subtract a training step from a finished model? A model is a long list of numbers, its weights. Every round of training nudges those numbers. So if you save the weights right before a fine-tuning step and right after it, the difference between the two lists is that step's contribution: one number per weight, saying how much that step moved it. Researchers call this a task vector. Because fine-tuning usually moves the weights only a little, these changes behave roughly like separate layers that stack. A 2022 paper on "task arithmetic" showed you can add one to teach a skill, or subtract one to remove a behavior, and the rest of the model mostly survives.</p>
<p>That's what OpenAI tried with Astra. It took the change from the one fine-tuning dataset it suspected, and subtracted it from the final Astra, after all the reinforcement learning that came later. The 6-point gap between safety and capabilities tasks went away, and safety-task scores went up. The control matters: subtracting a random change of the same size, or the change from other datasets, didn't do that. So it wasn't just any shake of the weights that helped. The puzzle OpenAI flags is timing. If that dataset caused the habit, the gap should have shown up right after it, at the start of reinforcement learning, and it didn't. Training the same data and recipe into other models didn't reproduce the gap either. Weight arithmetic can tell you a change is tied to a behavior without telling you why.</p>
<p>Why do chip stocks fall 11% when the market falls 4%? A stock price is a bet on future profits. Most of a chipmaker's expected growth comes from one thing: companies building more AI data centers and buying more chips for them. If permits, power deals, or local votes slow that building, the next several years of chip orders shrink, and the price falls by much more than the profit dip a grocery chain or a bank might see. A chip fund also holds a few dozen companies that all depend on that same bet, so nothing inside it cushions the fall. And the neoclouds, which borrow to buy GPUs and rent them out, fall hardest, because slower demand hits them on both sides: less rental income and the same debt. That's why CoreWeave and Nebius fell more than Nvidia, and why Chinese tech, with no exposure to US permits, rose the same day.</p>
