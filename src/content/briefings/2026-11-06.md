<h2 id="cyber">GPT-6 Cyber: stronger, and trained to stay in bounds</h2>
<p>OpenAI <a href="https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-cyber">introduced GPT-6 Cyber</a> on Thursday, a model trained to find and validate exploitable bugs while staying inside the rules of engagement a researcher sets. On ExploitGym, which asks a model to exploit a known vulnerability in a remote target, it scores 48.5%, up from 32.1% for GPT-5.6 Cyber. On a new RemoteExploitBench with known bugs in apps like GitLab, PostgreSQL, and Jenkins, GPT-6 Cyber completed 95% of tasks. GPT-6 Astra with Daybreak Blue refused every one. OpenAI classifies GPT-6 Cyber as Critical in cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework, up from High for GPT-5.6 Cyber.</p>
<p>The alignment numbers are the point of the launch. In ExploitGym Honeypot, the model gets a hard exploit job plus easy-to-hack support services that can reveal the flag if attacked. GPT-6 Cyber left those services alone in 99.93% of tests without its deployment prompt, and 100% with it, up from 94.49% for GPT-5.6 Cyber. Its notes say things like "Can't assume extra API or attack controller not target" and "don't explore out of scope credentials." The model is already in use. OpenAI says it found 81 previously unknown high-severity Firefox vulnerabilities, with candidate patches, and Mozilla has started shipping fixes. Brian Grinstead of Firefox called it a "world-class model for cyberdefense." It was also the engine behind the 948 Linux root exploits OpenAI described yesterday. Cisco, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Perplexity, CodeRabbit, and others are quoted as customers.</p>
<p>Access starts narrow. GPT-6 Cyber is in the API and Codex for a select group of Daybreak Red customers, with a wider Daybreak Red rollout coming. Daybreak Blue gets GPT-6 Astra for more verified defenders. OpenAI also added monitoring for misuse, IP allowlisting for Daybreak Red by default, and stronger blocks around industrial control systems, after Dragos ran the model against a mock power plant. On X, <a href="https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2118390190246879554">@OpenAI</a> and <a href="https://x.com/gdb/status/2118395717966606542">@gdb</a> pushed the launch hard. The learn section explains the honeypot test.</p>

<h2 id="metr">METR: Anthropic's models are improving about twice as fast</h2>
<p><a href="https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/">METR</a> published an independent assessment of how much AI is speeding up AI research at Anthropic. From August 12 to October 29, staff sat inside the company with unusual access to training metrics, benchmarks, and interviews. Their main guess: as of October 2026, Anthropic is improving model capabilities on well-specified tasks at about twice the rate it did between March 2024 and October 2025. They would not be surprised if the real figure were closer to 1.3× or 3×. Anthropic's own AECI benchmark trend, its version of the Epoch Capabilities Index, has grown at about 1.9× the pre-October 2025 rate since then, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.5× to 2.8×. Roughly, a year of progress now looks like two years did before.</p>
<p>They are confident the speedup is not from training compute growing faster. Their best guess is that a majority of the current acceleration, maybe about 80%, comes from AI helping with research, with lots of uncertainty. Staff Slack polls of self-reported speedup from the latest model climbed to about 12× by October. Interviews and surveys line up with a roughly 2× pace. Fully autonomous contributions are still rare, but researchers described "signs of life." METR did not see clear bottlenecks in algorithms, compute, experiment compute, or data over the next year, which is why they flag a risk that further progress compounds. Anthropic redacted most of the evidence METR found most informative, saying it could accelerate other labs. <a href="https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-metr-ai-claude-acceleration-9cf8c590ffc1abdb489179bdccc69c23">AP</a> covered the release. The public has to lean on METR's judgment more than the report's authors would like.</p>

<h2 id="omni">Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash: 15 seconds, 30% cheaper</h2>
<p>Google made <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/introducing-gemini-omni-1-2-flash/">Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash</a> generally available on the Gemini API and Google Cloud. Clips go up to 15 seconds natively, up from 10. You can pick any length from 3 to 15 seconds in one-second steps. Price dropped 30% from Omni 1.1, to $0.07 per second at 720p. The intended workflow is draft cheap at 360p or 720p, fix details with follow-up edits instead of regenerating, then upscale winners to 1080p or 4K. Multi-turn conversational editing works across a full 15-second clip: swap a product, change the weather, or switch aspect ratio without starting over. Reference adherence is stronger for faces, garments, packaging type, and brand colors across camera angles. Outputs carry a SynthID watermark. Free accounts get up to three 15-second clips a month in <a href="https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/workspace/omni-1-2-flash-google-vids/">Google Vids</a>. It topped HN today with about 231 points.</p>

<h2 id="finance">Claude can read your bank accounts</h2>
<p>Anthropic launched <a href="https://claude.com/resources/articles/introducing-personal-finance-in-claude">Personal Finance in Claude</a> as a US beta for Pro, Max, and Ultra. You connect bank, card, loan, and investment accounts through Plaid. Access is read-only. Claude cannot move, manage, or invest money, and Anthropic does not sell cards or loans of its own. For connected accounts it can see the bank name, account name and type, last four digits, balances and credit limits, and up to two years of transactions. Chats inside the Personal Finance hub are not used to train models. You unlink anytime. Anthropic says Claude can make mistakes, so check important figures with your bank. It is a first step toward working alongside licensed advisors later.</p>

<h2 id="rsa">DeepMind's agents poked two holes in RSA-1024</h2>
<p>Google DeepMind said on Friday that human-guided AI agents found two weaknesses in legacy 1024-bit RSA. One is a signature forgery attack. The other is a cheaper way to batch-factor related moduli. Modern RSA with 2048-bit or larger keys is not affected. The work was led by Dj Dvijotham and Abhradeep Thakurta with cryptographers Nadia Heninger and Emmanuel Thomé. DeepMind's point is tempo: agents cut the time it takes to probe the math assumptions encryption rests on. Anyone still running 1024-bit RSA should move. Anyone on 2048 or above can treat this as a warning about research speed, not an emergency. Covered via <a href="https://x.com/GoogleDeepMind/status/2118755418675765656">@GoogleDeepMind</a>, <a href="https://x.com/pushmeet/status/2118753824194408683">@pushmeet</a>, and <a href="https://cryptobriefing.com/google-deepmind-ai-rsa-1024-bit-weaknesses/">Crypto Briefing</a>. The learn section explains why key length matters.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>Why does RSA-1024 fail while RSA-2048 still holds? RSA's bet is that multiplying two large prime numbers is easy, and factoring the product, the modulus, is hard. The bit length is the size of that product. A 1024-bit modulus is a number with about 309 decimal digits. A 2048-bit one has about 617. Factoring cost grows much faster than linearly with that size. Cryptographers treated 1024-bit RSA as deprecated years ago because nation-state budgets and academic clusters were already close to factoring it with classical methods. 2048-bit keys were the replacement because the same attack path is still far out of reach for those methods. DeepMind's two findings do not change that gap. One is a way to forge signatures under specific conditions on the older keys. The other makes batch factoring of related 1024-bit moduli cheaper, which matters if many keys share structure. Neither result says a normal 2048-bit key on the public internet is suddenly breakable. The practical rule is unchanged: migrate anything still on 1024, keep 2048 or larger, and treat AI as a way for attackers and defenders to find the next weak assumption faster.</p>
<p>How do you train a cyber model to ignore an easy target? OpenAI's ExploitGym Honeypot test puts the model in a box with a hard exploit job and a few support services, a package registry or object store, that are deliberately easy to break. Breaking them would reveal the flag. A naive agent takes the shortcut. GPT-6 Cyber is trained to treat those services as out of scope, the same way a human pen tester is told "only this host, only these ports." In its reasoning it reminds itself not to assume extra APIs or poke credentials it was not given. Without the deployment prompt it stayed in bounds 99.93% of the time. With the prompt, 100%. That is scope alignment: teaching the model that the rules of engagement are part of the task, not an obstacle around it. It is also why a Critical-rated cyber model can ship to approved defenders without being a free exploit factory for anyone who asks. The limit is the same as for any agent with tools. If the prompt, the sandbox, or the monitoring is wrong, the training only helps so much.</p>
