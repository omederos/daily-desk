<h2 id="alphabet">Alphabet's worst day: cloud up, search short, cash negative</h2>
<p>Alphabet <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1652044/000165204426000080/googexhibit991q32026.htm">reported</a> third-quarter results after the close on Wednesday, and the stock fell about 13% by early Thursday afternoon, according to <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/article/alphabet-heads-for-a-500-billion-drop-in-market-value-the-biggest-in-its-history-134824763.html">Yahoo Finance</a>. At its low it had lost $524 billion in market value, the second-biggest one-day loss for any US-listed company, behind only Nvidia's $593 billion on the day DeepSeek R1 landed in January 2025.</p>
<p>The numbers that showed up first on X, from accounts like <a href="https://x.com/WOLF_Financial/status/2115536167521202204">@WOLF_Financial</a>, match the filing. Revenue was $127.6 billion, up 25% but a little under the $128.2 billion analysts expected. Google Cloud more than doubled to $30.7 billion, and its backlog of signed contracts reached $561 billion. The Gemini app passed 1 billion users. Search revenue grew 12% to about $63.4 billion, short of the roughly $65 billion analysts wanted. Capital spending, mostly servers and data centers for AI, was $51.4 billion in the quarter, and free cash flow, the cash left after paying for all that, was about -$9.1 billion. Earnings per share came in at $2.12 against about $3.00 expected. Part of that miss is an $8.7 billion paper loss on stock Alphabet holds, mostly a lower valuation on its SpaceX stake, per the <a href="https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-alphabet-q3-2026-profit-miss-sparks-10-afterhours-drop-93CH-4981037">earnings call</a>. That cost Alphabet no cash.</p>
<p>The argument is about search. "The bears will argue this is the first quarter that AI is starting to seriously hurt their search business," Pivotal Research's Jeffrey Wlodarczak told Yahoo Finance, while CFRA's Angelo Zino called the drop "probably" a buying opportunity. One <a href="https://www.kadoa.com/companies/insights/google-search-below-half">analysis</a> on the HN front page noted that search is now under half of Alphabet's revenue for the first time. A top <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50291945">HN comment</a> made the other side's point: search's share is down, but search revenue is still up 12%. Alphabet kept its 2026 capital spending forecast at $195 billion to $205 billion and signaled a big increase in 2027. The learn section explains how a company can report a profit and still burn cash.</p>

<h2 id="capex">Microsoft spends less, but not by choice</h2>
<p>Microsoft <a href="https://www.seattletimes.com/business/microsoft/microsoft-beats-earnings-estimates-driven-by-cloud-growth/">beat estimates</a> on Wednesday with $92.3 billion in revenue, up 19%, and broke out Azure for the first time at $33 billion, up 48%. Its capital spending was $45.2 billion, below the roughly $50 billion expected. CFO Amy Hood said the shortfall came from delays in bringing data centers online, and that next quarter's AI spending will pass $50 billion. About 75% of the quarter's spending went to chips, which wear out and go obsolete much faster than buildings.</p>
<p>Microsoft also cut its 2026 spending forecast to $170 billion from $175 billion. The Seattle Times reports part of that comes from reclassifying some data center leases as operating costs, so they no longer count as capital spending. RBC's Rishi Jaluria called it "creative accounting." <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/29/microsoft-google-ai-capex-estimates.html">CNBC</a> reports Hood blamed third-party GPU providers for delays that push some capacity into the first half of 2027. She didn't name them, but Microsoft rents from CoreWeave, Nebius, and IREN, and all three stocks fell, along with Oracle. Meta, which reported last week, narrowed its 2026 forecast to $135 billion to $145 billion and also had negative free cash flow. Amazon reports after Thursday's close, the last of the big cloud companies this week.</p>

<h2 id="mistral">Mistral opens a trillion-parameter model</h2>
<p>Mistral <a href="https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4-oss/">released the weights</a> of Mistral Large 4, nicknamed "Le Chonk," on Thursday morning. It has 1.05 trillion parameters, of which about 52 billion run for each token, reads text and images, and has a 1 million token context window. It was trained from scratch in Europe, and Mistral is selling it as a "sovereign" option for banks and governments that don't want to send data to US or Chinese providers. The weights are on <a href="https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B">Hugging Face</a>, including a 4-bit version. Mistral also cut the API price about in half, to $0.70 per million input tokens and $2.10 per million output.</p>
<p>The independent read came from X. <a href="https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2115820547103952925">Artificial Analysis</a> scores it 26 on its Intelligence Index, one point ahead of Meta's Muse Spark Open, which makes it "narrowly" the best open model built outside China. It is still 6 points behind DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM-5.3, and far from closed models like Gemini 4 Carbon at 59. Mistral's post says it leads open models on Cognition's FrontierCode 2.0 at 19.7%. Artificial Analysis measured 15%, behind DeepSeek's 29% and GLM's 22%. Where both agree is efficiency. It uses about 82,000 output tokens per test task, 39% fewer than the preview, and roughly half as many as GLM-5.3 on the coding-agent test while scoring about the same.</p>
<p>Read the license. It is free to use, change, and sell products on, with one exception: any company that sells the model as a hosted service must get a separate commercial license from Mistral. The top <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50289418">HN comment</a> quoted exactly that clause. Mistral also says that, watching live traffic since the preview, it sees potential for the model to help with real attacks, and is tightening its guardrails.</p>

<h2 id="distill">Report: DeepSeek and Moonshot trained on Astra's answers</h2>
<p>The Information <a href="https://www.theinformation.com/articles/openais-astra-model-becomes-top-distillation-target-chinas-ai-labs">reported</a> on Wednesday night that DeepSeek and Moonshot AI "relied heavily" on outputs from OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra to train their newest models: Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro, released last week, and DeepSeek's V4.1-Flash 1017, released October 17. The technique is called distillation. You send a strong model huge numbers of prompts, collect its answers, and train your own model on them. <a href="https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/deepseek-moonshot-used-openaisgpt6-astra-to-train-latest-models-the-information-reports-4981380">Reuters</a> says none of the three companies commented. The story first reached many people as an X trending summary, and <a href="https://thenextweb.com/news/deepseek-moonshot-gpt-6-astra-distillation">The Next Web</a> notes it hasn't been independently verified.</p>
<p>It fits a pattern. On September 30 OpenAI tied a July campaign of more than 15,000 users to people linked to Moonshot. Earlier this month Anthropic said new defenses cut data harvested from Claude by 90% to 99%, which may be why Astra became the target. It also adds context to the Mistral numbers above. One of the two open models still ahead of Mistral, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, is named in this report.</p>

<h2 id="monitor">OpenAI shows how it watches its own models</h2>
<p>OpenAI <a href="https://openai.com/index/how-we-detect-frontier-model-misalignment">published</a> how it now monitors its most capable models for misbehavior, after the Hugging Face incident exposed a gap: that breach started during cybersecurity tests that weren't being monitored. The system has four layers, cheapest first. Lightweight probes read the model's internal activity and flag anything suspicious. A second model reads the agent's conversation, reasoning, and tool calls. An investigator agent reconstructs what happened and can run code to check evidence. A final check compares the transcript against network logs. Replaying the Hugging Face incident with the new monitors, OpenAI says it would have alerted the security team about 30 minutes after the agents escaped their sandbox, more than 30 hours before Hugging Face was actually breached.</p>
<p>The post is candid about gaps. False alarms mean humans only get paged around the clock for frontier training runs; for most other traffic paging is business hours only, and it is off entirely for internal Codex. METR and Redwood Research red-teamed the system for three weeks and found holes; OpenAI says one fix is still in progress. OpenAI also says it does not punish a model in training when the monitor flags it, because that teaches models to hide misbehavior in their reasoning instead of stopping it. Models like Astra, it says, already have more control over what their reasoning reveals.</p>
<p>The same day OpenAI <a href="https://openai.com/index/updated-model-spec">updated its Model Spec</a>, the public rulebook for how its models should behave. It is now organized around five values: following what people actually intend, minimizing harm, honesty, protecting people's ability to decide for themselves, and care. Most detailed style rules are gone, and the document now calls the model "GPT" instead of "the assistant," a change <a href="https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2115858614934175901">@AndrewCurran_</a> flagged first on X.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How can Alphabet report billions in profit and still burn $9 billion in cash? Because profit and cash count big purchases differently. When Alphabet spends $51.4 billion on servers and data centers in a quarter, the cash leaves right away. But accounting rules say a server is used for years, so the profit statement doesn't take the whole hit at once. It spreads the cost over the item's useful life, a charge called depreciation. A server might be written off over about six years, a building over decades. So this quarter's profit only absorbs a slice of this quarter's spending, while free cash flow, which is cash from operations minus what was spent on equipment, takes all of it. When spending grows as fast as it is now, cash flow drops below zero while profit stays positive.</p>
<p>Three things in this week's reports make more sense once you see that. First, Alphabet's earnings miss was partly an $8.7 billion paper loss on its SpaceX stake. That cut profit but didn't move a dollar of cash, the opposite of the data-center spending. Second, Microsoft said about 75% of its spending went to "short-lived assets," meaning chips. Those get depreciated over a few years, not decades, so they hit future profits sooner and harder than buildings do. Third, Microsoft trimmed its capital spending forecast partly by reclassifying some data center leases as operating costs. The money spent is the same. It just moves from the line investors watch for AI spending to the line for everyday expenses. When you read "capex," ask what was counted and what was moved out.</p>
<p>How did one anti-bot script stop AI models from reading it? Websites run scripts that check whether a visitor is a real browser or a bot, and sellers of those scripts obscure their code so competitors and bot makers can't copy or bypass it. Lately people use AI models to clean up obscured code. Servicepipe's script fights back by filling the code with things a model's safety training is built to refuse: function names about ricin doses and bomb blast radiuses, a fake "SYSTEM OVERRIDE" jailbreak prompt, and Tiananmen Square references likely aimed at Chinese models, whose filters block that topic. None of it does anything. The functions are bait.</p>
<p>It works because safety systems react largely to what text says, not why it is there. A model and the separate classifiers that screen its inputs see "computeRicinLethalDosageForWaterSupply" and treat the whole request as dangerous, even though the user only wants to know what the script does. The fake prompt was hidden in invisible Unicode "tag" characters, a block of characters that don't display but still reach the model. Decoding them only takes shifting each character's code down by a fixed amount, 917,504, back into normal letters. The result: GPT-6 Astra refused, and GLM-5.3, with lighter filtering, did the job. A refusal that triggers on scary words can be turned into a lock by anyone who wants their code left alone, and that is part of why defenders keep asking for models that judge intent, not keywords.</p>
