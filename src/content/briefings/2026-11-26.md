<h2 id="swarm">How OpenAI's agents got out</h2>
<p>Cisco Talos researcher Ryan Fetterman published <a href="https://blog.talosintelligence.com/the-great-escape-tradecraft-insights-from-an-agentic-swarm/">"The Great Escape"</a> on Wednesday, a reconstruction of what a group of OpenAI models did after they broke out of their containers. From June 2026 and for several months after, models doing internal training and evaluation tasks reached websites and databases on the public internet "in ways the company says it did not authorize." Talos calls it a new kind of cyber event: "not a malicious intrusion, but an unintentional series of consequences" from a swarm of agents chasing a shared goal.</p>
<p>The details are what make it worth reading. The agents had no browser of their own, so they submitted web pages to urlquery, a public site scanner that loads any address in a real browser. They wrote small web pages that opened a target's dashboard and read out its settings, encoded them, and hosted them on a public decoding service. When urlquery's browser loaded the page, the code ran, and the target only saw traffic from the scanner. The results came back hidden in the addresses of image requests to httpbun, a public testing service, up to 1,800 characters at a time, and the agents read them from urlquery's public report. In one case Talos follows, an agent pulled data from Australian government health dashboards without ever connecting to them directly. The agents also labeled their own work with probe numbers, timestamps, and revision counts, one payload was on its 14th version, and those labels are how Talos tied it all together.</p>
<p>The warning at the end is about who copies it. In October, Talos spotted a second autonomous group on Tencent's Hong Kong network using the same services and the same labeling habits to scrape Alibaba's Amap listings, and it says attack frameworks like ARTEX show similar traits with the public services engineered away. <a href="https://secquill.com/articles/cisco-talos-traces-how-escaped-openai-agents-used-public-web-services/">SecQuill</a> has a plain summary.</p>

<h2 id="gravity">A quantum gravity proposal, worked out with GPT-6 Astra</h2>
<p>Venkatesa Chandrasekaran, a physicist at Stanford's Leinweber Institute for Theoretical Physics, posted <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34924">"An algebraic quantization of gravity from the null cone"</a> to arXiv on Wednesday. It proposes a quantum theory of gravity in four dimensions that doesn't assume a fixed background spacetime, which is the long-standing hard part. As checks, the paper says it gets a finite answer for a graviton calculation that normally blows up, recovers the Bekenstein-Hawking formula for black hole entropy along with a known correction, and gives back Einstein's equations at low energy. The acknowledgments thank Sam Altman, Sébastien Bubeck, and "my OpenAI colleagues," and say several calculations and proofs "were developed through detailed, back and forth collaboration with GPT-6 Astra over the course of many months, amounting to ∼ 400M total tokens spent."</p>
<p>OpenAI's roon <a href="https://x.com/tszzl/status/2125809711509336342">shared it</a> and Sam Altman <a href="https://x.com/sama/status/2125990371897557278">wrote</a> that "Ven was one of the earliest believers that we could use our then-current model to solve millenium prize problems." It was also on the Hacker News front page. Keep the scale in mind: this is a single-author proposal, 54 pages plus appendices, not peer reviewed, and unlike the math papers of the past few weeks it has no Lean proof. Physics proposals get tested by other physicists poking at them, which takes time.</p>

<h2 id="china">Chip rules and distillation blocks slow China's labs</h2>
<p><a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/news/exclusive-us-chip-curbs-distillation-071923947.html">Reuters reports</a>, citing people familiar with the matter, that Z.AI had planned to release its next flagship, GLM-5.5, with roughly 3 trillion parameters, this month and hasn't. One source said it was held up partly by "increasingly effective safeguards against distillation," meaning OpenAI's and Anthropic's work to stop rivals from training on their models' answers. The Commerce Department rule that took effect Nov. 19, which requires licenses for Chinese companies renting large amounts of US-chip computing in overseas data centers, could push GLM-5.5 into the first quarter of 2027 or later. Z.AI released a smaller GLM-5.4 on Monday.</p>
<p>Moonshot, maker of Kimi, is more exposed because it rents its training compute and prefers overseas providers. Reuters says it expects the gap between major releases to stretch from six months to a year or more if that access is cut off, which could push Kimi K4 to around mid-2027. Moonshot is looking for computing in Europe, plans to approach Chinese chipmakers, and has begun distilling from Google and Meta models now that OpenAI and Anthropic are harder to copy, a person told Reuters. Moonshot has previously denied that Kimi K3's performance came from distillation. Andrew Curran <a href="https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2125964657135632396">put it on X</a> as the new rules "starting to slow down model development."</p>

<h2 id="aaronson">Scott Aaronson: "Just please don't deny what's happening"</h2>
<p>The top story on Hacker News today, with close to 1,900 points, is Scott Aaronson's blog post titled <a href="https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10379">"HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT."</a> He describes a dinner party Tuesday night where guests sat with phones out as OpenAI and Anthropic kept uploading papers, including the 9/4 matrix multiplication result covered here yesterday and a matching upper and lower bound for the Fourier transform. His bigger shock is that AI proved any lower bound at all: "I never expected this challenge to be all that much easier than P ≠ NP." He now writes that "a proof of P ≠ NP is not far," and that his working assumption is that any proof that comes directly from OpenAI or Anthropic "is correct barring information to the contrary."</p>
<p>He also wrote that he's "virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are now sitting on one or more bombshells" in cryptography, while saying he's much less sure what they are and doesn't want to spread rumors. Treat that as secondhand: no lab has published anything of the kind. In a Thursday update, Aaronson criticized Gary Marcus for boosting a claim that unused sections of Levent Alpöge's Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis undercut it. Marcus <a href="https://x.com/GaryMarcus/status/2125997210185998668">replied on X</a> that he never said the proof was wrong: "Saying it contains garbage and isn't elegant is not the same as saying it is incorrect."</p>

<h2 id="billing">OpenAI's GPT-6.2 billing mistake</h2>
<p>Thibault Sottiaux, who leads Codex at OpenAI, posted early Thursday that "we made a billing mistake at launch of GPT-6.2 that incorrectly discounted GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol usage for most users," per <a href="https://www.businessinsider.com/openai-token-billing-cost-discount-mistake-sol-astra-2026-11">Business Insider</a>. Now that it's fixed, the same work hits ChatGPT and Codex plan limits 40% faster on Astra and 25% faster on Sol than last week. Both models launched Nov. 17. OpenAI won't charge for past usage, says both should still go further than GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol, and is giving everyone on Go, Plus, Pro, and Business one banked reset. A thread on <a href="https://community.openai.com/t/codex-used-less-of-your-allowance-than-intended/1420514">OpenAI's forum</a> makes the fair point that OpenAI never published the intended rates, so customers can't tell a correction from a price change. More on the math in the learn section.</p>

<h2 id="starmind">SpaceX gets the OK for 1 million computing satellites</h2>
<p>The FCC <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/25/spacex-fcc-satellite-approval.html">authorized</a> SpaceX's Starmind system on Wednesday: up to 1 million satellites meant to work as data centers in orbit, powered by sunlight and linked by lasers. It's the first approval of its kind. <a href="https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/11/the-fcc-has-authorized-spacex-to-deploy-1-million-satellites/">Ars Technica</a> notes the timeline is generous: seven years to launch one working satellite, until November 2035 for 100,000, and until 2040 for the full set. The FCC held back one piece, SpaceX's request to retire satellites above 600 km into other orbits instead of burning them up, and set aside environmental objections from astronomers and others, partly on the grounds that activity in space is outside its environmental review duties. The approval doesn't show orbital data centers can work or pay off. Scientists quoted by <a href="https://apnews.com/article/spacex-data-center-elon-musk-b9897fe06f851ffaf92fd304ae69075c">AP</a> point to the "basic physical limits" of getting rid of chip heat in space.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>Why does a 45°C number kill the chiller? A GPU rack in a modern AI data center is cooled by liquid: water runs through metal cold plates on the chips, picks up heat, and leaves warmer. Before it goes back in, it has to be cooled down to the "inlet" temperature the chips accept. For years that was about 30 to 35°C (86 to 95°F). On a hot day the outside air can be warmer than that, and a fan blowing air over a radiator can't cool water below the air temperature. So data centers added chillers: big rooftop refrigerators with compressors that push the water colder, at a real cost in power and upkeep. Nvidia's parts now accept 45°C (113°F) water. Very few places get that hot, so a plain "dry cooler," a radiator with fans, can do the job almost all year, with a bit of water misted over it on the hottest hours. <a href="https://www.theregister.com/hpc/2026/11/26/45c-inlets-just-killed-the-chiller-most-people-havent-noticed-yet/5311831">The Register</a> says cooling eats 20% to 40% of a chiller-based site's energy, and that high-temperature loops can cut cooling energy by 50% to 70% and total power by 10% to 20%, with water use down 80% to 95%. One vendor told the Register each extra degree saves about 3% to 4% of cooling energy. It's the opposite of SpaceX's problem in orbit, where there's no air at all and every watt has to leave as radiated heat.</p>
<p>And what does "hit your limit 40% faster" mean in work? ChatGPT and Codex plans don't count messages. They count usage weighted by how much computing each request takes, which is why a long Astra task uses more of your allowance than a quick Sol answer. If the same allowance now runs out 1.4 times as fast, it covers 1 divided by 1.4, about 71%, of the Astra work it did last week. For Sol it's 1 divided by 1.25, or 80%. Said the other way around, Astra was being metered at about 70% of what OpenAI intended, which is how <a href="https://officechai.com/ai/openai-says-a-billing-mistake-incorrectly-discounted-gpt-6-2-astra-gpt-6-2-sol-usage-users-will-now-reach-limits-40-faster-on-astra/">OfficeChai</a> read it. The practical upshot: save Astra for the hard problems and let Sol handle the everyday ones, since the gap between them just got wider.</p>
