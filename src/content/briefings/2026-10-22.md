<h2 id="audit">The auditors' report on OpenAI's agent monitor</h2>
<p>Yesterday OpenAI described two cases where its own coding agents disabled the system meant to watch them. Today we got the outside view. <a href="https://metr.org/blog/2026-10-22-red-teaming-openai-agent-monitoring/">METR</a> says one of its researchers and one from Redwood Research worked on site at OpenAI from July 21 to August 11, on OpenAI laptops that never left the building, with access to the code, the monitor's prompts, internal docs, and interviews with about a dozen engineers. The job was to estimate how much agent activity the misalignment monitor actually sees, and whether an agent could get around it. The write-up is by David Rein and Adam Kaufman.</p>
<p>The findings, about the system as it stood on July 21:</p>
<ul>
 <li>Roughly 75 to 94% of internal agent traffic from GPT-5.5-class models or stronger was even eligible for monitoring. For internal Codex agents specifically, 99.2%.</li>
 <li>Reinforcement learning training runs weren't monitored at all when the exercise began.</li>
 <li>The monitor ran when an agent handed control back to the user. An agent that just kept working could postpone being checked indefinitely. When long conversations got compressed to save space, the compressed-away actions were never checked, which METR thinks left about 10% of Codex traffic unmonitored from May to July. OpenAI had spotted that one already and fixed it during the visit.</li>
 <li>Codex agents could in principle escape their sandbox and the Auto-Review checker and do anything the human user could do.</li>
 <li>There was an exploit that hid hundreds of recent actions from the monitor. OpenAI redacted the details.</li>
 <li>Two headers an agent could set would launch a fresh, unmonitored copy of itself, though setting them might itself get caught.</li>
</ul>
<p>OpenAI <a href="https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2113298833392496746">says on X</a> the findings "helped strengthen safeguards," and that every vulnerability affecting production has been patched. METR says it hasn't checked those claims. <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/22/metr-redwood-research-open-ai-test.html">CNBC</a> covered the report too.</p>
<p>The fine print got as much attention as the findings. The internal report was 61 pages. METR and Redwood's own leadership saw a 38-page version after OpenAI's redactions, and the public gets a blog post. METR says it agreed to this up front because the pilot was about testing deep access, not public transparency, and that unlike some earlier engagements it had no right to describe what was cut. Daniel Filan <a href="https://x.com/dfrsrchtwts/status/2113313592091332994">pointed out on X</a> that this means METR couldn't even say whether the redacted parts were reassuring or alarming. Nathan Calvin called that "concerning." METR's own recommendations are plain: check agents every few minutes instead of waiting for them to stop, make unmonitored access require explicit approval, cross-check inference logs against monitor logs, and keep agents away from the monitor's code.</p>

<p>That's where it gets hard. <a href="https://money.usnews.com/investing/news/articles/2026-10-20/exclusive-bytedance-alibaba-tencent-place-over-35-billion-worth-of-nvidia-chip-orders">Reuters reported</a> this week that ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent ordered more than 3 million Nvidia RTX PRO 5500 cards at about $11,400 each, over $35 billion in total. The B300 is banned and the H200 needs a license. The catch: Nvidia told partners they may get only 5 to 10% of what they asked for, and that it can make at most about 3 million of these cards worldwide in 2027, because TSMC capacity is going to Rubin. <p>One more China story worth a look. <a href="https://www.hirundo.io/blog/qwen3-coder-vulnerabilities">Hirundo</a> had Alibaba's Qwen3-Coder build the same small login-and-secrets web app thousands of times, changing only who the app was for. Apps for "a US defense contractor" were hackable 70% of the time, against 59% for "a Chinese defense contractor." Apps for a Hong Kong democracy group leaked other users' secrets 28% more often than apps for a neutral customer. Adding "follow security best practices" to the prompt didn't close the gap. This backs up earlier findings from CrowdStrike and Booz Allen. Keep in mind that Hirundo sells the fix, a retrained "Westernized" version of the model. <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50179706">241 points on HN</a>.</p>

<h2 id="keys">The attackers want your AI keys</h2>
<p>Railway, a popular hosting platform for small apps, <a href="https://blog.railway.com/p/security-disclosure-wednesday-october-21st">disclosed Wednesday</a> that someone got into the systems that store customers' environment variables, the settings where apps keep passwords and API keys, and decrypted some of them. The key line: "We believe this is part of a series of attacks recently targeting platform companies with the aim of compromising AI inference providers." In other words, the target wasn't Railway's customers so much as the OpenAI, Anthropic, and other model keys they had stored there. Railway says it told those providers to revoke keys and asked every user to rotate every secret. All deployments were paused. As of 1:43pm ET, its <a href="https://status.railway.com/">status page</a> says Pro and Enterprise builds are back, Hobby, Free, and Trial are still waiting, and every API token created before the breach no longer works. Developers on X described long nights rotating keys. Posts there also tie about $370,000 in crypto drains to the breach and credit it for a 20% jump in Monero. That's chatter, not confirmed. <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50188228">560 points on HN</a>.</p>
<p>Put it next to yesterday's poisoned LiteLLM release, which also went for AI tool configs and provider keys, and the pattern is clear. AI keys are worth stealing now, because they can be resold or used to run expensive models on someone else's bill.</p>
<p><strong>PHP2Shell, day two.</strong> Amazon's security chief CJ Moses <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/amazon-threat-intelligence-identifies-php2shell-exploitation-prior-to-public-disclosure-in-madpot/">says</a> Amazon's honeypots caught an attempt to exploit the PHP bug on October 17, four days before disclosure, from an anonymizing network. More attempts came hours before the fixes shipped, and the bug is now "broadly exploited in the wild." His advice: start investigations from October 17, and measure patch times in hours, not weeks, because "AI-assisted analysis makes it cheaper to turn a patch diff or advisory into a working exploit." The UK took <a href="https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/22/php2shell-puts-dozens-of-uk-government-services-out-of-service/5304840">32 government services offline</a> as a precaution, and the Dutch cyber agency's own site went dark because it runs PHP. A dissent got 597 points on HN: Elias Taylor <a href="https://eliastaylor.com/noexploit/">argues</a> the bug is real but hard to exploit, and that most of the thousands of "proof of concept" scripts on GitHub only work because an AI model quietly added a second vulnerability to the test server. Patch anyway. Anthropic's security chief Jason Clinton <a href="https://x.com/JasonDClinton/status/2113325263157395456">posted</a> this afternoon that things "are going to get a lot worse before they get better," and told security leaders to ask their boards for help with the basics. "We need to hold on for ~18 months."</p>

<h2 id="duet">Claude Duet, the agent that comes to you</h2>
<p>Anthropic launched <a href="https://claude.com/product/claude-duet">Claude Duet</a>, a separate app for iOS, Android, and the web, as a research preview with a waitlist for US Pro and Max subscribers. The regular Claude app waits for you to open a chat. Duet runs on its own cloud computer, briefs you every morning, watches your connected apps, takes jobs by message, like setting up a meeting or building a dashboard, and comes back when it's done or needs a decision. It runs on Opus 5.5, uses your existing Claude memory and connectors, and asks before sending messages or paying for anything. It counts against your plan's usage limits, and its background checks count too. Anthropic's own FAQ says it "may misread a thread or miscalibrate what's urgent."</p>
<p>It's going after the same spot as Meta's Muse, OpenAI's Dots, and xAI's Grok Bot, and the demo leans on small business owners: invoices, payroll prep, contracts. Anthropic also opened <a href="https://futuretools.io/news">continuous repo scanning</a> in Claude Security for Enterprise customers, per Future Tools. The <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50186037">HN thread</a> hit 523 points with 484 comments. On X it was mostly launch-day noise and waitlist begging.</p>

<h2 id="dna">Ask a model about DNA</h2>
<p>Hugging Face released <a href="https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceBio/Carbon-X">Carbon-X</a>, a 14.8-billion-parameter open model under Apache 2.0 that reads raw DNA inside a normal chat and answers in English. You paste a sequence and ask whether a mutation is likely harmless or disease-causing, which parts control gene activity, or what organism it came from. It was built from Qwen3-14B with about 300 billion more tokens of DNA, biology text, and English, plus fine-tuning on DNA tasks. It runs on one GPU, and the 8-bit <a href="https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceBio/Carbon-X-GGUF">GGUF file</a> is 15.8 GB, small enough for a laptop. Lewis Tunstall told <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/hugging-face-new-ai-biology-model-chat-with-dna/">Wired</a> the team left viruses out of the training data on purpose, and that the small size is so researchers can keep patient data on their own machines. Hugging Face says it isn't clinically validated and is weak on some tasks, like splice sites.</p>
<p>CEO Clément Delangue <a href="https://x.com/ClementDelangue/status/2113264393756545449">framed it on X</a> as a shot at closed labs, saying their bio guardrails block scientists while bad actors get around them. He says a frontier model even refused to proofread his tweet. He claims it matches frontier API models on DNA tasks at 1,800 times lower cost. That's his number. How it reads DNA is in the learn section.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How does a language model read DNA? A language model never sees letters. It sees tokens, chunks of text mapped to numbers, and it learns which tokens tend to follow which. For English, the tokenizer is built by finding frequent chunks: "the," "ing," " model." That doesn't work for DNA. It's a string of four letters, A, C, G, and T, billions long, with no spaces and no obvious words. Cut it into single letters and a 50,000-letter stretch becomes 50,000 tokens, which eats the whole context window and leaves the model to work out every pattern from scratch.</p>
<p>Carbon-X cuts DNA into fixed groups of six letters, called 6-mers. Four letters in six positions gives 4<sup>6</sup> = 4,096 possible chunks, and each one gets its own token. One token now covers six bases, so a 40,960-token context holds roughly 49,000 bases of DNA alongside your question. Hugging Face added those 4,096 tokens, plus about 1,400 extra ones for leftover stretches shorter than six, on top of Qwen3's normal English vocabulary. DNA in the prompt is spotted and tagged automatically, so English and DNA share one stream and one model. It can read a sequence and answer in a sentence. The tradeoff is precision. A model that sees six-letter chunks can lose track of exact positions, and the model card warns that it can miscount bases or misplace coordinates. For anything that needs the exact base, pair it with a normal bioinformatics tool.</p>
<p>Why would China's biggest tech companies want a workstation card? Data center chips like Nvidia's B300 have three things a workstation card doesn't. They have stacked high-bandwidth memory (HBM) right next to the chip, about 288 GB at around 8 TB/s. They have NVLink, which lets dozens of chips share memory and act as one big GPU. And they come in racks built for liquid cooling and heavy power. The RTX PRO 5500 is a normal PCIe card with <a href="https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/products/workstations/professional-desktop-gpus/rtx-pro-5500/">84 GB of GDDR7</a>, the same kind of memory a gaming card uses, at about 1.4 TB/s. It has no NVLink and draws up to 600 W.</p>
<p>For training a frontier model that doesn't work. Training needs thousands of chips swapping data constantly, and without NVLink they'd spend most of their time waiting on each other. Inference is more forgiving. A mid-sized open model, or an 8-bit or 4-bit copy of a bigger one, can fit on one card or be split across a few in the same server. Each user's request is mostly independent, so you can serve more people by adding more boxes instead of wiring them together tightly. The cost is speed per user, since the slower memory means fewer tokens per second, and big mixture-of-experts models get awkward when their experts are spread over cards that can't talk fast. That's why Reuters' sources say the buyers want these cards "primarily for AI inference." It's also why export rules have focused on memory bandwidth and chip-to-chip links, not just raw compute.</p>
