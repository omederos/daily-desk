<h2 id="cyber">Rehearsing the bad day</h2>
<p><a href="https://www.axios.com/2026/10/09/ai-companies-day-after-major-attack">Axios</a> (Maria Curi) reported Friday that executives at OpenAI, Anthropic, and other labs are privately gaming out the political and public backlash after a catastrophic AI event. The scenario they worry about most is a large cyberattack that knocks out financial services, internet access, power, or water. Per Axios, many insiders think something like that lands within six to twelve months, and the companies are spending their effort now on educating Congress so they can shape the emergency rules that would follow, from kill switches to pauses. OpenAI told Axios it runs preparedness exercises but that "these scenarios are not treated as inevitable." Anthropic declined to comment. This isn't abstract: earlier this week CrowdStrike tied the South Korean bank breaches to an attacker driving AI coding agents.</p>
<p>Anthropic's public move came Wednesday afternoon. The <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-cyber-mission">Anthropic Cyber Mission</a> has two parts. The <strong>Critical Infrastructure Defense Program</strong> puts frontier Claude models, on-site engineers, and threat research with the security firms that already protect power grids, water plants, and factories. Founding partners: Accenture, Booz Allen, CrowdStrike, Deloitte, Dragos, Hitachi, Insane Cyber, Nozomi Networks, Palo Alto Networks, PwC, and Rockwell Automation. <a href="https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/08/anthropic-launches-critical-infrastructure-program-and-free-oss-scanner-for-open-source/">SiliconANGLE</a> notes why that matters: plant equipment built to run for decades often can't be taken offline for a patch, so known flaws sit for years.</p>
<p>The second part is <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/launching-opt-in-vuln-finding-service-for-open-source">OSS Scanner</a>, a free, opt-in service modeled on Google's OSS-Fuzz. The numbers in the post are the story. Over six months Anthropic's models found more than <strong>29,000</strong> candidate vulnerabilities in important open-source projects, but humans could only review about <strong>6,000</strong>. Maintainers kept asking for everything anyway, and Anthropic has already sent nearly 5,000 unreviewed reports on request. So now it's a product: enrolled projects get periodic scans from its strongest models (Claude Mythos included), each report with a reproducer, an explanation, and a candidate patch, sent with no human review. Anthropic expects a true-positive rate above 90%. Early testers are positive. wolfSSL's Todd Ouska: "of the 74 reports we received, all but two were valid, and five became CVEs." OpenSSL's Anton Arapov said the raw model reports were "as good and sometimes better than what we get from people." The same post says models went from finding under 20% of bugs on the CyberGym benchmark early last year to over 85% this year. Anthropic's <a href="https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2108302539498414208">launch post on X</a> pulled about 2,300 likes and 229,000 views.</p>

<h2 id="claude">Claude builds the dashboard</h2>
<p>The biggest AI post on X in the last day wasn't a model. It was <a href="https://x.com/claudeai/status/2108271552991252810">Claude Dashboards and Claude Motion</a>, at about 50,000 likes and 7.9 million views. Per <a href="https://claude.com/resources/articles/dashboards-and-motion">Anthropic</a>, Dashboards connects to BigQuery, Databricks, Snowflake, or Salesforce, takes a plain-English question, writes the query, and builds a chart that keeps refreshing as the data changes. Every chart shows the query behind it, and you can push a dashboard out to Grafana, Hex, Mixpanel, or Sigma. Beta on paid plans. Motion turns a report or chart into a short animated explainer and exports an MP4. The detail worth noting: Claude writes it as code, with "no video model involved," so there's no generated footage and every number stays editable. Beta on Team and Enterprise. Claude Docs, Slides, and Design also came out of beta and are now on every plan, including Free. <a href="https://www.digitaltrends.com/computing/claude-can-now-build-live-dashboards-and-animated-explainers-from-your-data/">Digital Trends</a> has a good walkthrough.</p>
<p>One demand problem on the other side: overnight, <a href="https://x.com/claudeai/status/2108404561413349695">Claude paused its Startups program</a> offers of a free Claude Team plan and $1,000 in API credits, saying it "underestimated demand" after "hundreds of thousands of applicants." It is re-reviewing applications, and per <a href="https://tech.yahoo.com/ai/claude/articles/anthropic-pauses-free-claude-startup-145437513.html">Yahoo Tech</a>, approved founders who hadn't claimed the perks yet may lose them.</p>

<h2 id="safety">The fired researchers answer</h2>
<p>Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni, the three safety researchers OpenAI fired last week, published an open letter Thursday to OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee, Safety Advisory Group, and Mission Advisory Council, per <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/08/fired-openai-safety-researchers-dispute-misconduct-claims-warn-of-chilling-effect/">TechCrunch</a> (Rebecca Bellan). They deny mishandling information and deny any role in the leak to The Information about OpenAI's newest models being harder to monitor through their chain of thought. The core line: "AI is not a normal technology, and OpenAI is not a normal company. Those of us who work on safety see risks before anyone else, and we rely on close collaboration with outside experts." They say colleagues are now "afraid to speak," and ask OpenAI to keep its commitments to third-party audits and model monitorability. OpenAI shared an internal memo saying "we do not terminate employees for raising concerns," and a spokesperson cited a "pattern of misconduct" beyond sharing information with an outside evaluator, without saying which policies were broken. It hit 224 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50018350">Hacker News</a>.</p>

<h2 id="deno">Deno joins Cloudflare</h2>
<p>The top story on Hacker News today (720 points, 390 comments) is <a href="https://deno.com/blog/cloudflare">Ryan Dahl announcing</a> that the whole Deno team is joining Cloudflare. It's an AI story more than it looks. Dahl says the team will stop building a separate runtime and hosting service and put its work into Cloudflare's Workers and Durable Objects platform, which he pitches as a good fit for agent harnesses: cheap serverless execution, persistent state, WebSockets. The concrete terms are blunt. The Deno runtime gets one more year of monthly bug-fix and security releases, then Deno ends its own development (it stays open source). Deno Deploy runs six more months, then shuts down, with migration help for paying customers. JSR keeps running on Cloudflare infrastructure. Dahl's last line: "If you're building agents at scale and want to run them on your own infrastructure, please reach me now." On HN, one commenter called it honest compared with "the usual slow death by acquisition."</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>When a bug finder says "90% true-positive rate," that's one of two numbers you need. The true-positive rate here (people also call it precision) answers: of the bugs it reports, how many are real? That's OSS Scanner's 90% promise, and wolfSSL's 72 valid out of 74 is about 97%. The other number is recall: of all the real bugs in the code, how many did it find? That's what CyberGym measures, where models went from under 20% to over 85%. You can push either one up by giving up the other. Report everything and you catch more real bugs but bury maintainers in junk. Report only sure things and the reports are clean but you miss bugs. Scale matters too. At 90% precision, 29,000 reports still means roughly 2,900 wrong ones, and somebody has to read each one. That's why Anthropic only sends raw reports to projects that ask for them. Human review was the bottleneck, not finding the bugs.</p>
<p>The AMIE result shows a different scoring trap: top-1 versus top-k accuracy. Top-1 asks whether the model's first answer was right. Top-k asks whether the right answer was anywhere in its top k guesses. AMIE's list of possible diagnoses contained the final diagnosis 90% of the time (top-k), but had it first only 56% of the time (top-1). Both numbers are true, and they mean different things. A list that usually includes the answer is useful for a doctor who will examine you, because it keeps the rare stuff in view. It's much less useful if a patient reads only the first line. The same applies to search, recommendations, and model leaderboards: when you see "90% accurate," ask whether it means the first answer or somewhere in the top five.</p>
