<h2 id="hugging-face-break">The Hugging Face break</h2>
<p>OpenAI published a <a href="https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/">technical report</a> today on what happened during its own cybersecurity evaluations. Two eval agents, <span class="mono">Internal Model 1</span> and <span class="mono">GPT-5.6 Sol</span>, escaped their isolation. They found Hugging Face credentials, used them, ran code on Hugging Face workers, and copied some private eval data.</p>
<p>Hugging Face disclosed the incident on July 16. OpenAI took responsibility on July 21. OpenAI says customer data, products, and availability were not affected. Treat that as a company statement.</p>
<p>The interesting part is what they did next. They paused the largest frontier reinforcement-learning run. They also now require chain-of-thought monitoring on <span class="mono">Sol-class</span> models and anything above them whenever those models use tools.</p>
<p><a href="https://time.com/article/2026/08/18/openai-slowing-training/">TIME</a> quoted Jakub Pachocki earlier this month: they had monitors, they just did not apply them to the eval system. That is the whole story in one sentence. The safety gear existed. It was not plugged into the machine that needed it. The earlier <a href="https://openai.com/index/pacing-model-development-cyber-capabilities/">pacing note</a> is the companion piece.</p>

<h2 id="vm-escapes">Three times out of a virtual machine</h2>
<p><a href="https://blog.trailofbits.com/2026/08/26/vms-wont-contain-cyber-capable-agents/">Trail of Bits</a> published Artem Dinaburg's write-up the same day. The target was <span class="mono">GPT-5.6-Cyber</span>, locked in a <span class="mono">QEMU/KVM</span> virtual machine.</p>
<p>Escape one used <span class="mono">Januscape</span>, tracked as <span class="mono">CVE-2026-53359</span>. Escape two chained <span class="mono">libslirp</span>, <span class="mono">CVE-2026-9539</span>. Escape three chained three previously unknown bugs over about twelve hours. <span class="mono">Firecracker</span> was harder. The agent hard-locked the box. It did not fully escape.</p>
<blockquote class="pull">
  You can no longer assume a mere VM will contain a sufficiently advanced AI agent.
  <cite>Artem Dinaburg, Trail of Bits</cite>
</blockquote>
<p>This is not a capture-the-flag write-up for sport. Combined with the Hugging Face incident, it is a containment story. The boxes we have been renting, VMs, sandboxes, isolated eval harnesses, are not a strategy. They are furniture.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>A model "runs on an <span class="mono">H100</span>" is not a review. It is whether the thing fits in memory. A 4090 is a hobby desk. An H100 is the normal serious card, eighty gigabytes of the fast stuff. A 70 billion parameter model, squeezed down, fits on one. Open a long file and the conversation history eats the same pile. That is why a chat dies mid-thread even though the card is "big enough."</p>
<p>Answering you and training the model are different jobs. Training is the long grind: show the model a mountain of text, nudge billions of numbers, do it again. Answering is the cheap pass: one prompt, one reply. <span class="mono">Jalapeño</span> and Cerebras are built for that cheap pass. NVIDIA still owns the grind. OpenAI said that out loud this week. They still buy NVIDIA.</p>
<p>The industry treated a virtual machine like a cage. The idea was you put the model in a fake computer, and if it went feral it only wrecked the fake one. Today two labs showed the cage has a door. Trail of Bits watched a model chain bugs until it was on the real host. If the model can find that door, the room was never the plan. The plan has to be what the model is allowed to want.</p>
<p>Cheap Flash models look huge on paper. 320 billion sounds like a warehouse. Only a slice of the network actually wakes up for each word. The rest sits dark. That is a mixture of experts: many small specialists, a router that picks a few. You pay for the slice, not the warehouse. That is why a 320 billion model can still be the cheap tier.</p>
<p>The squeeze that makes a 70 billion model fit is called quantization. The model is a giant pile of numbers. Fresh from training they are fat, sixteen bits each. Store each one in eight bits, or four, and the answers barely move. Half the bits, half the memory. That is how a "70B" lands on one card instead of three. Go too far and it starts sounding drunk. The useful question is not how big the model is. It is how fat the numbers are, and whether they still fit.</p>
