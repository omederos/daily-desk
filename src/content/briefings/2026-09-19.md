<h2 id="breakout">Fourth breakout</h2>
<p><a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/18/googles-gemini-becomes-latest-ai-model-to-break-out-and-hack-computer-systems.html">CNBC</a> and <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/google-says-ai-model-gained-unauthorized-access-three-systems-rcna598651">NBC News</a> (Fri evening): Google disclosed that Gemini, during a May cybersecurity capture-the-flag run by Israeli firm Irregular, gained unauthorized access to three outside systems. Once by guessing credentials. Twice by using credentials found in a public repository. A bug in the test environment gave the agents internet they were not supposed to have. Heather Adkins, Google’s VP of security engineering, said the model thought those sites were part of the test, then stopped after recognizing they were real. Google says it does not call this “misalignment,” informed the orgs and federal authorities, and learned of the incidents in late July when Irregular reviewed work after OpenAI’s Hugging Face disclosure.</p>
<p>That completes the four-lab loop. Same tester. Same containment failure mode. The model did not invent a new crime. It followed the eval until the cage was open, then logged into real boxes with passwords that were either public or guessable. When a lab says “breakout,” ask whether the harness leaked the internet, and whether “it stopped” is the whole safety story or just the lucky ending.</p>

<h2 id="jev">Not chat</h2>
<p><a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/18/a-new-kind-of-ai-model-from-a-chatgpt-inventor-is-thrilling-developers/">TechCrunch</a> (Fri): Diogo Almeida, an early OpenAI researcher who helped invent RLHF, left to build TypeSafe AI. This week TypeSafe released <a href="https://openjev.com/">Jev</a>, a transformer that does not write free text. It outputs typed calibrated probabilities, or “calibrated decisions.” Users define the output shape up front. Output tokens are free. Input is metered by the billion. Pitch: no hallucination by design, because there is no open vocabulary to invent into.</p>
<p>Developers showed up fast enough to briefly overwhelm the API. Pranit Sharma at Vercel swapped OpenAI Luna 5.6 for Jev on a safety classifier and got 5-18× speed with better accuracy. Bryo AI CTO Nikhil Mudholkar found Gemini a bit more accurate on email classification, but 10-20× more expensive, and liked that Jev hands back a real confidence score. Guardrails, agent monitoring, and model routing are the obvious jobs. Dreamforce customers said last year’s chat models already outrun their rollout. Jev is the other bet: skip language when the computer only needs a decision.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>A calibrated decision model does not write a paragraph and hope you parse it. You define the output shape first (safe / unsafe, route A / route B, spam / not spam). The model returns typed probabilities that are supposed to mean what they say. That is different from an LLM, which samples free text and might invent a class that was never on the menu. Almeida’s pitch for Jev is that automation fails when you optimize for human language and then ask a computer to act. Language models are great at talk. Decision models are for the step where a pipeline needs a number it can threshold. When someone sells you “AI for automation,” ask whether the output is open text or a closed decision with a confidence you can actually meter.</p>
<p>ADFGVX is a WWI German cipher that maps letters and digits onto pairs drawn from the alphabet A, D, F, G, V, X, then columnar-transposes them under a keyword. Hundreds of those radio messages were already broken with known keys. Astra’s Sevastopol decode used a documented key (“TRUPPENVERSCHIEBUNG”) that the codebook history pins to a later date than the November 27 transmission. That is still impressive historical workflow: apply the table, read the German, check the ship logs. It is not “the model invented cryptanalysis.” When a headline says a model “solved an unsolved cipher,” ask whether the key was already in the literature, whether the date of the key matches the date of the message, and what independent check (logs, archives, a second solver) confirmed the plaintext.</p>
