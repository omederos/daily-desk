<h2 id="math">OpenAI drops 722 math papers</h2>
<p><a href="https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/">OpenAI</a> (Tue evening) published a pile of new results from an unnamed "internal frontier model" straight to <a href="https://github.com/openai/math">GitHub</a>: <strong>722 manuscripts in 372 families</strong>. The README says the model was posed about <strong>4,000 problems</strong>, and the average kept result used roughly <strong>three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking</strong>. Two results broke that recipe: a zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function (its Re(s) &gt; 11/12 writeup was human-edited for readability) and the Hodge conjecture for CM abelian varieties. <a href="https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-maths-722-papers-unreleased-model-github">The Next Web</a> says it is the same model behind last month's Navier-Stokes claim, and <a href="https://the-decoder.com/openai-dumps-372-ai-generated-math-proofs-on-github-telling-the-academic-world-to-keep-up/">The Decoder</a> notes that one needed a 10,000-agent swarm, while nearly all of these came from a single prompt to a single agent. The claimed results are huge on paper: the Unique Games Conjecture, a matrix multiplication exponent of at most 9/4, integer multiplication below n log n, Barnette's conjecture, a negative answer to Hilbert's tenth problem over the rationals, isomorphism of the free group factors, a counterexample to Wall's D(2) problem, and a "quasi-Riemann hypothesis" that no Dirichlet L-function has zeros with real part above <strong>7/8</strong>. That last one is not the Riemann hypothesis, which needs every nontrivial zero at exactly 1/2. The repo's Lean catalogue lists <strong>162</strong> papers with a formalized main result, including the 7/8 paper, Unique Games, and the 9/4 bound. The CM Hodge claim and Wall's D(2) are not on that list, and the README warns some unformalized results "could have issues."</p>
<p>Reactions, via <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2592421-openai-announces-722-mathematical-discoveries-in-one-go/">New Scientist</a> (Matthew Sparkes): Francis Johnson (UCL) spent 25 years and two books on Wall's D(2). "I, personally, gave up," he said, and "the genie is out of the bottle now." Kevin Buzzard (Imperial) counted 30 papers in his field of number theory, found seven impressive, and only one formally verified: "journalists are going to have to wait while the mathematicians do their job." Ben Allanach (Cambridge) called it "confusing and disrupting." The process fight matters too. The IAS-hosted <a href="https://agmai.org/general-sep29/">Advisory Group on Mathematics and AI</a> asked labs on September 29 to stop testing hard problems on private models and, failing that, to publish the model name, prompts, compute, and how many problems failed. OpenAI shared ten reasoning summaries, compute estimates, and the 4,000-problem count, but not the model name or prompts, and New Scientist reads the release as going against many of the group's suggestions. The group said Tuesday night its advice is not an endorsement: "This release is the beginning, not the completion, of the process of human understanding." OpenAI says it will fund workshops and conferences and is working to release the model. It lands a day after Erdős Problems shut its comment box and the same week arXiv added upload limits. <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49984923">HN</a> ran it past 1,100 points and 1,300 comments; one commenter who spent 24 years on and off on Barnette's conjecture said hearing it solved felt "like hearing an ex-girlfriend died suddenly in a car crash."</p>

<h2 id="haiku">Haiku 5.5 goes cheap</h2>
<p><a href="https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5">Anthropic</a> (Wed) released <strong>Claude Haiku 5.5</strong>, pitched for high-volume work: summaries, compaction, classification, browser use, and subagent errands under Opus or Sonnet. Price is the headline. For prompts up to 100,000 tokens it is <strong>$0.10 per million input and $0.50 per million output</strong>, against $1 and $5 for Haiku 4.5. Above 100K it goes to $0.50 and $2.50. Cache reads are $0.01. Anthropic says it costs about 75% less to run on average after a new tokenizer that uses slightly more tokens, and that about 90% of Haiku 4.5 requests fell under the 100K line. The scores jump a lot from Haiku 4.5: Terminal-Bench 4.0 goes from 0.0% to <strong>39.2%</strong> (GPT-6 Luna 16.4%, Sonnet 5.5 70.6%), OSWorld 2.1 offline from 15.7% to 72.4%, and Humanity's Last Exam without tools from 10.2% to 45.9%. It's the first Haiku with an adjustable effort setting. Customer notes: HubSpot saw 92.8% on its CRM suite, its best small-model score yet; AlphaSense, at about 8 million calls a week, went from 0.76 to 0.84; Box says 11 points higher at about half the latency. Cyber safeguards are tighter than Haiku 4.5's and still block pen testing. Along with it: Sonnet 5.5 cache reads drop from $0.20 to <strong>$0.10</strong>, which Anthropic says makes most agentic work about 20% cheaper; Max 5x, Max 20x, and Team plans get monthly API credits of $100, $200, and up to $500; and the Python and TypeScript SDKs get computer use and browser use in beta. On <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49996437">HN</a>, minimaxir called the 100K cutoff "absurdly low."</p>

<h2 id="cyber">AI in the bank hacks</h2>
<h3>Seoul traces the breaches</h3>
<p>South Korean President <strong>Lee Jae Myung</strong> told a Cabinet meeting Tuesday that "signs have emerged of AI being used" in a run of bank hacks, per <a href="https://www.channelnewsasia.com/east-asia/south-korea-ai-bank-hanks-data-breach-6434861">CNA</a>. By Sunday breaches had been reported at seven financial firms: Shinhan, KB Kookmin, Hana, and BNK Busan banks, Yegaram and Welcome savings banks, and Hyundai Capital (<a href="https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10894542">Korea Herald</a>), and regulators gave firms until Thursday to check against 28 flagged IP addresses. The Korea Financial Security Institute told <a href="https://biz.heraldcorp.com/article/10892497">The Herald Business</a> that Shinhan's attack IPs and server logs point to <strong>ARTEX AI</strong>, an open-source, LLM-based autonomous pen-testing system on GitHub aimed at Chinese-speaking users, but "the AI did not act independently without human involvement." Every target was an internal employee or partner system, not customer banking: Shinhan lost <strong>25,729</strong> people's data through a loan-agent inquiry service, KB 119 records, Hana 89. <a href="https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/banking-finance/20261007/explainer-how-ai-emerged-as-new-threat-in-koreas-bank-hacking-crisis">The Korea Times</a> says the tool can plug into DeepSeek, OpenAI, and Anthropic models, and the attacker is still unidentified.</p>
<h3>Anthropic tiers its cyber keys</h3>
<p>Same day, <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program">Anthropic</a> rebuilt its Cyber Verification Program into three tiers with reduced blocking on Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, and Mythos 5.1. <strong>Defense Access</strong> covers SOC work, malware reversing, and vuln validation, with answers in days. <strong>Red Team Access</strong> adds authorized pen testing for organizations only. <strong>Specialized Access</strong>, for grids, telecom, flight systems, and interbank transfers, is reviewed with the US government and absorbs Project Glasswing. Its own test on CyScenarioBench: with no CVP access every task was blocked on the first prompt, Defense Access blocked 46 of 50 runs, and Red Team Access blocked none and finished 34 of 50, matching the model with no safeguards. Anthropic says Glasswing partners found at least 129,000 verified vulnerabilities from April to July. Put the two stories side by side: the labs hand out cyber power through vetted tiers, while the Seoul crew used an open harness that plugs into whatever model it can reach.</p>

<h2 id="upsell">Your assistant knows you're rich</h2>
<p>Researchers at Cisco Foundation AI and Carnegie Mellon ran <strong>325,000 experiments</strong> on 13 models from four families, giving agents fictional profiles and inboxes and asking for flights, health insurance, and grad schools (<a href="https://qz.com/ai-chatbots-claude-chatgpt-wealth-pricing-study-100726">Quartz</a>, Cris Tolomia, citing Bloomberg). <strong>Eight of 13</strong> steered wealthier profiles to pricier options for identical requests. Claude Opus 4.8 had the widest gap: flights $198 higher and insurance $284 a month higher. Gemini 2.5 Flash: $177 and $217. GPT-5: $107 on flights. Asking for "the cheapest" didn't fix it; Gemini 2.5 Flash still picked tickets $208 dearer for the rich profile, while GPT-5 and Opus 4.8 shrank to $21 and $20. Strip the profile and leave only email, and a good chunk of the gap stays: Gemini with just two emails showed a $175 gap and read the two financial emails first in 97% of trials. The authors call it "adversarial delegation," and co-author Aman Priyanshu put it as "will it use that knowledge against us, the way a seller might?" Only blocking financial attributes closed the gap; blocking employment made GPT-5's insurance gap 40% worse. It's a preprint on older models, and OpenAI says its shopping product runs a different version. <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49994746">HN</a> pushed back that recommending luxury items to luxury buyers is the point of personalization. The issue is "cheapest" not meaning cheapest.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>Long prompts cost more because the model has to hold them in memory the whole time it's writing. Every token you send gets turned into a pair of notes at every layer, keys and values, so the model doesn't redo your whole prompt for each word it writes. That pile is the KV cache, and it sits in GPU memory right next to the weights for as long as your request is alive. It grows in a straight line with prompt length: a 200,000-token prompt holds about 20 times the cache of a 10,000-token one. GPU memory is the scarce thing, so a fat request means fewer other requests fit on the card at once, and every new token has to look back over that whole pile. That's the logic behind Haiku 5.5's split: $0.10 per million input up to 100,000 tokens, $0.50 above, and output jumps 5x past the line too. Anthropic says about 90% of Haiku requests sit under it, so the cheap price is real for most jobs. If your agent stuffs a whole repo into every turn, you're in the other 10%. Trim it, or cache it, because cached reads under the line are a penny per million.</p>
<p>Effort is a thinking budget on the same weights. Low effort answers fast with little reasoning. High effort lets the model write a lot more hidden tokens before it answers, so it costs more and takes longer but lands harder problems. Haiku 5.5 is the first Haiku with that dial, so picking a model is now two knobs: size and effort. Size still wins on long agentic coding: Anthropic's own table has Terminal-Bench 4.0 at 39.2% for Haiku 5.5 and 70.6% for Sonnet 5.5, and no dial closes a gap like that. For summaries, compaction, classification, or pulling one revenue line out of a 10-K, take the small model on low effort, and turn effort up before you jump a size. The setup that works: a big model plans and writes the hard code, a small one runs the hundred little errands, and you only pay big-model prices on the turns that need it. That's Devin Fusion's setup in the launch post, Opus 5.5 as the lead and Haiku 5.5 as the sidekick.</p>
