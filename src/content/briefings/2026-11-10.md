<h2 id="carbon">Gemini 4 Carbon is out, and it's cheap per task</h2>
<p>Google <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-carbon-now-available/">made Gemini 4 Carbon available</a> on Tuesday to all paying developers in the Gemini API and AI Studio, to businesses, and to Google AI Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app, AI Mode in Search, and Antigravity. Google announced Carbon on October 28 and gave it first to cyber defenders in its Fairwind program. Its pitch is a model that spends more tokens than Gemini 4 Argon to explore, reason, and check its own work on hard multi-step jobs. Argon stays the default suggestion for most coding and office work. Carbon costs the same as Argon for now, $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output, until November 30. After that it goes to $4 and $20. Claude Fable 5.2 costs $8 and $40.</p>
<p>The independent numbers back up the price story more than the score story. On <a href="https://cursor.com/cursorbench">CursorBench 4.0</a>, Cursor's test built from real Cursor sessions, Carbon at High effort scores 60.4% at $9.22 per task, Carbon at Medium scores 60.1% at $8.49, and Fable 5.2 at Max scores 60.0% at $32.79. That's a tie on score. The difference is tokens. Carbon used about 40,000 tokens and 71 steps per task, and Fable at Max used about 353,000 tokens and 218 steps. Artificial Analysis and Google DeepMind also <a href="https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2120220567038984212">launched MKA</a>, a benchmark where agents read schematics, drawings, and clinical scans and produce slide decks and spreadsheets. There Fable 5.2 leads at 57%, Carbon follows at 54%, and Opus 5.5 is at 53%. Fable averages $31.97 a task, over five times Carbon's $6.29. Google co-built this benchmark, so read it with that in mind.</p>
<p>Google also previewed <span class="mono">/swarm</span> in Antigravity, which turns one prompt into a team of agents, with Carbon in the planning and checking roles and lighter models doing parallel work. On Google's 375-task honesty test, Carbon passed 92% of tasks against 70% for Argon, and it declined to make up missing experimental data 98% of the time. In Google's simulated cyberattack tests, the safeguards blocked 99% of an adaptive red-team agent's attack attempts. Demis Hassabis <a href="https://x.com/demishassabis/status/2120210297948696997">posted</a> that Carbon "reverse engineers my original Theme Park binaries, adds a new DeepMind themed ride, and ports it to the web in one shot." Ethan Mollick, who had early access, <a href="https://x.com/emollick/status/2120198832202125322">wrote</a> that it's "useful to have an AI that thinks differently than Claude/GPT." Developers on X had spotted Carbon answering on Vertex AI about 2am ET, hours before the announcement. The launch drew 233 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50442303">HN</a>.</p>

<h2 id="signal">DeepMind proved the code behind Signal, not just the design</h2>
<p>Google DeepMind <a href="https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/">introduced AlphaProof Code</a>, a system where human experts decide what to prove and AI agents do the proving. The first target was the Signal protocol, the encryption in Signal and Google Messages. Researchers worked with several models to turn Signal's security goals into formal statements, and human experts reviewed every one. Agents running on Carbon then wrote over six million lines of Lean, a language where a computer checks each step of a proof. The agents flagged about 1% of the work for a human to look at, and Lean checked the rest automatically. It took four weeks. DeepMind says this kind of work has historically taken expert teams years.</p>
<p>The key claim is depth. Most protocol proofs check the design on paper. DeepMind says these proofs cover the compiled code that runs on devices, including the property that messages stay protected before and after a device is temporarily compromised. To test that the system can tell good code from bad, the team put two real, already-fixed bugs back into an isolated copy of Signal's code. In both cases it produced a machine-checked proof that the buggy version was insecure. DeepMind says this is the first time the implementation of a widely deployed protocol like this has been formally verified by any method. Signal CTO Ehren Kret said it gives Signal "even greater confidence" in its privacy guarantees. The scope is "key security properties," not a proof that the whole Signal app is bug-free. DeepMind is open-sourcing the proof files. Sundar Pichai <a href="https://x.com/sundarpichai/status/2120207062655549933">posted</a> the result, and it drew 483 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50439193">HN</a>.</p>

<h2 id="decider">Amazon swapped Sonnet for a model that answers in one token</h2>
<p>Two Amazon engineers, Carr Onstott and Mehul Jaiswal, <a href="https://strandsagents.com/blog/amazon-saves-millions-replacing-sonnet-with-strands-decider/">wrote up</a> how they replaced Claude Sonnet 4.6 in one of Amazon's internal ticketing systems. Sonnet's job was to put each ticket into one of 20 categories. The replacement is Strands Decider, a decision model from AWS's Strands team. It can't write text. You give it a ticket and a list of options, and it returns a probability for each one. They used the version built on Google's Gemma 4 E4B, running on one A10G GPU per machine. Per ticket, tokens fell from 6,724 to 2,010, output from 170 tokens to 1, and time per call from 4.2 seconds to 0.9 seconds. Cost per million tickets went from about $22,200 to about $500, a 98% cut that they say saves millions of dollars a year.</p>
<p>Two details are worth copying. They ran both models on live traffic behind a three-way switch, Sonnet only, both, or Decider only, and judged every disagreement before cutting over. Going back was a 45-second config change. And they found that changing the order of keys in the JSON they sent moved the answer probabilities by up to 0.19, so they now sort keys on every request. They're clear about the limits: this works for a fixed list of labels at high volume, and not when the answer needs explaining or the categories change every week. It was the top AI story on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436640">HN</a>, with 634 points.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>What is an AI "training and inference stack," and why would SpaceXAI rewrite one in C? A stack is the software between the model's math and the GPUs. Two pieces matter here. JAX is a Google library that lets researchers write model math in Python and compiles it into programs the chips run. It's used for training, meaning updating the model's weights. SGLang is an open-source serving engine. It takes requests, packs many into a batch, manages the memory that holds each conversation, and generates tokens. That's inference. Reinforcement learning, the stage Grok 4.8 is in now, needs both pieces in a loop. The model generates many attempts at a task, a scorer grades them, the trainer nudges the weights toward the good attempts, and the new weights get copied back to the generators. Then it repeats, often thousands of times. Generating the attempts usually takes most of the time, so the serving engine's speed sets the pace of training. Python and general-purpose libraries add overhead at every step, and they decide for you how memory is laid out and how weights move between machines. Rewriting in C gives you direct control over all of that and nothing running that you didn't write. The cost is that you lose the free fixes and new features thousands of outside contributors add to JAX and SGLang, and every bug is yours. Musk hasn't published speed numbers, so whether it paid off will show up in how fast Grok improves, not in the announcement.</p>
<p>What's the difference between proving a protocol's design and proving its code? A design proof works on an idealized version of the protocol. It assumes the encryption functions behave perfectly and that each step happens exactly as written on paper. Real software adds many things the paper version leaves out: parsing bytes off the network, storing and deleting old keys, handling errors, and updating state when messages arrive out of order. Many real security bugs live there, in a check that got skipped or a key that wasn't erased, while the design stays sound. A code-level proof states the security goal as a theorem about the actual program and proves that the program meets it. Lean checks every step mechanically, so you don't have to trust the AI that wrote the six million lines. You do have to trust two things. The first is the specification. If the theorem says the wrong thing, you've proved the wrong thing, which is why DeepMind had human experts review every specification. The second is the model of the compiled code that the proofs rely on. That's why DeepMind's test with two old bugs matters. A proof system that can also prove buggy code insecure shows that the specifications catch real failures, not just that they pass.</p>
