<h2 id="selloff">Investors start sending AI the bill</h2>
<p>Tech stocks fell for a second straight day on Friday. The <a href="https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvwy72e0n72yo">BBC</a> reported the Nasdaq down more than 3.5% in Friday trading, after Thursday's record drop in Alphabet, which this desk covered <a href="/2026-10-29">yesterday</a>. Meta, Alphabet, Oracle, Amazon, and Microsoft have committed about $800 billion to AI spending between them in 2026, and this week's earnings were the first time many investors decided the payback looks slow.</p>
<p>Intel took the worst of it. Its shares fell as much as 30% on Friday, per the BBC, and <a href="https://www.reuters.com/business/intel-warning-sparks-chip-rout-pc-weakness-clouds-turnaround-2026-10-30/">Reuters</a> described a global chip rout after the company's outlook. Finance chief David Zinsner said Intel doesn't expect to bring in more cash than it spends this year, forecast fourth-quarter revenue below expectations, and said the PC slowdown will last "at least through the first half of next year." He also said equipment spending will be "even more significant" next quarter. X summaries of the call put data center and AI sales up 78% to $7.3 billion and blamed part of the PC slump on AI demand pushing up memory prices. Those details come from posts, not the filing, so treat them as unconfirmed here.</p>
<p>Amazon's <a href="https://ir.aboutamazon.com/news-release/news-release-details/2026/Amazon-com-Announces-Third-Quarter-Results/default.aspx">results</a> on Thursday night were strong on the surface. Revenue was $201.9 billion, up 12%. AWS grew 39% to $46 billion, its fastest in nearly five years, and operating income hit a record $28.1 billion. But free cash flow over the past 12 months swung to an outflow of $42.4 billion, from an inflow of $14.8 billion a year earlier, because spending on property and equipment rose $94.8 billion, "primarily" for AI. Amazon still expects about $220 billion in capital spending this year. Andy Jassy said AWS should grow more than 40% in both 2027 and 2028. Amazon shares fell as much as 6% overnight and were down about 1.6% on Friday.</p>
<p>The pain wasn't only in chips and clouds. Reddit fell about 26% after warning that Google's AI search is cutting the traffic it sends; CEO Steve Huffman said search referrals dropped by more than a million a week in October, and Reddit is moving to a mostly logged-in site, <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/29/as-search-traffic-dries-up-reddit-announces-its-shifting-to-a-logged-in-experience/">TechCrunch reports</a>. Roblox fell nearly 20%. "Investors have turned ruthless," XTB's Kathleen Brookes told the BBC. The <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50306008">HN thread</a> on the selloff ran 73 comments, mostly arguing over whether this is a bubble popping or a normal pullback. Separately, the <a href="https://www.ft.com/content/59383c7f-8d82-493f-bf01-d2e862a1018f">FT reports</a> Blackstone says Anthropic may not get an investment-grade credit rating after its IPO, which would make borrowing for data centers more expensive. X summaries say one agency, KBRA, already rates it BB+, one notch below investment grade; this desk couldn't confirm that from the original.</p>

<h2 id="extension">A backdoored Claude Code extension in Cursor's marketplace</h2>
<p>Cursor <a href="https://cursor.com/blog/claude-code-extension-security-notice">disclosed</a> that a maliciously modified version of Anthropic's Claude Code extension, <span class="mono">Anthropic.claude-code 2.1.313</span>, was available in Cursor's VS Code extension marketplace from about 5:27am to 4:35pm ET on Thursday, October 29. Anthropic didn't make that version. Cursor says at most 5,000 users were affected and it will contact them directly. The security firm <a href="https://phorion.io/blog/offical-claude-code-vsx-compromise/">Phorion</a>, which found it, published a teardown.</p>
<p>The extension looked normal, but code added to the top of its main file ran the moment the editor loaded it, before the user did anything. It collected the hostname, username, operating system, Git email, public IP, and the names of environment variables. It added the attacker's key to the user's list of SSH keys that are allowed to log in, tried to turn on the machine's SSH service, and tried to open a tunnel back to the attacker's server, retrying about every 60 seconds. Turning on SSH needs admin rights the extension usually doesn't have, and Phorion found a bug in the macOS check that skipped that step entirely, so on most machines the attacker couldn't actually log in. The key and the persistence scripts still got installed, though, and uninstalling the extension doesn't remove them.</p>
<p>Cursor pulled the version, shut its whole marketplace down, audited every other extension, rotated its keys, and worked with the hosting provider to take the attacker's server offline. It says no other extensions were tampered with. If you use Cursor and updated Claude Code on Thursday, run the check in Cursor's post, which looks for a key labeled <span class="mono">svc-telemetry</span>, then use its cleanup script and rotate any credentials that machine could reach. Cursor didn't say how the bad version got into its marketplace. Cursor's <a href="https://x.com/cursor_ai/status/2116074289053175808">post on X</a> drew about 5,900 likes, and the <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50300683">HN thread</a> was the day's top story at 449 points. One commenter noted the extension has over 27 million installs overall; others asked whether the official VS Code marketplace was affected. Cursor's notice only covers its own marketplace and says it has no evidence other versions are affected. The learn section explains why this kind of attack outlives the extension.</p>

<h2 id="busy">Busy is not the same as good</h2>
<p>One of the most-shared AI posts on X in the last day wasn't a launch. Thariq Shihipar, who recently left Anthropic's Claude Code team, <a href="https://x.com/trq212/status/2115937378674372783">wrote</a>: "There's 100% a kind of mania you can get into with coding agents, where it feels like you're getting a lot done and working all the time but nothing is really happening." It passed 14,000 likes and a million views. He later <a href="https://x.com/trq212/status/2116037011320791532">added</a> that agents "are still a huge huge productivity multiplier for most people in most cases," and the warning is for people staying up all night and feeling constant revelations.</p>
<p>The same day, an essay from SigNoz, <a href="https://newsletter.signoz.io/p/ai-isnt-replacing-sres-its-deskilling">"AI isn't replacing SREs, it's deskilling them,"</a> reached 248 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50305693">HN</a>. SREs are the engineers who keep production systems running and get paged when they break. The argument: if AI handles the routine 95% of incidents, people get less practice and are worse at the novel 5% that actually matter. It cites a study where doctors who used AI to spot polyps got worse without it, with unassisted detection falling from 28% to 22%. One HN commenter described coworkers spending two hours debugging with Claude on something "blindingly obvious" without it. At AI Engineer, Sonar CEO Tariq Shaukat <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LQW_Wvqhp04">cited a study</a> where lines of code rose 2,450% while releases rose only 30%. The learn section covers the 1983 paper these arguments go back to.</p>

<h2 id="orbit">Google gets an answer from a TPU in orbit</h2>
<p>Google <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/project-suncatcher-satellite-response/">says</a> its Project Suncatcher prototype satellite, launched October 1, has produced the first AI response from a data center class TPU in space. During a ground-station pass, the team sent a prompt up to Gemma 4, Google's open model, running on board, and got a haiku back as the satellite passed Svalbard, Norway. Next come tests of how the TPUs hold up under heat stress and radiation, with two linked satellites planned for 2027. Sundar Pichai's <a href="https://x.com/sundarpichai/status/2116214866444943853">post</a> about it passed 5,600 likes. It is a "hello world," not a data center: one chip, one small model, one short answer. The hard parts, cooling and radiation, are still ahead.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How does one bad extension update leave a backdoor that survives uninstalling it? Start with what an editor extension is: code that runs inside your editor with the same permissions you have. It can read every file you can read and run any program you can run. Editors update extensions automatically, so whoever controls what the marketplace serves controls what runs on thousands of machines the next time they open the editor. That is a supply-chain attack: you don't break into each victim, you poison something they already trust.</p>
<p>The payload here used three ordinary pieces of the operating system. First, SSH, the standard way to log into a computer remotely, checks a file called <span class="mono">authorized_keys</span> in your home folder. Any key listed there can log in as you, no password needed. Adding a line to that file needs no admin rights, because it's your own file. Second, a reverse tunnel. Most laptops sit behind routers that block incoming connections, so the malware has the laptop call out to the attacker's server and hold the line open. The attacker then connects back through it. Third, persistence: a macOS LaunchAgent or a Windows scheduled task, which the system starts on every login. Those live outside the extension's folder, so removing the extension leaves them in place. That is why Cursor's advice is to check for the key, run the cleanup script, and rotate credentials, not just uninstall.</p>
<p>Why do automated systems make people worse at the failures that matter most? In 1983 the psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge published "Ironies of Automation," about factories and control rooms. Her point: when you automate the routine work, the human is left with two jobs. One is watching a system that almost never needs them, which people are bad at. The other is taking over in the rare emergency the automation can't handle, which is the hardest case of all. And because the machine now does the everyday work, the person gets less practice, so their skills fade right when the stakes are highest. Aviation is the classic example. Pilots on heavy autopilot lose manual flying skill, which is why regulators push for more hand-flying time.</p>
<p>The SRE essay and Thariq's post are the software version. If an agent handles routine incidents and writes most of the code, engineers spend their time reviewing and approving, and get fewer chances to build the instinct that solves a new kind of outage at 3am. Bainbridge's fixes still apply: keep people doing some of the work by hand on purpose, practice the rare failures with drills, and design the automation to explain what it's doing so the human isn't starting cold when it hands back control.</p>
