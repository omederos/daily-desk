<h2 id="nvidia">Huang is back</h2>
<p>Nvidia's <a href="https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-president-ceo-and-board-director">press release</a> says the board reinstated Huang as president, CEO, and board member, effective immediately, after "a series of discussions and meetings" left it "satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead." It also says the board "received input from senior levels of the United States government" and is confident his return "poses no national security risk." Colette Kress, who ran the company as interim CEO, goes back to being CFO. Huang said he is "honored to return" and "can't wait to get back to work."</p>
<p>How it happened, per <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo.html">CNBC</a>: senior officials briefed the board late last week that no national security laws were broken. The board and Huang talked Saturday for the first time since he left, and decided Sunday. The falling stock sped things up. Trump told CNBC he spoke with the board "directly and indirectly," that Huang "did nothing wrong," and that he called Huang Sunday and asked him to promise to "work hard." Elon Musk, Brad Gerstner, and Gavin Baker had been pushing publicly for Huang's return. On Sunday the New York Times <a href="https://cn.nytimes.com/technology/20261123/alice-huang-china-spy-agency-nvidia/zh-hant/dual/">reported</a> that Alice Huang was in contact with an officer of China's Ministry of State Security for at least part of the time she was involved with him.</p>
<p>The market reaction was huge. The <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/business/live/2026/nov/23/tech-sell-off-ai-stock-market-support-measures-deployed-south-korea-taiwan-china-ftse-nasdaq-latest-news-updates">Guardian's live blog</a> had Nvidia up 33.5% at $167.56 by late morning, adding just over $1 trillion and taking it to about $4 trillion. It closed Friday at $125.53, an 18-month low, and it was $186.64 before the scandal broke, so it still hasn't made back all of the drop. The S&amp;P 500 rose about 4.5% and the Nasdaq about 6.5%. Dell, Super Micro, Marvell, and CoreWeave all jumped too.</p>
<p>The investigations continue. The board's independent committee and its law firm, Wachtell Lipton, will finish their review, and Nvidia is creating a Chief Compliance Officer role. Elizabeth Warren, who is expected to chair Senate Banking in January, called it "reckless" and said Nvidia still owes Congress answers on export controls. Some Republicans pushed back too. Utah's John Curtis asked "Who in our government weighed in?" and Nebraska's Pete Ricketts said Huang's "behavior does not support reinstatement." The schedule hasn't changed. Huang has a closed-door transcribed interview with three House committees on Dec. 1, general counsel Tim Teter appears on Dec. 2, and Senate Banking chair Tim Scott said Monday that Huang will testify in public on Dec. 3 as the only witness. California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, who <a href="https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-serves-investigative-subpoena-nvidia">subpoenaed Nvidia</a> last week, <a href="https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/california-attorney-general-says-huang-reinstatement-changes-nothing-5046096">said</a> the reinstatement changes nothing about his probe. The <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50611576">Hacker News thread</a> topped 1,000 points and 2,000 comments.</p>

<h2 id="loop">GLM-5.4 runs its middle layers twice</h2>
<p>Z.ai, the Chinese lab behind the GLM models, <a href="https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.4">released GLM-5.4</a> today, its first flagship built as a "Loop Transformer." In a normal model, each layer runs once per token. GLM-5.4 takes 28 layers from the middle of its 750B GLM-5 base and runs them twice, so the second pass refines what the first pass produced. Z.ai says that gets it to the level of 2 to 3 trillion parameter models. It didn't train a looped model from scratch. It converted an already trained model with more training, because, the post says, looped models trained from scratch still trail normal ones for the same compute. It's the second big looped model in four days, after Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini on Friday.</p>
<p>The post explains three engineering changes. Per-token gates control how much each pass writes back, because simply overwriting the hidden state made the numbers blow up. The first pass ends up writing carefully and the second writes in full, and harder tokens get bigger first-pass updates. The second pass reuses the first pass's memory of the context plus a small window of its own, so the extra loop doesn't double the memory bill (see the learn section). It also reuses the first pass's choice of which past tokens to attend to. On a 30B test model with the same parameter count and about 10% more training compute, the looped version did better, mostly on math, science, and long-context tasks. It also wrote 10 to 14% fewer tokens, mostly from shorter chains of thought. Z.ai's read is that some of the reasoning moved inside the loop.</p>
<p>The catches are spelled out. The model "tends to get caught in overly long 'marathon thinking' loops," which Z.ai says still improves accuracy. The post has no benchmark table for the flagship, which the top <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50609685">Hacker News</a> comments noticed right away. It's available now in Z.ai's coding plan, ZCode, and chat. The API is "coming soon," and the open weights will come "after completing necessary safety hardening." Z.ai quotes 200 tokens a second off-peak and 70 to 80 at peak. It also says a "significantly larger" model is coming soon.</p>

<h2 id="quantum">Agents that prove their quantum circuits</h2>
<p>Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI <a href="https://deepmind.google/blog/finding-faster-quantum-algorithms-with-agents-and-formal-verification/">published</a> work today where teams of Gemini 4 agents improved known quantum algorithms. The problem is that you can't test a big quantum circuit. Classical computers can't simulate it and today's quantum chips are too small to run it. So the team built a Lean library for quantum programs. The agents had to prove that each new circuit is correct and what it costs, or the result didn't count. Separate teams worked on each problem, shared results through wikis, and every four hours met at a virtual "conference" to trade ideas, per the <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09822">paper</a>.</p>
<p>The results, compared with the best prior designs: 11.8x cheaper for a circuit used in laser fusion modeling, with memory down from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits. 8x cheaper for simulating a light-sensitive molecule. 2.5x for Cytochrome P450, an enzyme that breaks down most medicines, with memory down from 481 to 333 qubits. 2.57x for one version of Grover's search, and 290x for arcsin, a small math routine many quantum algorithms use to load numbers. DeepMind's own caveat is that these still need more than the first error-corrected quantum computers will likely have. So this is real progress on paper, not something you can run yet. The <a href="https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-quantum-computation">Lean library and verified solutions</a> are open source. Same theme as the Riemann week: let the AI search, and make it prove what it found.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How does GLM-5.4 run 28 layers twice without doubling its memory? Start with the KV cache. When a model writes a token, every attention layer looks back at all the earlier tokens. To avoid redoing that work, each layer saves two small vectors for every token it has seen, called keys and values, and that saved store is the KV cache. It grows with every layer and every token of context. For long chats it's often what fills the GPU, more than the weights. A looped model has a problem here. The second pass through the 28 layers sees different hidden states than the first, so done naively it needs its own full cache, which adds 28 more layers' worth of memory for every token in a context that can reach 1 million. GLM-5.4 splits the job in two. For looking far back, the second pass reuses the cache the first pass already built. For recent tokens, it keeps its own fresh cache, but only inside a sliding window, meaning just the last stretch of tokens. Older entries fall out of that window. So the extra memory is a fixed-size window, not a second copy that grows with the context. Z.ai says the two can overlap, so for recent tokens the model sees both the first-pass and second-pass versions. When a looped or "thinks longer" model claims it barely costs more to serve, the question to ask is what it does with the KV cache.</p>
<p>And what is an "error-corrected qubit," the unit in DeepMind's fusion result? Real qubits, called physical qubits, are noisy and lose their state quickly. Error correction spreads one reliable qubit, called a logical qubit, across many physical ones and keeps checking them for errors. In the usual surface-code designs that takes hundreds to around a thousand physical qubits for each logical one, depending on how good the hardware is. For scale, Google's Willow chip from 2024 has 105 physical qubits. So "4,338 error-corrected qubits" means a machine with millions of physical qubits, which nobody has built. Operations count too. In those designs, certain gates can't be done directly on protected qubits. Each one needs a specially prepared state made in a separate part of the chip, so the number of those gates sets how long the program runs. That's why DeepMind's cost measure combines qubits and operations. Cutting 290x off a routine like arcsin matters even with no hardware to run it yet, because it lowers the bar the hardware has to reach. When someone says a quantum algorithm needs "N qubits," check whether they mean logical or physical. The two differ by about a thousand.</p>
