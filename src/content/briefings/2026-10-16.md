<h2 id="beam">Reflection opens Beam</h2>
<p>Reflection, the US lab led by Misha Laskin, put <a href="https://reflection.ai/blog/beam-is-available">Beam's weights</a> on Hugging Face this morning under Apache 2.0, a looser license than the custom terms on Meta's Spark Open or StepFun's Step 5. It is a text-only mixture-of-experts model: <strong>501B</strong> total parameters, <strong>23B</strong> active per token, a <strong>1M</strong>-token context, built for coding and long agent runs. Reflection first showed it on October 5 and promised weights "later this month." They arrived with BF16, FP8, and NVFP4 checkpoints, a DFlash2 speculative-decoding drafter, a safety technical report, and 40+ launch partners including AWS, Google Cloud, Azure, Nvidia NIM, Together, Fireworks, Baseten, Modal, OpenRouter, Ollama, and LM Studio.</p>
<p><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/reflection-beam">Hugging Face's write-up</a> has the useful plumbing. The BF16 checkpoint is about <strong>1 TB</strong>; NVFP4 cuts it to roughly <strong>280 GB</strong>, so four B200s or two B300s serve it with room for long contexts. Most layers only look at the last 512 tokens and every sixth layer sees everything, which keeps the memory a 1M-token conversation needs to about <strong>36 GiB</strong> instead of 208. Reflection's own numbers include 81.6% on SWE-bench Verified and 80.1% on Terminal Bench 2.1. Independent: <a href="https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/reflection-beam">Artificial Analysis</a> puts it at <strong>30</strong> on its Intelligence Index, #2 among US open weights behind Muse Spark Open (39) and ahead of Nemotron 3.5 Super VL (26). Among all open models it is tenth. Chinese labs still hold the top of that board.</p>
<p>Reflection also open-sourced Mirror CLI, the agent harness Beam was trained against (<a href="https://x.com/MishaLaskin/status/2111080248997491049">@MishaLaskin</a>). That matters more than it sounds: a model RL-trained inside one harness usually does best inside that harness. Per Unite.AI, pretraining used <strong>23.8T</strong> tokens and the RL run produced 100M+ rollouts on 10,500 GB300s over four weeks. Baseten lists API pricing at $1.30 per million input tokens. This one surfaced first on X, where <a href="https://x.com/reflection_ai/status/2111079965529968798">@reflection_ai</a> and a Hugging Face livestream with the team led the open-model chatter all morning.</p>

<h2 id="export">Beijing drafts an AI export list</h2>
<p>China's Commerce and Science ministries <a href="https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/blgg/art/2026/art_1666fcaa24424766afe0683498ff0908.html">published a draft</a> today adding 40 technologies to the catalog of exports China bans or restricts. Twenty-three are AI: large models, agents, computing clusters, and information processing. The rest cover cell and gene therapy and solar manufacturing. Per <a href="https://www.straitstimes.com/business/china-plans-ai-export-curbs-to-exert-control-over-key-tech">Bloomberg via The Straits Times</a>, the restricted list would cover weights and source code for models trained on "important data" with at least <strong>2 trillion</strong> parameters (Bloomberg names Kimi K3 and Qwen3.8-Max as examples), or models ranked in the global top 10 on performance, usage, or safety. Some AI chip design tech is on the list too.</p>
<p>Two details decide how much this bites. First, the ministry says tech already public through open-source releases or papers is out of scope, so StepFun's 600B Step 5 Preview weights, released Thursday, and the open Qwen and GLM lines are not suddenly contraband. Second, a temporary registration rule took effect today: before exporting any listed tech, companies must register, and that covers deals already underway. Public comment runs through November 15 (<a href="https://apnews.com/article/china-export-restrictions-ai-chips-solar-us-5ac381c0c6d6628bbea2e0da7d855a5b">AP</a>, <a href="https://www.wsj.com/economy/trade/china-proposes-export-controls-on-ai-technologies-75ac2030">WSJ</a>). Read it as Beijing claiming the right to decide which frontier weights leave the country, while leaving the open-weight pipeline it already uses for global reach alone. X's news feed flagged this before most Western outlets had it up.</p>

<h2 id="mai">Microsoft opens its training ledger</h2>
<p>Microsoft AI <a href="https://microsoft.ai/news/frontier-pre-training-at-microsoft-ai/">finished pretraining MAI-Base-2</a>: a mixture-of-experts base model with <strong>2.8T</strong> total and <strong>80B</strong> active parameters, trained on about <strong>50T</strong> tokens across <strong>16,000</strong> GB200 GPUs for <strong>38 days</strong> in August and September. The unusual part is how much it disclosed. Average MFU (how much of the chips' peak math went into useful training) was <strong>21%</strong>. Goodput (share of time training actually advanced) was <strong>89%</strong>. Microsoft says MAI-Base-2 beats every open-weight base model it tested on held-out sets and is 4.2x to 12.3x more compute-efficient than DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base. Those are vendor numbers on data nobody outside has seen.</p>
<p>It is not open. The model is in post-training, with no release date, and Microsoft plans to license weights to trusted partners through Foundry. The pitch is provenance: trained on "organic data" without distilling other labs' models. Something to learn below uses this ledger to show how to check a training-compute claim with a calculator.</p>

<h2 id="evaluators">Anthropic gives METR a desk</h2>
<p>Anthropic <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/an-update-on-independent-evaluators">says</a> six staff from METR, the nonprofit model evaluator, now have company hardware, office space, and access to internal tools "roughly on par" with Anthropic's own risk-assessment teams. Employees are told to share with them like they would with a colleague working on alignment risk, except for customer data, privileged legal material, and model architecture. METR will publish its findings. Anthropic cannot veto publication; it can force redactions only in those predefined categories, and METR may say publicly whether it thinks a redaction was justified.</p>
<p>This is the third tier of a ladder Anthropic described: narrow scoped reviews, then publish-without-approval, then employee-like access (<a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/15/anthropic-metr-securebio-ai-safety-evaluators.html">CNBC</a>). SecureBio and Accenture are also evaluators, and talks with Hugging Face are early. Anthropic says it is not funding METR's work. It follows the White House superintelligence accord's pledge on outside auditors and the late-September "Outside auditors" news. On X, <a href="https://x.com/ClementDelangue/status/2110835673003667527">Clément Delangue</a> joked about sending "Microducks," then said it is not approved yet but open-source engineers should be in the room.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How do you check a training-compute claim? Use the rule of thumb <strong>training FLOPs ≈ 6 × active parameters × training tokens</strong>. The 6 comes from about 2 math operations per parameter per token on the forward pass and about 4 on the backward pass. For MAI-Base-2: 6 × 80 billion × 50 trillion ≈ <span class="mono">2.4 × 10^25</span> FLOPs. Now check it from the hardware side: 16,000 GPUs × 2.5 petaFLOPs peak × 38 days (about 3.3 million seconds) ≈ <span class="mono">1.3 × 10^26</span> FLOPs if every chip ran flat out the whole time. Multiply by the 21% MFU and 89% goodput Microsoft disclosed and you get about <span class="mono">2.5 × 10^25</span>. The two numbers agree, so the ledger is internally consistent. Two lessons. Active parameters set the bill, not total: a dense 2.8T model on the same data would cost 35 times more. And the chips spent roughly four fifths of their peak on waiting, communication, and failures, which is normal at this scale. For scale, Beam's pretraining works out to about 6 × 23B × 23.8T ≈ <span class="mono">3.3 × 10^24</span>, roughly a seventh of MAI-Base-2. Both sit under <span class="mono">10^26</span>, the reporting line in the 2023 US executive order (since revoked) and the number you will keep seeing in policy fights.</p>
<p>"5.7x more throughput per megawatt" needs a second number: at what speed per user? A serving rack can either cram many users together, each getting slow tokens, or serve fewer users fast. Artificial Analysis plots that whole curve. At each system's peak, Vera Rubin NVL72 serves 272M tokens per second per megawatt on DeepSeek V4 Pro against GB300's 239M, only <strong>1.14x</strong>. The <strong>5.7x</strong> shows up at about 190 tokens per second per user, where GB300's throughput collapses (23M) and Rubin's holds up (132M), helped by 2.4x the memory bandwidth from HBM4. Fine print worth knowing: about 96% of the tokens counted are cached input that agents resend every turn, the megawatts are GPU power only (no cooling or networking), and Rubin took longer to send the first token (1.65 s vs 1.08 s). When a chip launch quotes one multiplier, ask where on the speed curve it was measured, and whether your product lives there. A fast coding agent does; an overnight batch job does not.</p>
