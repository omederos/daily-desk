<h2 id="fund">Anthropic gives away a slice before it lists</h2>
<p>On Sunday Anthropic <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/public-benefit-fund">said</a> it will put <strong>5%</strong> of its shares into a new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, subject to stockholder approval. The board can add up to 2% more for every 50% rise in the company's value above a baseline it hasn't disclosed. The fund is a wholly owned LLC rather than a 501(c)(3), so it can switch between kinds of giving. Anthropic mentioned "large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations," named workers hurt by AI as one example, and said none of the fund's Anthropic stock will go to lobbying or political giving. Add the co-founders' old pledge to give away 80% of their equity and an employee donation match that about half the staff use, and Anthropic says roughly <strong>20%</strong> of the company is now committed to the public good. <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/19/ai-firm-anthropic-to-commit-shares-to-fund-for-public-good">The Guardian</a> puts the 5% at about $48 billion, using the $965 billion valuation from Anthropic's May funding round. Anthropic's <a href="https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2111881963657412903">post on X</a> at 2:07pm ET Sunday passed 20,000 likes and 6.4 million views.</p>
<p>The timing is the IPO. <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/18/anthropic-ai-public-benefit-fund.html">CNBC</a> reports Anthropic told investors its third-quarter revenue is $16.8 billion, ahead of a listing reportedly set for November. Lise Buyer of the IPO advisory firm Class V Group told CNBC the move is both required prep for going public and "beneficial from an image perspective." Anthropic says the fund's governance and the valuation milestones will come "in the coming months."</p>
<p>The governance question investors actually care about is a different body. In the <a href="https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/anthropic-ipo-private-trust-188f41f7">Wall Street Journal</a> Sunday night, Theo Francis wrote about Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust. Its trustees own no equity, yet they can pick and fire four of the company's seven directors. Per the Journal's reporting, as <a href="https://businessmodelanalyst.com/anthropic-ipo-long-term-benefit-trust-covenants/">summarized by Business Model Analyst</a>, Anthropic must give the trust five days' notice before taking on more than routine debt, selling half or more of the company, or handing control of a model above thresholds the board updates every quarter. Those thresholds aren't public, and neither is the trust agreement. Two of the five seats are empty, and the trustees include former Fed chair Ben Bernanke. Some legal experts told the Journal the notice rule works as a veto, because the trust can replace the board majority that would approve a deal. An Anthropic spokeswoman said it doesn't give trustees veto power.</p>
<p>Musk weighed in fast. Two hours after Anthropic's post he <a href="https://x.com/elonmusk/status/2111914396490604547">replied</a>, "I recommend giving the shares directly to the public." At 3:52am ET Monday he <a href="https://x.com/elonmusk/status/2112089556615123114">posted</a>, "Next year, I will give one of my SpaceX shares to every kid in America. This will be the first of many." That post has about 432,000 likes. Andrew Curran <a href="https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2112207832091333091">says</a> it will go through the government's Trump Accounts, which would cover children under 18 with a Social Security number. Musk hasn't posted the details himself. The Guardian notes SpaceX stock rose 1% before the market opened Monday, and that OpenAI has reportedly held early talks about giving the US government a 5% stake. This story was shaped by X.</p>

<h2 id="breach">One hacker, a hundred break-ins, $50 each</h2>
<p>Reuters <a href="https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/telcos-media-tech/ai-agents-targeted-1200-websites-and-compromised-more-100-report">published</a> a report Monday from Snorre Fagerland, principal threat researcher at the security firm NorForge. Starting September 25, a Chinese-speaking hacker set AI agents loose on e-commerce sites in the US, Canada, Australia, and Japan. The instructions described the hacker's preferred tactics in fewer than 100 Chinese characters. By late last week the agents had tried more than <strong>1,200</strong> sites and broken into sites belonging to more than <strong>100</strong> companies. Reuters identified the victims from the hacker's own logs, including Deere, SharkNinja, and Cummins. A successful break-in typically took about 23 hours of probing. Fagerland estimates the campaign used about <strong>$5,000</strong> of tokens from a mix of DeepSeek and Anthropic's Claude Code, or about <strong>$50</strong> per successful hack. Reuters couldn't tell how much data left. Deere said it had "no indication that sensitive information was accessed." SharkNinja said only a standalone product-support site was hit. Cummins told <a href="https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1013643/ai-agents-hack-john-deere-shark-ninja-cummins">The Verge</a> it is looking into an attack on a site run by one of its vendors.</p>
<p>Fagerland got to watch because the hackers left their dashboards open. Since October 2 he has found about two dozen exposed copies of Cairn, an open-source agent platform, being used for attacks. One other hacker stole credit card data, and another tried to get agents to rob a crypto exchange. In one case the agents failed to find a hole in a big company's main app, so they searched public records for related servers, found an exposed control panel, spotted a subtle flaw in how it handled data, and after hours of trying got the server to run their commands. "It's not script kiddie stuff," Fagerland told Reuters. Cairn's author, who posts as le1xia0, has said it "was not designed for hacking attacks."</p>
<p>Fagerland said he had assumed attack agents would still need a human watching. He no longer thinks so. Andrew Curran <a href="https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2112227100291969513">wrote</a> that after this it's "no longer a safe assumption" that a large attack like the one on OpenCode, covered here yesterday, needs a team. The economics are the news. At $50 per successful break-in, a small online store is worth an attacker's time.</p>

<h2 id="flash">Space Bunny gets a name</h2>
<p>For weeks an anonymous model called Space Bunny sat near the top of the OpenRouter and OpenCode charts. At 7:56am ET Monday MiniMax <a href="https://x.com/MiniMax_AI/status/2112150867868541221">said on X</a> that it was an early version of <a href="https://www.minimax.io/blog/minimax-m31f">M3.1 Flash</a>, and that it handled more than 200 trillion tokens while nobody knew whose it was. The model takes text, images, and video, holds a <strong>1M</strong>-token context, and has an effort setting from low to max that defaults to max (<a href="https://platform.minimax.io/docs/guides/text-generation">API docs</a>). The price is flat: <strong>$0.15</strong> per million input tokens, $0.60 per million output, $0.015 for cached input. Weights are due October 23. MiniMax's own numbers are 73.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, 61.7 on OSWorld V2, and 82% on CyberGym, with agent tasks costing 70% less than on its previous model.</p>
<p>The first independent check landed within hours. <a href="https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2112211865128608002">Artificial Analysis</a> scores it <strong>42</strong> on its Intelligence Index, level with GLM-5.3-Flash, just ahead of Gemini 3.8 Flash at 41, and behind DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash 1017 at 45. It is the cheapest of that group to run, about $0.22 per index task against $0.26 for DeepSeek's Flash and $1.24 for Gemini 3.8 Flash, because it wrote fewer output tokens. The catch is guessing. On Artificial Analysis's factual-recall test it got 56% right but attempted almost every question, which gives it a 93% hallucination rate. On Arena's WebDev board it <a href="https://x.com/arena/status/2112156275282968930">debuted</a> at #12 among open models. Found via X.</p>
<p>The other cheap fast model of the day is Inception's <a href="https://www.inceptionlabs.ai/blog/introducing-mercury-3">Mercury 3</a>, a diffusion model that drafts and refines many tokens at once instead of writing one at a time. Inception says it beats GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 on its benchmarks, is more than 40 times the size of Mercury 1, holds 1M tokens, and runs at 1,068 tokens a second. It costs $0.20 in and $0.70 out per million tokens, half that at launch. Co-founder Stefano Ermon says the limits on scaling diffusion are "compute and data, not fundamental algorithmic barriers." These are all Inception's numbers. The Learn section looks at why OpenRouter currently measures it far slower.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>What does it take for a chip that isn't NVIDIA's to run your PyTorch code? Most AI models are written in PyTorch. When you move a model to <span class="mono">"cuda"</span>, PyTorch hands every operation, each matrix multiply, softmax, and add, to NVIDIA's libraries of GPU programs. A rival chip needs its own version of every one of those operations. It also needs the plumbing for moving data, splitting a model across many chips, and profiling. Until now, using Trainium meant adapting models with Amazon's separate Neuron software, as Hugging Face's Jeff Boudier put it in the TorchNeuron announcement. TorchNeuron plugs Trainium into PrivateUse1, the official slot PyTorch keeps for outside hardware. So the switch becomes <span class="mono">model.to("neuron")</span>, and the usual multi-chip and profiler calls work unchanged.</p>
<p>The one-line switch is the easy part. The hard parts are coverage and speed. PyTorch has two modes. In eager mode each operation runs the moment Python reaches it, which is what you want while debugging. On Trainium, AWS says, eager mode compiles every operation into its own little program and sends the in-between results out to main memory, the slow round trip the October 18 Learn section was about. The other mode, <span class="mono">torch.compile</span>, captures the whole model as a graph that the Neuron compiler can optimize, and that's the production path. Then comes coverage. AWS runs <span class="mono">100,000</span> Hugging Face models through a short test on the chip. It checks whether each one loads, runs forward and backward passes, and compiles, and which operations fall back to the CPU. In PyTorch's own test suite, about <span class="mono">22,000</span> tests are skipped because those operations don't have a Trainium version yet. Every fallback sends work off the chip to the CPU, which slows the step. This is what people mean by NVIDIA's software moat. The rival chip can be cheaper. Deepgram says Trainium processes about 60% more audio seconds per dollar than its GPUs. But that came after a multi-month porting project with AWS. Tenstorrent, which raised $1.5 billion today, makes the same pitch with its own open-source software. When someone says a model "runs on" a non-NVIDIA chip, ask which mode it ran in, how many operations fell back to the CPU, and how many engineer-months the port took.</p>
<p>Why does Mercury 3 claim 1,068 tokens a second while OpenRouter shows 279? Both numbers can be honest. Inception measured its figure itself, "on NVIDIA GPUs," and the post doesn't say how many users shared those GPUs or what settings it used. OpenRouter's <a href="https://openrouter.ai/inception/mercury-3">279 tokens a second</a> is the median it measured on real requests through its service, all served by Inception, in the model's first days. It includes whatever prompt lengths, reasoning settings, and traffic those requests happened to have, and OpenRouter says it doesn't have enough data yet for a full chart. MiniMax's "200+ tokens a second" for M3.1 Flash is the same kind of number, measured by the company that sells the model. A launch-page speed is the best case under the vendor's own setup. For the speed your app will get, look for a third-party median measured over at least a week, or time your own requests.</p>
