<h2 id="open-models">Kimi's weights are out, Hy4's are coming, and a US lab trained one for $32 million</h2>
<p>When Moonshot launched Kimi K3.1 Pro in its apps and API on <a href="/2026-10-23">October 23</a>, it said the weights would follow "by Nov 4." At 11 a.m. ET on Wednesday they <a href="https://huggingface.co/moonshotai/Kimi-K3.1-Pro">appeared on Hugging Face</a>, announced in a <a href="https://x.com/Kimi_Moonshot/status/2118010528123773297">post on X</a> that passed 4,400 likes. The model has 2.8 trillion parameters, of which 104 billion work on each token, and a 1-million-token context. The files come to 1.56 TB, in 96 pieces. Moonshot trained the model in a 4-bit format called MXFP4, so that is what you download, not a squeezed copy. Its own table puts K3.1 Pro at 65.7 on Terminal-Bench 4.0, ahead of GPT-6 Astra at 58.2 and just behind Claude Opus 5.5 at 66.4. Those are Moonshot's numbers, run in Claude Code's harness.</p>
<p>The license is free to use, change, and fine-tune, with two strings. A company that sells access to the model, say as an API, and has more than $20 million in revenue over 12 months needs a separate deal with Moonshot. And a product with more than 100 million monthly users or $20 million in monthly revenue has to display "Kimi K3.1-Pro," <a href="https://artificialwatch.com/wire/kimi-k3-1-pro-open-weights-out">ArtificialWatch</a> found after comparing it to the K3 license, which it matches word for word. Nathan Lambert of AI2 <a href="https://x.com/natolambert/status/2118006783751213133">noted on X</a> that the Chinese open labs "always have delivered on their promises of when weights come out."</p>
<p>Tencent launched <a href="https://hy.tencent.ai/research/hy4">Hy4</a> overnight, the finished version of the preview it open-sourced in August. It has 770 billion parameters, 49 billion active, and for the first time it reads images and can operate a computer, both in the terminal and through on-screen apps such as Blender and CAD tools, per <a href="https://www.tencent.com/tencent-launches-hy4-joining-the-ranks-of-the-worlds-leading-open-source-ai-models/">Tencent</a> and <a href="https://siliconangle.com/2026/11/04/tencent-releases-hy4-powerful-frontier-llm-introducing-visual-understanding-and-computer-use/">SiliconANGLE</a>. It costs $0.83 per million input tokens and $2.50 per million output on Tencent Cloud and OpenRouter, and Tencent promises the weights and a safety report "in the coming days," plus a cheaper Hy4 Flash. Arena <a href="https://x.com/arena/status/2117878267504103555">posted</a> that Hy4 ranks 14th on its Agent Arena, built from 8,500 real agent sessions, which would tie Kimi K3.1 Pro as the top open model once its weights ship. Posts quoting Arena's figures put Hy4 at $0.55 a task against Kimi's $1.46. On X, several people noticed that 4 of Hy4's 6 wins over Kimi in Tencent's own chart come from Tencent's internal tests, and that Kimi wins 6 of the 8 public ones. On HN, one commenter called K3.1 "benchmaxxed." Both readings come from vendor charts, so wait for outside tests.</p>
<p>The US entry came from an unexpected place. Ricursive Intelligence, a startup that builds AI for chip design, released <a href="https://www.ricursive.com/ripple">Ripple-65B Preview</a>, a 65-billion-parameter model it trained from scratch on 12 trillion tokens. It says it spent $32 million in total, including all the failed experiments, and that the model scores 80.5 on TerminalBench 2.1, above much larger US open models such as Reflection's Beam 501B and Nvidia's Nemotron 3 Ultra 550B. Unlike Kimi and Hy4, Ripple is "dense": every parameter works on every token. Ricursive says that kept training simple, and its recipe started from AI2's Olmo 3. Lambert, who worked on Olmo, <a href="https://x.com/natolambert/status/2118049168967290897">called it</a> "so cool to see what our baby looks like scaled up." The weights are on <a href="https://huggingface.co/ricursiveAI/Ripple-65B-Preview">Hugging Face</a>. The learn section does the napkin math on what each of these needs to run.</p>

<h2 id="voters">Voters said no to data centers next door</h2>
<p>Democrats took the House and the Senate on Tuesday, and AI was on the ballot in a small way. In the national exit poll that SSRS ran for NBC, ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox, the AP, and MS NOW, 57% of voters said AI will do more to hurt society than help, and 24% said it will help, <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/exit-poll-trump-midterms-voters-economy-disapproval-rcna607045">NBC News</a> reported. 76% said they would oppose a new data center in their own community. Asked how much "AI data centers" mattered to their vote for Congress, 23% said crucial and 33% a major factor. That put it last of five issues, behind corruption at 90%, cost of living at 89%, the war in Iran, and immigration enforcement, per <a href="https://the-decoder.com/exit-polls-show-ai-ranked-last-among-voter-priorities-in-the-2026-u-s-midterms/">The Decoder</a>. Voters who called it crucial or major backed Democrats 64% to 34%. On which party could better handle AI, 43% said neither. <a href="https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/nbc-exit-poll-56-say-ai-data-centers-were-a-crucial-or-major-factor-in-congressional-vote-decision/">DatacenterDynamics</a> counted at least 43 local data center moratoriums that passed. On X, Elon Musk <a href="https://x.com/elonmusk/status/2117862462976594112">posted</a> "Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now," with 190,000 likes.</p>

<h2 id="gemini">Paid Gemini chats train Gemini unless you give up history</h2>
<p>A Hacker News post with 510 points pointed out something many subscribers missed. On a personal Google account, paying for Google AI doesn't keep your Gemini chats out of training. Google's <a href="https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en">privacy hub</a> says Gemini app activity is used to improve Google services, "including by training generative AI models," if Keep Activity is on, which is the default. "A subset of chats are reviewed by human reviewers," and those reviewed chats are kept for up to three years, even if you delete your activity. The only way out is to turn Keep Activity off, which means chats vanish after 72 hours, or to use temporary chats. One commenter said Gemini on a Pixel won't even set a reminder with history off. Work and school accounts and the paid API follow different rules. Yesterday's HN thread about Codex training on chats was one user's report. This one is Google's own documentation.</p>

<h2 id="rtx-spark">An Nvidia AI laptop runs four times slower on Linux, when plugged in</h2>
<p>Nvidia's RTX Spark laptops, such as the MSI Prestige N16 Flip AI Plus, use the same chip design as its DGX Spark desktop and up to 128 GB of memory shared between CPU and GPU. They ship with Windows on Arm. <a href="https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/linux-nvidia-rtx-spark-laptop">MindStudio</a> installed Linux on one. Running GPT-OSS-120B, Windows wrote 65.4 tokens a second, and so did Linux inside Windows through WSL. Native Linux wrote 13.9. The GPU sat at about 780 MHz on wall power instead of the roughly 2.2 GHz it reaches on Windows. The suspected cause is a Windows-only Nvidia power controller whose settings table is reported as encrypted. MindStudio says turning that controller off in Windows reproduced the slowdown. <a href="https://theterminal.space/hardware/rtx-spark-linux-wall-power-gpu-clock-780-mhz">The Terminal</a> points to an odd detail from YouTuber Alex Ziskind's test: on battery, Linux ran at 61 tokens a second. That points to a software limit, not a slow chip. It also found the "35%" in the HN headline is the clock ratio, not a measured speed. This is one laptop, one tester, and Nvidia hasn't commented or promised Linux support. If you want one of these for local models, plan on Windows plus WSL for now. 489 points on <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50362905">HN</a>.</p>

<h2 id="proofs">Anthropic shows a way to prove which model answered</h2>
<p>Today you can't check that the model you picked is the one that answered. A router could swap in a cheaper model, or an attacker could tamper with the text. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team, working with cryptographers from EPFL, StarkWare, and MIT, released <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/provable-inference">FlashProve</a>, open-source code that attaches a cryptographic proof to each response. They say Claude produced many of the speedups. On Llama 4 Maverick, a 400-billion-parameter model in 4-bit form, one H100 makes proofs at about 110 tokens a second. Each 1,024-token answer comes with a proof of about 19 MB that a 24-core CPU checks in 17 seconds. The catch is cost: proving takes 70 to 95 times the GPU time of just running the model. Anthropic calls it intermediate research, released under Apache 2.0 with Lean proofs of its security claims. It went up Monday and reached HN on Wednesday. The learn section explains how a proof can say which weights ran without showing them.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>How can a proof show which model wrote an answer without revealing the model? FlashProve uses a tool from cryptography called a zkSNARK, short for zero-knowledge succinct argument of knowledge. The name lists what it does. "Zero-knowledge" means the proof reveals nothing except that the claim is true. "Succinct" means checking the proof is far cheaper than redoing the work. Here, the work is running a 400-billion-parameter model, and the check is 17 seconds on a CPU.</p>
<p>It works in two steps. First, the provider publishes a commitment to its weights. Think of it as a fingerprint: a short string that matches exactly one set of weights, without revealing them. It's like sealing your answer in an envelope before the game starts. Then, for each answer, the provider proves this statement: "the weights in that sealed envelope, run on this prompt, produce these exact tokens." To do that, the prover rewrites the model's math, every multiply and add in every layer, as equations over whole numbers, and builds a proof that all of them hold. The math is designed so that faking a proof for different weights would mean solving a problem believed to be impossible with any realistic computer. That's also why FlashProve uses a 4-bit model and integer math: decimal arithmetic on a GPU rounds slightly differently from run to run, and a proof needs every step to come out exactly the same.</p>
<p>The cost is that proving takes 70 to 95 times the GPU time of serving. That's fine for audits, such as a regulator spot-checking that a lab is serving the model it says it is, or an evaluator confirming they tested the model that shipped. It's too expensive for every chat today. It also proves which weights ran, not that the answer is right or safe. And the proof hides the weights but not the model's design.</p>
<p>What does it take to run Kimi K3.1 Pro's weights yourself? Start with the file size, 1.56 TB, because all of it has to sit in fast memory even though each token uses only 104 billion of the 2.8 trillion parameters. Each token goes to a different set of experts, so you can't leave the unused ones on disk. Nvidia's B300 has 288 GB of memory, so an 8-GPU B300 server holds about 2.3 TB. That fits the weights with roughly 700 GB left for the memory each conversation uses. An 8-GPU H100 server has 640 GB, so you'd need three of them working together. Four 512 GB Mac Studios add up to 2 TB, enough to hold the model but slow to write with it. That's why HN's top comment said you "probably need something like an 8x B300 node." Hy4, at 770 billion parameters, will need roughly a quarter of that if its weights ship at the same precision. Ripple-65B shrunk to 8 bits is about 65 GB, so it fits on one 128 GB DGX Spark. Being dense, though, it reads all 65 GB for every token, and the Spark's memory moves only about 273 GB a second. Expect a few tokens a second, not dozens. For most people, "open weights" on a 2.8T model means choosing among hosts, or building on top of it, not running it at home.</p>
