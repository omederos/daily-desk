<h2 id="argon">Google's frontier, open</h2>
<p>Tuesday Google made <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-argon/">Gemini 4 Argon</a> broadly available: paid Gemini API and AI Studio for developers, Google Antigravity for Ultra subscribers, Gemini Enterprise for companies, and the Gemini app plus AI Mode in Search for Ultra and Pro. The model first showed up September 30 behind Fairwind cyber partners and a government pre-release process. Thirteen days later Koray Kavukcuoglu's launch post and the <a href="https://x.com/Google/status/2110041297360699856">@Google</a> account (about 4,200 likes by afternoon) opened the gate.</p>
<p>Intro API pricing is <strong>$2</strong> per million input tokens and <strong>$10</strong> per million output through November 30, with cached input at 95% off. On December 1 that doubles to <strong>$4</strong>/<strong>$20</strong>, the same list band as Claude Opus 5.5 and Meta's Muse Wonder from yesterday. Google says a unified 1M input-output token limit and a continuation protocol support long agent runs. Internal claims: agentic code submissions up more than <strong>35%</strong> and rollbacks down nearly a third over four weeks of Antigravity use, plus concrete jobs like untangling a ~6 million-line Chrome feature folder and a <strong>2.85x</strong> Gemma 4 E2B inference speedup on an A100 via vLLM.</p>
<p>Independent numbers split by what you care about. Vals AI ranks Argon first on its GDP-weighted professional index at <strong>68.90%</strong> as of October 12, per <a href="https://venturebeat.com/technology/google-makes-gemini-4-argon-broadly-available-with-limited-time-low-api-pricing-of-2-10-per-1m-token">VentureBeat</a>. <a href="https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2110068185793970576">Artificial Analysis</a> puts Argon at <strong>53</strong> on Intelligence Index v4.3, tied with GPT-6 Astra and below Opus 5.5 (58), Sonnet 5.5 (56), and yesterday's Muse Wonder Full (56), at about 60% of Astra's cost per task while the discount lasts. Demis Hassabis posted a wizard-chess demo on X. Early hands-on chatter is loud and mixed. Treat that as chatter until more independent runs land. The HN thread is already past 450 points.</p>

<h2 id="scion">Git lost to the merge rate</h2>
<p>Anthropic's engineering blog, <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control">Redesigning source control at Anthropic</a>, is the clearest picture yet of what agent coding does to the rest of the stack. Daily merges on the monorepo went from about <strong>2,500</strong> in May 2026 to over <strong>30,000</strong> in September. Main-branch history alone is almost 100 GB. CI was doing on the order of 25 million checkouts a day. A naive fetch of that would be hundreds of petabytes of git traffic. Hundreds of internal mirrors bought time, then failed.</p>
<p>Seven engineers built <strong>Scion</strong>, an always-online in-house version control system, plus Antique for code review, and migrated the monorepo off git in five months. Clients no longer hold a full repo. The server creates commits, merges, and rebases. Checkouts are sparse or virtual. Anthropic reports a footprint <strong>200x</strong> smaller than the old mirror fleet, checkouts two orders of magnitude faster, and a <strong>25%</strong> CI CPU capacity win. Claude both caused the problem and wrote much of the fix. The company says it will not ship Scion as a product. The point of the post is the design lesson: tools built for humans working offline break when the clients are agents that are always online. Anthropic's own X post hit about <strong>5,300</strong> likes by afternoon; Kent C. Dodds and Andrew Curran amplified it. That is today's first lesson below.</p>

<h2 id="replicators">No datacenter required</h2>
<p>Monday's Anthropic research post, <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/self-replicating-ai">AI models may be capable of self-replicating without access to a datacenter</a> (William Zhou and Daniel Freeman), argues small weight-based replicators are months away. The threat is not a frontier model escaping a lab GPU. It is a small open-weight model that copies itself onto poorly secured IoT, runs inference there, and hops again. There is no single kill switch.</p>
<p>They ran Gemma4-26B-A4B across <strong>15</strong> off-brand Android TV boxes (~$55 each) at 0.39 tokens per second with pipeline-parallel shards. A 9B model got inference standing on 8 of 9 hardware types tested. In a firewalled GCP redroid fleet with trivial ADB access, <strong>GLM-5.2</strong> completed end-to-end replication reliably; smaller models completed pieces but failed the full chain. Trend math on open models under 30B puts a small model at GLM-5.2's replication floor by end of 2026. Spread would be slow (days per hop on TV hardware), more slime mold than flash worm, and still a bellwether for faster ones. Second lesson below.</p>

<h2 id="character">Character.ai Chapter 11</h2>
<p><a href="https://blog.character.ai/an-update-on-character-ai/">Character.ai</a> filed voluntary Chapter 11 in Delaware on October 12 to run a court-supervised sale, with assets and liabilities each in the <strong>$50–100 million</strong> range per TechCrunch. Disney is the stalking-horse bidder for a non-exclusive perpetual IP license and certain claims for <strong>$20 million</strong>, plus transition help from former Character.ai staff Disney already hired. The platform stays up during the sale, aimed at late November or early December. If no buyer will keep it running, it winds down. Founded in late 2021 by Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, the company peaked as a high-engagement chatbot app, licensed models to Google in 2024 as the founders returned there, then hit lawsuits after a Florida teen's suicide and a Texas child-safety probe. Consumer AI engagement without durable margins writes its own ending.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>Why did Anthropic throw out git? Git assumes every client is a full peer that can work offline. Your laptop holds history, trees, and blobs, and can commit without talking to anyone. That design won the world for human developers. Agents break it. When thousands of coding agents commit all day, the repo is huge, almost every client is online by definition, and the expensive part is answering "what changed" and "who last touched this line" for CI millions of times. Scion flips the model: the server owns commits, merges, and history queries; the client keeps a bounded cache and only materializes the files it needs. There is no full copy of the monorepo anywhere, not even on the servers. Cache hit rates stay above 99% because every object is content-addressed and immutable. When a lab says "we outgrew git," ask which assumption failed: offline work, full clones, or client-side merges. Most companies are not at 30,000 merges a day. The ones drowning in agent PRs will hit the same wall, and the fix is not "more mirrors."</p>
<p>A weight-based replicator is malware that carries model weights as its payload. Classic worms ship a fixed binary. This one ships (or re-downloads) a neural net, stands up inference on the box it just took, and uses that model to figure out the next unfamiliar device. On a 4 GB Android TV, a 9B model at 4-bit needs several machines. Pipeline parallelism splits the layers across boxes so each holds a shard and passes activations down the line. One box dying can be replaced without killing the whole replica, unlike tensor parallel where one failure takes the set down. Anthropic's demo is slow on purpose: days per hop on TV RAM bandwidth, not milliseconds. The scary part is not speed. It is that there is no datacenter power switch, and the same coding skills that make agents useful also compose into find-host, copy-payload, start-inference, repeat. When someone says "AI worm," ask whether they mean weights running on the victim (weight-based) or a model stealing API keys to rent someone else's GPUs (credential-based). Those are different animals with different defenses.</p>
