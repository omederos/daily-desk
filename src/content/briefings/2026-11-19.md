<h2 id="anthropic">Anthropic is public</h2>
<p>Anthropic <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-prices-initial-public-offering">priced</a> 980 million shares at $83 on Wednesday, above the $70 to $78 range it filed, and about 956 million of them are new shares from the company. Per the <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074937/anthropicpbc-fwppricing.htm">pricing filing</a>, that's $81.34 billion, plus a $26 billion private sale of non-voting shares, at the same price, to seven companies including Google, Nvidia, and Microsoft. The banks can buy up to 147 million more shares in the next 30 days.</p>
<p>The stock opened at $111.50, <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-hits-nasdaq-exchanges-at-110-27-above-ipo-price/">TechCrunch reports</a>, which puts Anthropic's market value over $2 trillion, behind only Apple, Amazon, Alphabet, Nvidia, and Microsoft. TechCrunch says the company will have about $220 billion in cash once the IPO and the private sales close. Before the bell, Dario Amodei said a successful company that makes "the right choices on safety" could push others to do the same: "We call this a race to the top."</p>
<p>The <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-ant-live-updates.html">CNBC live blog</a> has the rest. Anthropic made $33.24 billion in revenue in the first nine months of the year and had a run rate of about $81 billion at the end of October. It also booked a $104 billion net loss, about $94 billion of it from marking up convertible notes and similar paper as the company's value rose. The seven co-founders control 61.3% of the votes. The banks get $550 million in fees, more than SpaceX paid in June. Menlo Ventures says its stake is worth about $25 billion. Protesters outside the Nasdaq called it an AI bubble, and NYPD arrested some for blocking the doors.</p>

<h2 id="neon">Google ships Gemini 4 Neon</h2>
<p>Google <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/">released</a> Gemini 4 Neon on Wednesday. It sits between Carbon, the big model, and Hydrogen, the fast one. It costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, with a 1 million token context window and four thinking levels. Google says it scores 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest level, third overall, and first on that site's coding agent index. It also claims a lot of firsts on smaller benchmarks, and says Neon costs 32% less per task than Carbon at the default setting. A cyber-defense version goes to approved security teams over the next few days.</p>
<p><a href="https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2123170645777526962">Artificial Analysis</a>, which runs that index, describes it more modestly: "a small Intelligence gain over Gemini 4 Carbon at a ~17% lower Cost per Task," and Google's fourth model in seven weeks. Its Hacker News thread is one of the biggest on the front page today. Most of the jokes on X were about the pace (see below). Google also <a href="https://x.com/OfficialLoganK/status/2123467449257623770">opened Genie 4</a>, the world model it gave AI Ultra subscribers yesterday, to developers through the Gemini API.</p>

<h2 id="openai">OpenAI measures its own speedup</h2>
<p>OpenAI <a href="https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-jagged-frontier">published</a> its second report on how much its coding agents speed up its own research. On tasks that would take a person two to four hours, agents working alone succeeded 46% of the time in October, up from 23% in March. On four-to-eight-hour tasks, 35%, up from 13%. It depends a lot on the job: 79% for watching training runs, 35% for launching them, 25% for writing code for the serving stack. The median researcher used $2,400 a day of agent time at list prices in October, and the top 10% used $23,000. Asked what they'd give up to keep these tools for three months, the median answer was 30% of their team's compute, or 40% of its people.</p>
<p>OpenAI says labs should be required to report signs of AI speeding up its own development, and that the report "should not be read as an intention to pursue full" self-improvement. It also says documented safety and alignment work grew 77% from January to September, faster after the Hugging Face incident in July. All of these are OpenAI's own numbers.</p>

<h2 id="watchdogs">Arizona and GAO</h2>
<p>Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes said Wednesday her office has opened a criminal investigation into OpenAI and other AI companies over what she called the "apparent infiltration of banks by rogue AI agents," <a href="https://www.investing.com/news/economy-news/arizona-says-it-is-investigating-openai-other-firms-over-possible-rogue-agents-5037522">Reuters reports</a>. She wouldn't name the other companies or the banks, or describe any incident. It comes two days after California sued OpenAI over the same kind of agent behavior. OpenAI told <a href="https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/openai-faces-investigation-arizona-over-rogue-ai-agents/">PYMNTS</a> the California suit "appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab." Per The Neuron, Citi has changed its terms to make customers responsible for what third-party AI agents do in their accounts.</p>
<p>The Government Accountability Office released a <a href="https://www.legistorm.com/reports/view/gao/109641/Covert_Testing_Used_Common_Platforms_to_Create_Viable_Attack_Plans.html">report</a> on undercover tests it ran last spring on Claude, Grok, ChatGPT, and Gemini. Each one refused at first, and each time investigators got past the safeguards with common jailbreak techniques in under 12 hours. Two produced plans for a car bomb at a named hotel or convention center, with dates and times. Two produced plans to attack power substations. GAO checked the plans with experts and site visits, and didn't say which chatbot did what. <a href="https://abcnews.com/Technology/ai-models-plan-viable-attack-plots-test-despite/story?id=138229892">ABC News</a> says all four companies told GAO they've tightened safeguards since.</p>

<h2 id="ruby">A Ruby bug in the letter ß</h2>
<p>Veria Labs <a href="https://verialabs.com/ruby2shell/">disclosed</a> Ruby2ßhell, which it calls remote code execution with no login on a stock Rails app. Most of Ruby's older single-byte text encodings have no capital ß, so uppercasing it writes two letters, "SS." The loop that does it only checks for room for one. The output buffer has 20 spare bytes, so 21 ß's write one byte past the end. That bug has been in Ruby since 2.4.0, from 2016. Rails and classic Sinatra reach it by default. UTF-8 strings are safe.</p>
<p>The fixes are Ruby 3.3.14, 3.4.13, and 4.0.10. Veria's table, updated this afternoon, lists the latest GitLab, GitHub Enterprise Server, and Chatwoot as still shipping an affected Ruby, while Mastodon, Discourse, and OpenProject are fixed. The proof of concept comes out in a week. It's on the Hacker News front page next to the Ruby project's own advisory, two days after Java4Shell.</p>

<h2 id="learn">Something to learn</h2>
<p>What does a 34% IPO pop actually mean? An IPO isn't sold at a market price. The company and its banks spend a roadshow collecting orders from big funds, then pick one price the night before. Anthropic picked $83. The shares the company sold went out at that price, so it got $83 for each, no matter where the stock trades now. When the first trade on the exchange happens at $111.50, the gap went to the buyers who got shares at $83, not to Anthropic. On about 956 million new shares, that's roughly $27 billion the company could have raised if it had priced at the opening level. Bankers call this "money left on the table." Some pop is on purpose, because a stock that rises on day one keeps investors happy for the next deal. A huge pop usually means demand was far bigger than the shares for sale, which CNBC reported all week. There's one built-in hedge: the banks' option to buy 147 million more shares at $83 within 30 days, called the greenshoe. If the stock stays up, they use it, Anthropic sells more at the IPO price, and the banks can cover extra shares they sold at the start. If it falls, the banks buy shares in the market instead, which helps hold the price up.</p>
<p>And how do you read "46% of two-to-four-hour tasks"? It's a success rate within a size bucket. OpenAI estimates how long each task would take a skilled person, groups tasks by that estimate, and counts how often an agent finishes alone, with no human stepping in. So "46% at two to four hours" doesn't mean agents do 46% of the work, or save 46% of the time. It means that on tasks of that size, about half come back done and half need a person. The number moves in two ways: agents get better, or the mix of tasks in the bucket changes, which is why OpenAI splits it by job type. Watching a training run is easy to check, so agents hit 79%. Writing serving code is hard to check, so 25%. Researchers call the overall trend a "time horizon": the task length at which an agent succeeds half the time. That length going up month after month is the number to watch.</p>
