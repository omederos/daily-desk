Jensen Huang weighs a comeback

Last Thursday Jensen Huang resigned from Nvidia after reports of affairs, one with Alice Huang, an executive at the Singapore firm Megaspeed whom U.S. officials suspect of spying for China. On Sunday the Financial Times reported, via Reuters, that he is considering a return and has discussed it with confidants. One ally told the paper they are building a "war room" of Silicon Valley executives to lobby the board and win support from the White House, and that Huang backs the effort. Spokespeople for Nvidia and Huang declined to comment.

The public side of that campaign is a "Bring Jensen Back" letter. Fortune counted about 175,000 signatures, including Elon Musk, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney, Travis Kalanick, and thousands of Nvidia staff. Shareholders Brad Gerstner of Altimeter and Gavin Baker of Atreides wrote separately on Friday asking the board to keep "all options on the table," per Yahoo Finance. Even one person involved in the push conceded to the FT that "having an affair with a subordinate is a fireable offence." Huang doesn't hold a controlling stake and can't overrule the board.

Washington is pulling the other way. On Fox News, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the board should "go outside the company" for a "dramatic shake-up," and asked whether Nvidia was "pressing these seemingly illicit sales to goose their revenues and their earnings." He wants answers from the board and its outside counsel within 10 days, and said that if Nvidia were a bank, regulators "would be in there right now." He also blamed the Commerce Department for not putting Megaspeed on the Entity List sooner. Nvidia says it follows U.S. export law. Senator Maria Cantwell made the same complaint about Commerce in a letter to Secretary Howard Lutnick, as reported from The Verge, and Lutnick has agreed to testify to the Senate Banking Committee on Dec. 10.

The timing is awkward. Interim CEO Colette Kress presents quarterly results on Tuesday, Nvidia's first since Huang left, and has a company-wide meeting Wednesday. The board said Friday it hasn't hired a search firm or formally engaged any candidates, days after the FT reported it had approached Arm's Rene Haas. Fortune cites a UBS estimate that if a post-Huang Nvidia pulled back on investing in startups and suppliers, more than $50 billion could leave the market. That's the worry for neoclouds like CoreWeave and Nebius, which sold off again. Prediction markets, per Fortune, give him about a 40% chance of being back by next year.

Qwen4-Max ships, and you can't download it

Alibaba made Qwen4-Max generally available on Monday after a preview that opened Nov. 11. It's a mixture-of-experts model with 2.4 trillion parameters, about 71 billion of which run for each token, plus a one million token context window and image and video input. On QwenCloud it costs $1.50 per million input tokens and $4.50 per million output. Alibaba says it uses 35% fewer tokens than Qwen3.8-Max at high reasoning effort and cuts the average cost of a task on its own Work360 benchmark by around 60%. It placed sixth on Arena's WebDev coding board, according to Qwen's own post.

The bigger story is the license, or the lack of one. The Next Web notes the weights can't be downloaded, a reversal from August, when Alibaba released a version of Qwen3.8-Max. The New Stack points out that even that release was text-only and came with a custom license that charged big commercial users. Alibaba says smaller Qwen4 models will get open weights, with no date. Its own charts are mixed, per AI Pro Playbook: ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 on the DeepSWE coding test, behind Opus 5.5 and Kimi K3.1 on Terminal-Bench 4.0. Alibaba also says it removed training data aimed at attack skills while keeping defensive security data.

The same day, Tencent launched Hy4 flash, a 224 billion parameter model with 12 billion active, at 7 cents per million input tokens and about 28 cents output. Tencent says it plans to release those weights. And DeepSeek users are hitting new chat limits; the service cites a shortage of computing capacity, per TechNode.

Perplexity bets 10,000 GPUs on an automated researcher

Perplexity launched Perplexity Frontier Research, a lab whose stated goal is "an automatic research scientist": a system that picks questions, forms hypotheses, designs experiments, and updates its conclusions on its own. The work covers multi-agent orchestration, reinforcement learning, continual learning, systems, and pre-training, backed by "in excess of 10,000 GPUs." Perplexity says it will publish papers, data, models, and code. CEO Aravind Srinivas wrote on X that they will "open source our models and tooling."

What's missing, as Digg's roundup notes, is any result, schedule, license, or GPU model. A GPU count is a budget, not a finding. It is a real shift for a company best known for building on other labs' models.

The Fed's survey puts AI at the top of its worry list

In the Federal Reserve's November Financial Stability Report, 87% of the market contacts it surveyed named AI as a major risk to the financial system, up from 50% in the spring, making it the most-cited concern. Reuters and Axios summarize the reasons: complicated ways the AI build-out is being financed, cyberattacks, and the chance of a sharp correction if AI earnings disappoint. With Nvidia reporting Tuesday and Anthropic's IPO pricing Wednesday, that last one gets tested this week.

A physicist says GPT-6 Astra led his proof

OpenAI started a science blog, and its first guest post is from Nathaniel Craig, who leads particle theory at UC Santa Barbara. He proposes a candidate way to define the full Standard Model on a lattice, a grid standing in for spacetime, without treating its forces as small corrections. Craig says he supplied the strategy and GPT-6 Astra turned it into a working construction and led the detailed proof, which outside lattice theorists then reviewed. He's careful about the limits: it's an answer more in principle than in practice, and experts still have to check whether it has every property physicists need.

Something to learn

What does "the proof is checked in Lean" actually tell you? Lean is a program that checks mathematical proofs step by step, and a proof that compiles in it can't contain a logic mistake. But a post on the Xena Project blog, a Lean blog for mathematicians, written after OpenAI's dump of 722 AI-written math papers, says you need four answers before trusting an AI-written one. First, does the Lean statement match the English one? The post's example: a two-line Lean version of Fermat's Last Theorem that compiles fine but is false, because Lean's natural numbers include 0, and 0³ + 0³ = 0³. No proof of it will compile, but a subtly wrong statement can have a real proof of the wrong thing. Second, does the code compile? Anthropic's Fermat proof was 13 million lines, and the author needed a 96-core machine with 500 GB of memory to check it, in a sandbox. Third, does the proof prove the statement as written, or did the millions of lines quietly redefine something, or add a new axiom? A tool called lake comparator checks that. Fourth, is it exploiting a bug in Lean itself? Lean has had soundness bugs, so the paranoid check runs the proof through several independent kernels, one of them formally verified. And even then, the Lean proof being right says nothing about whether the PDF next to it is.

And what does W4A4 mean, as in LongCat-2.5's Huawei build? You already know weights can be stored in fewer bits. Activations are the other half: the numbers flowing between layers as the model works on your prompt. W4A4 means both the weights and the activations are 4-bit. That matters because a chip can only use its fast 4-bit math units when both inputs to a multiply are 4-bit. Shrink just the weights, and the chip has to expand them back to 8 or 16 bits before multiplying, so you save memory but not much compute. Meituan says going from 8-bit to W4A4 raised throughput about 43% in its tests, with accuracy close to the original. The hard part is that activations have rare, very large values that 4 bits can't hold, so methods like the FlatQuant one Meituan uses first rotate and rescale the numbers to spread those spikes out.
