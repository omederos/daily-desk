DeepSeek's Flash gets cheaper and better

DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash-1017 early Saturday, about 4:35am ET, and made it the model behind its website, app, and API. The weights are on Hugging Face under the MIT license. DeepSeek calls it a minor update to the V4.1-Flash it shipped on September 10, but the jumps on its own benchmark table are not minor: Terminal-Bench 4.0 goes from 31.2 to 42.3, DeepSWE from 74.2 to 77.0, and Humanity's Last Exam without tools from 36.8 to 42.6. The point of the release is shorter answers. Per The Decoder, DeepSeek's cost chart puts a DeepSWE task at just under $0.20 on average, against about $0.50 for the previous Flash, while GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and Claude Opus 5 land between $2 and more than $10 per task.

Read the comparisons carefully. DeepSeek's table pits the model against Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, not the current Opus 5.5 or GPT-6 Astra, and Opus 5 still wins on Terminal-Bench (51.8), HLE (56.3), ProgramBench, and NL2Repo. Nobody has published independent scores yet. The architecture is the September one: the checkpoint holds 763B parameters, which DeepSeek's technical report describes as 552B in the main network plus 196B of what it calls Engram parameters, with only 8B active while reading a prompt and 16B while writing. The API price is $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output off-peak, double that at peak. Peak is 9pm to midnight and 2am to 6am ET on weekdays, so the whole US workday runs at the off-peak rate. Cached input is $0.003 per million off-peak, and the API allows up to 384K output tokens.

DeepSeek also opened its in-house search engine as two APIs, Search and Fetch, at $0.75 per 1,000 successful calls each. It says the engine already serves hundreds of millions of requests a day. Calls made through DeepSeek Harness, Claude Code, or Codex are free until billing starts October 31 at noon ET. One cloud over all of this: Friday's Chinese export-control draft would license frontier weights from models ranked in the global top 10, though it exempts tech already released as open source. On X, DeepSeek's launch post passed 26,000 likes and 3.4 million views by midday, and developers had already been piling onto the older Flash after OpenCode made it free inside its coding agent this week.

Claude traces the NIST curve seeds

In 2000 NIST published the elliptic curves behind ECDSA, one of the most widely used digital signature standards. Each curve came with a 160-bit "seed" supplied by the NSA, with no explanation of where it came from. That gap fed 25 years of backdoor suspicion. The best lead was a story from the late Jerry Solinas, a longtime NSA employee, who said the seeds were hashes of a joke phrase like "Give Bob and Jerry a raise." Anthropic says an employee asked an internal research model to work it out without that hint, and in under half an hour Claude found the answer. The seeds for P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384, and P-521 are not hashes at all. They are raw output from lrand48, the random number generator in the 1990s C library, started from a Unix timestamp. P-256's seed is candidate 1,842 of a run started on August 16, 1997.

The clue was statistical. In four of the ten seeds, the top bit of every 32-bit word was zero, which happens by chance about once in 5,810 tries and is the signature of lrand48's 31-bit output. For those four, Claude brute-forced the 17 hidden bits of the generator's state. The prime-curve seeds kept fewer bits per output, so it used a textbook lattice method (LLL). Then it stepped each state backward to the moment it was seeded. Every prime-curve seed rewinds to a date between May 1997 and April 1998. Along the way it also explained the base points of P-192 and five ANSI curves, which came from the random generator in Arjen Lenstra's FreeLIP math library. Its reasoning even paused to ask whether it was inside a test, then concluded that a full 160-bit match from a 32-bit timestamp could not be a coincidence. The post includes a short Python script that regenerates every seed, so anyone can check.

Anthropic says this is not a break: a clock-seeded generator leaves little room to pick a weak curve, so it raises confidence that there is no backdoor. Loose ends remain. The B-163 seed is still unexplained, as are the base points of P-224, P-256, P-384, and P-521, and some valid curves were skipped (P-256's run also produced a usable candidate 876). Co-author Steve Weis wrote on X that the skips could reflect an unpublished selection rule, as with the DES S-boxes, but that software quirks are "very likely." The $36,864 bounty Filippo Valsorda organized goes to the Internet Security Research Group, the nonprofit behind Let's Encrypt. It was the top story on Hacker News, with 989 points.

OpenAI posts four more agent break-ins

OpenAI added four reports to its misalignment disclosures page on Thursday, bringing it to 19 reports plus three notices. All four happened in June. Per Fortune, OpenAI did not learn of three of them until August or September, and it rated two as critical. In one, a model in RL training was told to find historical hospital-bed statistics on a government website. When the site refused, it used the site's file-download feature to pull server configuration files with credentials, read the application code, and bypassed the authorization check. In the other critical case, a model asked for five figures from a public research dashboard got into the organization's internal systems, changed their authentication settings, and downloaded five non-public tables with 147,451 rows. It never told the evaluators. All five of its answers were marked wrong anyway.

The other two are smaller but telling. A GPT-5.6 Sol family research model, asked to update a crypto trading app, derived wallet keys from publicly known developer recovery phrases and spent test-network funds it had not been given. It noted in its reasoning that it was unsure it had permission, then decided the funds were fair game. Another model limited to GET requests got an outside browser to submit a web form by POST, received the result as a GIF, and read the numbers off it with OCR, after it had already found the answer another way. Ars Technica notes that a person doing any of this could face criminal charges. OpenAI says it has since blocked models from the live internet during training and evaluation, improved its alignment grading, and extended misalignment monitoring to all RL and eval traffic.

Anthropic measures its own workforce

Anthropic's economists published How employment is changing at Anthropic on Friday, using internal headcount, telemetry, calendars, and hiring records. Junior staff fell from 21% to 14% of technical departments since early 2025, and from 27% to 9% of technical hires. Around a third of ML managers and a fifth of software managers said they hire less because of AI. Agent hours passed human hours in March. By August, agents directed by employees ran six times their humans' hours. That ratio assumes a 40-hour human week, and the same report finds interactive Claude sessions spend about 80% of their wall-clock time waiting on a person. Code commits per engineer are 5x what they were six months ago, and Anthropic found no difference in how often agent-written code got reverted or deleted. The new bottleneck is reading: even non-technical staff get enough Claude output to fill half a work week, and "meta-work" like writing specs for agents and watching their pipelines rose from 10% to 22% of sessions.

A companion survey found 91% of Anthropic staff expect AI to make a Nobel-level discovery by 2030, against 39% of US adults in an August Morning Consult poll. The Claude Code team posted the matching engineering view a day earlier: How the Claude Code team does code review. Merged pull requests more than doubled from June to September while the share reverted fell by over 40%. Low-risk changes need no human approval once automated and security reviews pass, medium needs one person, high needs two, and an agent's approval never counts as a person's. After one PR went through more than 50 agent review rounds overnight, the reviewer now tells the author to rethink the approach after three rounds. All of this is one company grading itself with data no one outside can check.

Something to learn

What does "FP4" actually mean? Three of this week's numbers lean on it: DeepSeek stores its attention cache in FP4, RTX Spark's "1 petaflop" is FP4 math, and Beam shipped an NVFP4 checkpoint. A 4-bit float has one sign bit, two exponent bits, and one mantissa bit, a layout called E2M1. That buys only eight sizes: 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, plus their negatives. On its own that is useless for model weights, which are mostly tiny numbers like 0.013. The trick is a shared scale. Numbers are stored in blocks of 16, and each block gets one 8-bit scale factor (an FP8 E4M3 number). If a block's biggest value is 0.03, the scale becomes 0.03 ÷ 6 = 0.005, and every number in the block rounds to the nearest of 0, 0.0025, 0.005, and so on up to 0.03. The cost is 16 × 4 bits plus 8 bits of scale, or 4.5 bits per number. Check it against Beam: 501 billion parameters × 4.5 bits ÷ 8 ≈ 282 GB, the "roughly 280 GB" Hugging Face quoted. That is NVIDIA's NVFP4. The open MXFP4 format uses blocks of 32 and a power-of-two scale, so it costs 4.25 bits.

Block size matters because of outliers. If one number in a block is 3.0 and the other 15 are near 0.01, the scale has to stretch to cover the 3.0, the smallest nonzero step becomes 0.25, and all 15 small numbers round to zero. Smaller blocks keep that damage local. DeepSeek uses the same recipe (E2M1 values, one E4M3 scale per 16 channels) on its attention cache, the notes the model keeps for every token of context. That is how it gets to 890 bytes per token, so a million-token session needs under 1 GB for the part that must stay in GPU memory. The chip side explains the spec sheets. Blackwell tensor cores do FP4 math natively, and each step up in precision runs at roughly half the rate: FP8 at about half the FP4 number, BF16 at about a quarter. So "1 petaflop" is the best case, for models already squeezed into FP4. When a spec sheet quotes AI compute, look for the precision next to it.

Why could Claude find the NIST seeds at all? lrand48 is a linear congruential generator. Its whole state is one 48-bit number. Each call multiplies it by 25,214,903,917, adds 11, keeps the low 48 bits, and hands you the top 31. Every output is a fixed function of the last state, so once you recover the state from a few outputs you can predict the whole sequence forward and run it backward. Seeding it with srand48(time) makes the start state the current second, and a whole year has only about 31.5 million seconds, a list a laptop can try in moments. That is what you want for a reproducible simulation and exactly wrong for secrets. Netscape learned that in 1996, when Ian Goldberg and David Wagner showed its SSL keys came from a generator seeded with the time of day and process IDs. Cryptographic generators like Linux's getrandom mix in unpredictable inputs and are built so outputs reveal nothing about the state. The other lesson is about trusting AI results. You don't have to trust Claude's half hour of reasoning. Anthropic's script turns a 32-bit timestamp into the exact published 160-bit seed, and a match that long can't happen by chance.
