Anthropic says Claude ported itself to AMD in three days

Anthropic's IPO roadshow video has a section on compute, and it's the most-quoted part of the 42-minute video. Simon Willison transcribed it. Co-founder and chief compute officer Tom Brown says Anthropic now trains and serves Claude "across all four major chip families": Amazon's Trainium, Google's TPUs, and GPUs from Nvidia and AMD. Then the line that spread: "In the past, bringing on a new chip platform took dozens of engineers months of work. This time, we had Mythos try it out. It got Claude running on AMD in just three days, with basically no human intervention. Claude ported or wrote almost 100% of the kernels." Kernels are the small, heavily tuned programs that do a model's math on one particular chip. Every chip family needs its own set, and writing them is a big part of why Nvidia's CUDA software has been so hard to leave.

The fine print is narrower than the clip. A footnote under the on-screen chart, reported by CellCog and The Decoder, says the three days is the approximate time for Claude Mythos Preview "to bring up prompt processing for one Anthropic model on AMD MI355X GPUs at a useful level of performance." Prompt processing is the stage where a model reads its input, before it writes anything. So this is one stage of one model, with no speed or cost given, and not training. CellCog also notes the prospectus itself gives no number of days. Brown's other claim is about "compute multipliers," tricks that let the same chips train a smarter model, and he says Claude will eventually find them on its own. Dario Amodei says in the video that cancer, Alzheimer's, heart disease, and mental illness will be "relics of the past" in, he thinks, less than a decade. That line is not in the prospectus either.

None of this has hurt demand. Anthropic's IPO has orders for several times the shares on offer, Bloomberg reported, driven by very large orders from big investors. Anthropic plans a livestream on Friday with Amodei and JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon. On X, the AMD clip turned into a wave of "the CUDA moat is dead" posts. Some pushed back, like @ssraavi: "the moat was never only kernels."

OpenAI says its newest models are getting harder to read

OpenAI's alignment team published research on a problem it saw with GPT-6 Astra. OpenAI relies partly on reading a model's chain of thought, its written-out reasoning, to catch plans to misbehave. With Astra, it saw that monitoring get worse. The team measured two warning signs. One is "no-CoT capability," how much reasoning a model can do in its head without writing anything. The other is "CoT controllability," how well a model can change its written reasoning when told to, for example inserting "meow" between every word. A model that's good at both could, in principle, hide what it's really thinking.

The findings are what the authors call "sobering." No-CoT capability rises smoothly as the base model gets better at predicting text, so Astra's jump was on trend, not a fluke. Some model designs push it up faster on planning and multi-step math. Controllability rose during reinforcement learning even when graders only saw the final answer, and it passed from teacher models to students through training data. OpenAI is careful about the limits. It calls controllability a noisy stand-in, and in one test the more controllable model was actually worse at evading the monitor. It also says Astra couldn't evade full monitoring of complex sabotage tasks without guidance. The conclusion is that "further progress in pretraining" will need new ways to monitor models.

Google ships its own one-token decision model

Yesterday's issue covered Amazon replacing Claude Sonnet with a model that only picks from a list. Today Google launched its own: Gemini Decision 1, behind a new Gemini Decisions API. You send one input, which can be text, images, audio, video, or PDFs up to 256,000 tokens, plus up to 1,000 questions. Each question is a yes/no, a pick from 2 to 255 options, or a score on a rubric. Instead of writing an answer word by word, the model scores every option in a single pass and returns probabilities with a confidence number. Google says that takes 150 to 250 milliseconds end to end and costs $0.10 per million input tokens, with nothing charged for output.

Google pitches it for tool selection, policy checks, picking which model handles a request, and grading context. The confidence score lets your code send unclear cases to a bigger model or a person. There are limits: no conversation history, no tool calls, and no caching or batch discounts, Unite.AI notes. Logan Kilpatrick announced it on X, and it's already on OpenRouter and Vercel's AI Gateway. Two decision models in two days suggests this is becoming its own product category: small, cheap models that never write a sentence.

Fractile raises $720 million to put memory on top of the chip

Fractile, a London and Bristol chip startup that Walter Goodwin founded in 2022 while a PhD student at Oxford, raised $720 million at a $7 billion valuation, Reuters reported. Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed, and Redpoint led. Google's CapitalG, BlackRock, and DST also put money in, and so did two UK government funds, the Sovereign AI fund and the British Business Bank, the government said.

Fractile builds chips for inference, the job of running a model, not training it. Most AI chips put HBM memory in stacks next to the processor. Fractile stacks DRAM, the same kind of memory HBM is made of, directly on top of the logic. Investor Lightspeed says that gives "far higher memory bandwidth than HBM" and much more capacity than chips that rely on on-chip SRAM. The company's goal is to serve frontier models at thousands of tokens per second, even with very long contexts. It hasn't shipped a production chip. Georgian, another investor, says the ramp is aimed at the second half of 2027. The learn section explains why where the memory sits matters so much.

Qwen4-Max arrives as a two-week preview

Alibaba's Qwen team opened a preview of Qwen4-Max, the top model of its next generation. Per Alibaba's docs, it takes text, images, and video, reads up to 1 million tokens, thinks by default, calls tools, and works with both OpenAI-style and Anthropic-style APIs. The terms are unusual. You can only use it through Token Plan, Alibaba's monthly subscription, not pay per token. It runs from November 11 to November 25 Beijing time, won't change during that window, and then gets retired. It's also in the Qwen app, Qoder, QwenWork, and QwenStudio, TechNode reports.

What's missing matters as much. DataNorth points out that Alibaba has published no benchmark scores, no parameter count, and no regular API price, and that the plan's terms limit it to interactive use in coding tools, not scripts or app backends. The official materials don't mention open weights. On X, the preview fed a running count of the week's launches: Fable 5.2, Gemini 4 Carbon, now Qwen4-Max. People are now watching for Google's faster "Hydrogen" model, which Google staff are testing internally, Business Insider reported. Google hasn't announced it.

Glasswing, seven months in, by the receipts

Patrick Garrity of VulnCheck has been tracking Project Glasswing, Anthropic's effort to find and report bugs in open source with Mythos, since April. His November update counts 442 CVEs credited to Anthropic so far, about 0.5% of this year's CVEs. He found 2 that have been exploited in the wild. Anthropic's own ledger lists 37,966 findings, of which 7,349 are public and 217 are marked fixed. Since September, findings grew by almost 12,000 while fixes grew by 15, and more than 1,600 findings are now marked as duplicates. The ledger claims a 94.2% true-positive rate.

The sharpest data point is curl. Maintainer Daniel Stenberg got 45 reports from Anthropic's self-service OSS Scanner, which Anthropic says are "fully model-generated, without human review." Of the 34 published so far, 19 were judged not to be vulnerabilities, 6 were confirmed as low severity, and 9 are pending. Garrity has been a consistent skeptic of Glasswing, so read his framing with that in mind. The numbers still point to the same problem: finding bugs got cheap, and checking them still falls on human maintainers. The post drew 440 points on HN.

Something to learn

Why is a cheap crypto-mining card secretly an A100? Chips are printed dozens at a time on a silicon wafer, and some come out with tiny defects. NVIDIA's GA100 chip, the one inside the A100, has 128 streaming multiprocessors (SMs), the blocks that do the math, plus memory controllers and links to other GPUs. If NVIDIA threw away every chip with one bad block, it would throw away a lot of expensive silicon. So it tests each chip, switches off the bad blocks and often some good ones too, and sells the result as whatever product it now qualifies for. That's called binning. NVIDIA's internal word for switching blocks off is "floorsweeping." The switches are eFuses, tiny links on the chip that get burned at the factory and can't be un-burned, so the setting is meant to be permanent. Every retail A100 has 108 SMs turned on, a number NVIDIA picked so that most chips qualify. The CMP 170HX, a mining card made from the same chip, was cut down much further: 70 SMs, a slow PCIe connection, and 8 GB of usable memory, even though 9 of the 10 such cards one researcher examined carry 80 GB of HBM.

That researcher, Kate, explains in a blog post how she built a small board that briefly drops the chip's supply voltage at exactly the right moment during startup. That's called voltage glitching. With the right timing, the chip's fuse controller came up in a state that let her burn new fuse records, and on this chip a later record can override an earlier one. She used that to switch the blocks back on. Her cards came up with 120 to 126 working SMs, and in one benchmark they did about 18% more FP16 math than a retail A100. It takes soldering, a custom board, and up to 48 hours of glitching per card, and she reported it to NVIDIA before publishing. The general lesson: one piece of silicon gets sold at many price points, and the label on a GPU tells you what the seller turned on, not what was printed.

Why put memory on top of a chip instead of next to it? Each time a model writes a token, it has to read its active weights from memory (the October 23 learn section). So memory bandwidth, the bytes per second that reach the math units, mostly sets how fast a model writes. There are three common setups. Most GPUs put HBM, stacks of DRAM, beside the processor, joined by thousands of wires that can only run along the chip's edge. Cerebras uses SRAM built into the chip itself, which is very fast but holds little: 44 GB on a whole wafer (the October 25 section). Fractile is betting on a third setup: DRAM stacked directly on top of the logic. Then connections can run straight down across the whole face of the chip, not just its edge, and each bit travels a fraction of a millimeter. More wires and shorter wires mean more bandwidth, and DRAM holds far more per square millimeter than SRAM, so capacity stays large. The catches are heat and manufacturing. Logic runs hot, DRAM has to refresh more often when it's hot, and bonding two kinds of chips face to face only works if every connection works. Fractile says it designed its DRAM together with memory suppliers to handle this, but it hasn't shipped a chip yet. When a new chip claims thousands of tokens per second, ask where its memory sits and how much of it there is.
